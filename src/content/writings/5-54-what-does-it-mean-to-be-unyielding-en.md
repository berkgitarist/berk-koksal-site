## Introduction

Greetings. Peace be upon you.

When someone is described as unyielding, what do we imagine? A person who will not abandon a principle, or someone who offers an opponent no mercy at all? The Turkish word *amansız* can evoke both relentless determination and frightening severity. When the word appears in a translation of the Quran, what should I understand? More importantly, how can I ask the question without presenting my private anger as sacred courage?

I begin with 5:54 because sternness does not stand alone there. It is situated alongside GOD's love, gentleness toward believers, striving, and freedom from fear of blame. Other passages use severe language: 9:73, 9:123, 48:29, and 66:6–9. I cannot pretend that they all address the same situation. One speaks directly to the prophet, another concerns armed resistance to attackers, another describes Muhammad and his companions, and another concerns angels guarding Hell. A similar English adjective does not make these different contexts interchangeable.

In <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/responding_to_evil_with_what_is_best_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Responding to Evil with What Is Best</a>, I considered forgiveness and proportionate justice. In <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/55-8-yasayi-cignemeyin-en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Do Not Transgress the Law</a>, I examined the relationship between a limit and equity. This article asks a different question. What do the Quran's statements about severity actually say, and what boundaries confront me when I am tempted to make sternness part of my own identity?

My purpose is to read these verses alongside relevant passages and consider what witnessing the oneness of GOD means for knowledge, truthfulness, justice, and submission. I am not searching for a doctrine of helplessness in the face of harm. But I do not wish to make contempt for others into a sign of spiritual strength. The demanding task is to stand firm where firmness is called for without dropping the measure of justice.

## 1. What Kind of Love Comes Before Sternness?

### Verse

> **5:54** — O  you  who  believe,  if  you  revert from  your  religion,  then  GOD  will substitute  in  your  place  people whom  He loves and who love Him. They  will  be  kind  with  the  believers, stern  with  the  disbelievers,  and  will strive  in  the  cause  of  GOD  without fear  of  any  blame.  Such  is  GOD’s blessing;  He  bestows  it  upon  whomever  He  wills.  GOD  is  Bounteous, Omniscient.

### Reflection on the Verse

The first thing that strikes me about 5:54 is that the most severe-sounding word does not stand at the beginning of the verse. It first speaks of people whom GOD loves and who love Him. Their manner toward believers, their firmness in the face of disbelief, and their willingness to strive without fear of blame follow. If I tear out one word and place it at the center of their character, I lose the order in which the verse speaks.

What does a person's severity serve? Is it a way to silence another person and enjoy the feeling of being powerful, or a willingness to stand by what is true even when the cost falls on oneself? Asking this question does not erase the firmness in the text. It is an attempt to put it where it belongs. I do not find in 5:54 a portrait of uncontrolled anger. I find love, commitment, and struggle brought together.

When I ask myself what makes me unyielding, I may not like the answer. Am I standing against injustice, or merely resenting the existence of people who disagree with me? Before claiming the quality described here, I have to face that difference.

Verse references: 5:54.

## 2. Is Gentleness a Form of Surrender?

### Verse

> **5:54** — O  you  who  believe,  if  you  revert from  your  religion,  then  GOD  will substitute  in  your  place  people whom  He loves and who love Him. They  will  be  kind  with  the  believers, stern  with  the  disbelievers,  and  will strive  in  the  cause  of  GOD  without fear  of  any  blame.  Such  is  GOD’s blessing;  He  bestows  it  upon  whomever  He  wills.  GOD  is  Bounteous, Omniscient.

### Reflection on the Verse

In 5:54, gentleness toward believers and sternness toward disbelievers appear side by side. I can read that contrast badly in two directions: by treating gentleness as weakness, or by treating sternness as permission to cause unlimited harm. Neither conclusion is contained in the sentence. I need to ask what these traits are directed toward, and what the surrounding verses allow me to infer.

Sometimes a person remains silent not because he is gentle, but because he is afraid to speak truthfully. At other times, someone speaks loudly not from courage, but from contempt. The test I hear in 5:54 is not a test of volume. Do I use my strength against someone vulnerable, or can I sacrifice my comfort rather than abandon a principle?

The gentleness mentioned here does not make every action of every believer beyond criticism. Nor can I conclude from this single sentence that every person of another faith is an enemy to be attacked. Those are further claims. Before making them, I have to listen to what other verses actually say.

Verse references: 5:54.

## 3. Does Fearlessness Mean Refusing Correction?

### Verse

> **5:54** — O  you  who  believe,  if  you  revert from  your  religion,  then  GOD  will substitute  in  your  place  people whom  He loves and who love Him. They  will  be  kind  with  the  believers, stern  with  the  disbelievers,  and  will strive  in  the  cause  of  GOD  without fear  of  any  blame.  Such  is  GOD’s blessing;  He  bestows  it  upon  whomever  He  wills.  GOD  is  Bounteous, Omniscient.

> **17:36** — You  shall  not  accept  any  information,  unless  you  verify  it  for  yourself. I  have  given  you  the  hearing,  the eyesight,  and  the  brain,  and  you  are responsible for using them.

### Reflection on the Verse

The freedom from fear of blame in 5:54 is moving. There are moments when speaking truthfully costs a person approval, belonging, or peace of mind. Yet fearlessness is not the same as infallibility. Someone may stop fearing condemnation and then decide that every criticism must be unjust.

That is why I read 17:36 alongside it. If information must be verified before I accept it, the same responsibility must apply to the claims I make. Otherwise, refusing to listen becomes a substitute for intellectual courage. I should be able to resist a flattering crowd and still hear a well-grounded objection.

Being right once does not make everything I say correct. Being criticized unfairly does not free me from answering good questions in the future. If remaining firm means being faithful to truth, I cannot exclude the courage to change my mind when truth demands it.

Verse references: 5:54; 17:36.

## 4. The Command to Strive: Who Is Addressed?

### Verse

> **9:73** — O  you  prophet,  strive  against  the disbelievers and the hypocrites, and be stern in dealing with them. Their destiny  is  Hell;  what  a  miserable abode!

> **66:9** — O prophet, struggle against the disbelievers and the hypocrites and be stern  with  them.  Their  abode  is Gehenna, and a miserable destiny. 

### Reflection on the Verse

Both 9:73 and 66:9 address the prophet with a call to struggle against disbelievers and hypocrites and to deal sternly with them. I would be changing their meaning if I pretended that sternness was absent. But I would also be making an unwarranted move if I treated every occurrence of striving as a command to fight with weapons. Verse 9:123 uses an explicit expression for fighting, making the distinction worth investigating.

This difference matters beyond vocabulary. Resisting wrongdoing, publicly challenging a falsehood, refusing to cooperate with oppression, and physically attacking someone are not interchangeable actions. Nor can I simply step into the position of the prophet being addressed and grant myself authority to punish whichever person I choose. The identity of the addressee matters.

Respect for a demanding verse requires more than repeating its strongest word. Sternness here is a real demand, but its target, form, and boundaries must not be surrendered to my private anger before I have studied its context.

Verse references: 9:73; 66:9.

## 5. Sternness in War: Who Is Attacking Whom?

### Verse

> **9:123** — O  you  who  believe,  you  shall  fight the  disbelievers  who  attack  you  −  let them  find  you  stern  −  and  know  that GOD is with the righteous.

### Reflection on the Verse

In 9:123, the issue becomes explicitly connected with fighting. The Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation identifies the people being fought as disbelievers who attack. The English text does the same. Removing that condition and presenting the verse as a limitless command against people merely because they believe differently would change the supplied wording.

A person under attack may have to defend life and safety with resolve. That is not the same as turning a difference of belief into a reason to choose a victim. The sternness described here is substantial. The enemy is to encounter resistance. Yet the final reference to righteousness reminds me that force does not become right simply because the person using it feels certain.

In an argument, my bruised pride can make an ordinary disagreement feel like a war. I begin to speak of enemies rather than people, battles rather than conversations. But an injured ego is not the condition described in 9:123. If I want to take the verse seriously, I must also take seriously the circumstances it names.

Verse references: 9:123.

## 6. The Moment Firmness Turns into Aggression

### Verse

> **2:190** — You may fight in the cause of GOD against those who attack you, but do not aggress. GOD does not love the aggressors.

> **2:193** — You may also fight them to eliminate  oppression,  and  to  worship GOD freely. If they refrain, you shall not  aggress;  aggression  is permitted only against the aggressors.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 2:190 puts a clear boundary beside the fighting language: responding to an attack and committing aggression are not the same. Verse 2:193 speaks about ending oppression and about refraining from aggression when the other side stops. This is not only a discussion of when force may be used. It is also about when force must be restrained.

Someone who began in self-defense can become driven by revenge. Having suffered harm does not mean that every response is just. Sometimes firmness requires persistence; sometimes it requires stopping at exactly the point where further action would be excessive. The word "limit" can sound modest, but for someone holding power it may be the hardest word of all.

These passages do not give me a complete answer to every modern conflict. They do, however, prevent me from dressing aggression in the language of devotion. Any reading of sternness that excuses aggression must be tested against these explicit limits.

Verse references: 2:190; 2:193.

## 7. Can Anger Cancel a Treaty?

### Verse

> **9:4** — If  the  idol  worshipers  sign  a  peace treaty  with  you,  and  do  not  violate it,  nor  band  together  with  others against  you,  you  shall  fulfill  your treaty  with  them,  until  the  expiration date. GOD loves the righteous.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 9:4 provides a concrete measure. If the other party has kept a peace treaty, the treaty must be honored until its term ends. The other party's idolatry does not itself cancel the obligation. This verse places a duty in front of me that I might conveniently forget if I spoke only about being stern.

We often measure strength by a person's refusal to give way. But keeping one's word is also a form of strength, especially when angry, especially when the promise was made to someone different from us, especially when others would applaud our betrayal. Loyalty to an obligation can demand more of me than forcing another person to yield.

The verse is not a comprehensive code for every private contract or international agreement. Yet it makes me examine a familiar temptation: to treat the commitments I have made as binding only while I feel friendly toward the person who received them. A principle that disappears when feelings change was never very secure.

Verse references: 9:4.

## 8. The Opponent's Safety: The Difficult Question of 9:6

### Verse

> **9:6** — If  one  of  the  idol  worshipers  sought safe  passage  with  you,  you  shall grant  him  safe  passage,  so  that  he can  hear  the  word  of  GOD,  then send  him  back  to  his  place  of  security.  That  is  because  they  are  people who do not know.

### Reflection on the Verse

When I place 9:6 near 9:73, I encounter a boundary I cannot ignore. An idol worshiper asks for safe passage; safe passage is to be granted, the person is allowed to hear GOD's words, and then is to be returned to a place of safety. I cannot overlook that sequence merely because I want the word "stern" to have unlimited reach.

Disagreeing with someone does not make that person's safety irrelevant. An opportunity to hear a message is not the same as being held under coercion. Nor does opening a conversation place the listener at the mercy of the speaker. The verse's concern for the security of a religious opponent is striking precisely because the surrounding discussion includes real conflict.

I do not claim that this scene resolves every modern legal question. But its sequence matters: request, protection, hearing, and safe return. Anyone who is tempted to turn scriptural sternness into disregard for an opponent's safety must answer the question this verse raises.

Verse references: 9:6.

## 9. Harshness and Mercy in the Same Verse

### Verse

> **48:29** — Muhammad—the  messenger  of GOD—and  those  with  him  are harsh and stern against the disbelievers, but kind and compassionate amongst themselves. You see them bowing and prostrating, as they seek GOD blessings and approval. Their marks are on their faces, because of prostrating. This is the same example as in the Torah. Their example in the Gospel is like plants that grow taller and stronger, and please the farmers. He thus enrages the disbelievers.  GOD  promises  those among them who believe and lead a righteous  life  forgiveness  and  a great recompense.

### Reflection on the Verse

In 48:29, harshness and sternness may be the first words that catch the eye. Yet the same passage speaks of kindness and compassion among the believers, and continues with their bowing, prostrating, and seeking GOD's blessing and approval. This community is not described through hostility alone. Its severe qualities are not presented as the entirety of its spiritual life.

This is a description of Muhammad and those with him. To turn it into an unrestricted personal license for anyone living centuries later requires more than a resemblance in tone. A harsh voice does not prove a person belongs among those commended in the passage. Nor does the verse teach me to identify someone's intentions by watching how forcefully they argue.

There is an uncomfortable question here. Can people be deeply compassionate toward members of their own community while forgetting the rights of outsiders? That possibility deserves scrutiny. Verses 60:8 and 5:8 prevent compassion and justice from being confined to whoever happens to be near me.

Verse references: 48:29.

## 10. Are the Stern Angels a Model for Human Anger?

### Verse

> **66:6** — O you who believe, protect yourselves and your families from the Hellfire whose fuel is people and rocks. Guarding it are stern  and powerful angels who never disobey GOD; they do whatever they are commanded to do.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 66:6 describes stern and powerful angels, and immediately connects those qualities to their obedience to GOD's commands. Their strength and their assigned task are inseparable in the description. The opening address, however, calls human beings to protect themselves and their families. Those are not the same roles.

A parent or community leader could read the verse and decide that being severe with people is a divine duty. But the passage does not appoint a human being as a guardian of Hell. The angels' assigned authority cannot be borrowed to justify punishment of my own invention. Protecting a family and ruling it by fear are not identical actions, and the verse does not make them identical.

In my earlier article <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/angels_unseen_world_divine_tasks_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The Unseen World of Angels</a>, I considered the difference between a task and ultimate authority. Here the same distinction becomes personal. When I take the strength of a being acting under command as a model for my own anger, have I mistaken my feelings for a command I was never given?

Verse references: 66:6.

## 11. Is Everyone Who Believes Differently an Enemy?

### Verse

> **60:8** — GOD  does  not  enjoin  you  from befriending  those  who  do  not  fight you  because  of  religion,  and  do  not evict  you  from  your  homes.  You  may befriend  them  and  be  equitable towards  them.  GOD  loves  the  equitable.

> **60:9** — GOD  enjoins  you  only  from  befriending  those  who  fight  you  because  of  religion,  evict  you  from your  homes,  and  band  together  with others  to  banish  you.  You  shall  not befriend  them.  Those  who  befriend them are the transgressors.

### Reflection on the Verse

Read together, 60:8 and 60:9 draw a distinction between people who do not fight or expel others on account of religion and those who do. The moral evaluation of a relationship is not reduced to a label attached to a group. Conduct, hostility, and expulsion matter.

That distinction has consequences for ordinary social life. A few painful experiences can become a judgment against an entire community. Soon I no longer see the individual in front of me; I see only the category I have assigned to that person. The permission to befriend and treat others equitably in 60:8 challenges that easy collective exclusion.

This does not mean that all disagreements between religious communities are insignificant. They may be serious. But the distinction made by these two verses prevents me from using the reality of some hostility as proof that everybody outside my own group is hostile. Firmness should include the discipline of identifying the actual wrongdoer.

Verse references: 60:8; 60:9.

## 12. Can Faith Be Produced by Force?

### Verse

> **2:256** — There shall be no compulsion in religion: the right way is now distinct from the wrong way. Anyone who denounces the devil and believes in GOD  has  grasped  the  strongest bond; one that never breaks. GOD is Hearer, Omniscient.

### Reflection on the Verse

The statement in 2:256 is brief, but it carries enormous weight in any discussion of religious sternness. Compulsion in religion is ruled out. If I turn the striving of 5:54, the severity of 9:73, or the harshness of 48:29 into a right to control another person's conscience by force, I have to answer for that conclusion before this verse.

I may desperately want someone I love to understand what I believe to be true. But wanting that and coercing the person are different things. Silencing a child's honest question, forcing an adult to repeat words of conviction, or using fear as a substitute for understanding does not establish sincere belief. I do not claim that 2:256 settles every aspect of education, family responsibility, or public law. Its explicit prohibition is nevertheless impossible to dismiss.

In <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/do-not-accept-without-verifying/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Do Not Accept Without Verifying</a>, I explored the responsibility of personal examination. Now I face a related question: can the certainty of my own belief replace another human being's responsibility to examine and choose? This verse gives me a reason to resist saying yes.

Verse references: 2:256.

## 13. The Difference Between a Firm Principle and a Hard Heart

### Verse

> **3:159** — It was mercy from GOD that you became  compassionate  towards them.  Had  you  been  harsh  and mean-hearted, they would have abandoned you. Therefore, you shall pardon them and ask forgiveness for them, and consult them. Once you make a decision, carry out your plan, and trust in GOD. GOD loves those who trust in Him.*

### Reflection on the Verse

I cannot discuss sternness fairly without reading 3:159. The passage brings compassion, pardon, consultation, decision, and trust in GOD together. It also warns that harshness and a mean heart would drive people away. How, then, could I make harshness an unconditional virtue?

There are moments when I think that speaking more sharply will make someone listen. Yet frightening a person is not proof that the person understands me. The verse suggests a form of leadership in which consultation does not erase decision and compassion does not cancel responsibility. Strength need not always announce itself through coldness.

I want to avoid two opposite claims. I cannot promise that gentle speech will resolve every dispute, because the verse does not promise that. But I also cannot display my rudeness as though it were the prophet's praised courage. A hard heart and a committed principle are not the same thing.

Verse references: 3:159.

## 14. Justice for the Person I Oppose

### Verse

> **5:8** — O  you  who  believe,  you  shall  be  absolutely  equitable,  and  observe GOD,  when  you  serve  as  witnesses. Do  not  be  provoked  by  your  conflicts  with  some  people  into  committing  injustice.  You  shall  be  absolutely  equitable,  for  it  is  more  righteous. You  shall  observe  GOD.  GOD  is fully  Cognizant  of  everything  you do.

> **4:135** — O  you who  believe,  you  shall  be absolutely  equitable,  and  observe GOD,  when  you  serve  as  witnesses, even  against  yourselves,  or  your parents,  or  your  relatives.  Whether the  accused  is  rich  or  poor,  GOD takes  care  of  both.  Therefore,  do not  be  biased  by  your  personal wishes.  If  you  deviate  or  disregard (this  commandment),  then  GOD  is fully  Cognizant  of  everything  you do.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 5:8 asks a difficult question: can I remain equitable toward people with whom I am in conflict? Verse 4:135 turns the same demand inward. Can I testify truthfully when the truth is against me, my parents, or my relatives? Together, these passages refuse to allow justice to become merely a shield for my own side.

If I want to understand sternness as steadfastness in truth, I have to be equally steadfast when fairness costs me something. It is easy to enlarge the wrong committed by an opponent and conceal the wrong committed by a friend. At that moment, I may no longer be fighting injustice; I may be protecting the pleasure of feeling superior. The louder I become, the easier it can be to overlook the weakness of my evidence.

Can I admit when an opponent is right? Can I refuse to excuse wrongdoing by someone close to me? These questions make 5:8 and 4:135 more demanding than any slogan about fearlessness. Severity that has lost justice cannot borrow justice's name.

Verse references: 5:8; 4:135.

## 15. Verbal Severity: Mistaking Insults for Truth

### Verse

> **6:108** — Do not curse the idols they set up beside GOD, lest they blaspheme and curse GOD, out of ignorance. We have adorned the works of every group in their eyes. Ultimately, they return to their Lord, then He informs them of everything they had done.

> **49:11** — O you who believe, no people shall ridicule other people, for they may be better than they. Nor shall any women ridicule other women, for they may be better than they. Nor shall you mock one another, or make fun of your names. Evil indeed is the reversion to wickedness after attaining faith. Anyone who does not repent after this, these are the transgressors.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 6:108 warns against cursing what others worship beside GOD, in part because of the insulting response it may provoke. Verse 49:11 warns against ridicule and mockery. Being able to say the most offensive thing about an idea I reject is therefore no proof that I have told the truth more faithfully.

Online, one angry sentence can reach hundreds of people before I have reconsidered it. An insult may turn an argument into a contest of humiliation. Sometimes I can be right about an issue and wrong in the way I treat a person. The difference between criticism and abuse matters precisely when I feel morally certain.

These verses do not prohibit every criticism of false beliefs. Criticism and cursing are not the same act. If I take 6:108 seriously, I must ask not only whether my objection is defensible, but also what my chosen words are likely to do. I cannot invoke the sternness of 9:73 as a reason to erase this boundary.

Verse references: 6:108; 49:11.

## 16. Another Kind of Struggle: Wisdom in Conversation

### Verse

> **16:125** — You shall invite to the path of your Lord  with  wisdom  and  kind  enlightenment, and debate with them in the best possible manner. Your Lord knows best who has strayed from His path, and He knows best who are the guided ones.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 16:125 speaks about how an invitation should be made: with wisdom, kind enlightenment, and the best possible manner of debate. It does not require abandoning conviction. It does insist that the way conviction is expressed carries moral significance. I find no basis here for claiming that harshness is the only language available to someone who believes strongly.

Sometimes I begin a discussion with the wrong goal. I want to win rather than understand, defeat a person rather than communicate a truth, answer a label rather than listen to a human being. It is easier to recognize that tendency in another person than in myself. The verse ends by placing the deepest knowledge of who has strayed and who has been guided with the Lord. I may offer evidence; I cannot claim the final verdict on another person's inner condition.

When firmness of speech is needed, perhaps the first discipline is to cite sources correctly and understand what the other person is actually saying. Disagreeing without contempt takes work. The finest possible debate is not necessarily the weakest one.

Verse references: 16:125.

## 17. Defending a Right Without Turning It into Revenge

### Verse

> **16:126** — And if you punish, you shall inflict an equivalent punishment. But if you resort to patience (instead of revenge), it would be better for the patient ones.

> **42:40** — Although  the  just  requital  for  an injustice  is  an  equivalent  retribution,  those  who  pardon  and  maintain  righteousness  are  rewarded  by GOD. He does not love the unjust.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verses 16:126 and 42:40 allow me to think about proportionate response and forgiveness in the same space. An equivalent response to a wrong is recognized, yet patience and pardon also have value. Justice is not exhausted by retaliation; its limits and the moral possibility of restraint matter as well.

Silence in the face of injustice is not always the most virtuous choice. I cannot condemn a harmed person simply for wanting to defend a right. But multiplying harm is not easily justified as justice either. Revenge often creates its own measuring system: the injured person seeks relief by passing suffering onward. The repeated emphasis on equivalence asks me to resist that temptation.

In <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/responding_to_evil_with_what_is_best_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Responding to Evil with What Is Best: Where Does Justice End and Forgiveness Begin?</a>, I examined the relationship between pardon and protecting one's rights. Here the question is different. At what moment does a resistance I call steadfast become a response that exceeds justice? I cannot use power confidently without being willing to ask.

Verse references: 16:126; 42:40.

## 18. What Will I Do When Peace Becomes Possible?

### Verse

> **8:61** — If they resort to peace, so shall you, and put your trust in GOD. He is the Hearer, the Omniscient.

> **41:34** — Not  equal  is  the  good  response  and the  bad  response.  You  shall  resort to  the  nicest  possible  response. Thus,  the  one  who  used  to  be  your enemy,  may  become  your  best friend.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 8:61 calls for answering an offer of peace with peace. Verse 41:34 holds out the possibility that the best response to an offense may transform a hostile relationship. Neither passage presents enmity as the only possible way for human beings to relate. There are moments for resistance and moments in which another path opens.

A person can become so attached to being the one who resists that the end of a conflict feels like a loss of identity. In that state, a peace proposal can appear unwelcome, not because justice requires more fighting but because anger has become familiar. Still, 41:34 does not guarantee that kindness will turn every enemy into a friend. It describes a possibility, not a mechanical outcome.

My question is simple: when rights can be protected and aggression has ended, do I search for new reasons to keep fighting? Verse 8:61 makes me consider the strength it takes to accept peace. Firmness is tested not only by the ability to continue, but also by the ability to stop when continuing is no longer justified.

Verse references: 8:61; 41:34.

## 19. Whose Authority Am I Defending: GOD's or My Anger?

### Verse

> **9:31** — They  have  set  up  their  religious leaders  and  scholars  as  lords,*  instead of GOD. Others deified the Messiah, son of Mary. They were all commanded  to  worship  only  one god. There is no god except He. Be He glorified, high above having any partners.

> **3:18** — GOD  bears  witness  that  there  is  no god except He, and so do the angels and  those  who  possess  knowledge. Truthfully  and  equitably,  He  is  the absolute  god;  there  is  no  god  but He, the Almighty, Most Wise.

### Reflection on the Verse

By the end of a study about severity, I have arrived at a question of authority. Verse 9:31 warns against elevating religious leaders and scholars to the position of lords instead of GOD. Verse 3:18 brings testimony, knowledge, justice, and GOD's oneness together. My claim to speak for truth does not automatically make my words true.

A religious leader, a researcher, a community, or I myself cannot turn personal preference into GOD's command. That matters especially when severity is being demanded. Anger combined with an unquestionable claim of authority can silence correction. If someone tells me to be merciless toward everybody who disagrees, I need to ask which verse is being invoked, which circumstances it addresses, and who is claiming the right to enforce it.

I cannot end this reflection by turning sternness into a virtue that swallows the whole of life. Scripture does contain serious language about struggle, firmness, and fighting; I do not wish to explain that language away. But the same scripture also speaks of justice, protection, keeping promises, freedom from coercion, mercy, and peace. If I select only the words that serve my ego, the center of my devotion may quietly move.

I want neither to bow before oppression nor to become oppressive while opposing it. The demanding work is to hold that line without handing responsibility for it to someone else.

Verse references: 9:31; 3:18.

## Arabic Roots: Is There Only One Kind of Sternness?

### Ayet

> **5:54** — O  you  who  believe,  if  you  revert from  your  religion,  then  GOD  will substitute  in  your  place  people whom  He loves and who love Him. They  will  be  kind  with  the  believers, stern  with  the  disbelievers,  and  will strive  in  the  cause  of  GOD  without fear  of  any  blame.  Such  is  GOD’s blessing;  He  bestows  it  upon  whomever  He  wills.  GOD  is  Bounteous, Omniscient.

> **9:73** — O  you  prophet,  strive  against  the disbelievers and the hypocrites, and be stern in dealing with them. Their destiny  is  Hell;  what  a  miserable abode!

> **9:123** — O  you  who  believe,  you  shall  fight the  disbelievers  who  attack  you  −  let them  find  you  stern  −  and  know  that GOD is with the righteous.

> **48:29** — Muhammad—the  messenger  of GOD—and  those  with  him  are harsh and stern against the disbelievers, but kind and compassionate amongst themselves. You see them bowing and prostrating, as they seek GOD blessings and approval. Their marks are on their faces, because of prostrating. This is the same example as in the Torah. Their example in the Gospel is like plants that grow taller and stronger, and please the farmers. He thus enrages the disbelievers.  GOD  promises  those among them who believe and lead a righteous  life  forgiveness  and  a great recompense.

> **66:6** — O you who believe, protect yourselves and your families from the Hellfire whose fuel is people and rocks. Guarding it are stern  and powerful angels who never disobey GOD; they do whatever they are commanded to do.

### Reflection on the Verse

The Turkish word *amansız* is a translation choice. The Arabic expressions rendered by it in these passages do not all come from the same root. That distinction matters. Yet identifying a root does not settle the meaning of a verse. The word still belongs to a sentence, an addressee, and a context.

**ع ز ز — ʿ-z-z.** The form **أَعِزَّةٍ** in 5:54 belongs to a field associated with strength, honor, and prevailing. Its translation as sternness should not automatically make it equivalent to pitilessness. The contrasting **أَذِلَّةٍ** describes the believers' manner toward other believers. These words create a meaningful contrast, not an independent verdict on every contemporary dispute.

**غ ل ظ — gh-l-ẓ.** The forms **وَٱغْلُظْ** in 9:73 and 66:9 and **غِلْظَةً** in 9:123 share this root, associated here with sternness and severity. **غِلَاظٌ** in 66:6 belongs to the same family. But an instruction addressed to the prophet and a description of angels assigned a task do not become the same command because they contain related vocabulary.

**ش د د — sh-d-d.** The words **أَشِدَّآءُ** in 48:29 and **شِدَادٌ** in 66:6 relate to strength and firmness. The nearby **رُحَمَآءُ** in 48:29 belongs to **ر ح م — r-ḥ-m**, a root associated with mercy. What draws my attention is that strength and compassion appear in the same description without one erasing the other.

The distinction between **ج ه د — j-h-d** and **ق ت ل — q-t-l** is equally important. **يُجَٰهِدُونَ** in 5:54 and **جَٰهِدِ** in 9:73 belong to the field of striving. **قَٰتِلُوا۟** in 9:123 explicitly refers to fighting. I should neither reduce every struggle to words alone nor translate every form of striving as armed combat.

Word roots do not give me a secret formula for deciding every conflict. They give me a more modest insight: several Arabic forms, addressees, and circumstances stand behind a single recurring Turkish adjective. I have to return each word to the complete verse rather than allowing an attractive etymology to govern the passage.

Verse references: 5:54; 9:73; 9:123; 48:29; 66:6; 66:9.

## Other Claims

### Verse

> **2:190** — You may fight in the cause of GOD against those who attack you, but do not aggress. GOD does not love the aggressors.

> **60:8** — GOD  does  not  enjoin  you  from befriending  those  who  do  not  fight you  because  of  religion,  and  do  not evict  you  from  your  homes.  You  may befriend  them  and  be  equitable towards  them.  GOD  loves  the  equitable.

### Reflection on the Verse

The modern Turkish dictionary offers a useful starting point. The Turkish Language Association's definition of *amansız* evokes a person or force that gives no respite (<a href="https://sozluk.gov.tr/gts?ara=amans%C4%B1z" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TDK dictionary</a>). This helps explain the force of the Turkish adjective, but a Turkish definition cannot by itself resolve the distinction between **أَعِزَّةٍ** in 5:54 and **وَٱغْلُظْ** in 9:73. For lexical and morphological comparison, the Quranic Arabic Corpus entries for <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/qurandictionary.jsp?q=Ezz" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">ع ز ز</a>, <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/qurandictionary.jsp?q=glZ" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">غ ل ظ</a>, and <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/qurandictionary.jsp?q=%24dd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">ش د د</a> are useful aids, not substitutes for the Authorized English Translation.

Historical and traditional approaches may place different weight on the forms of struggle in 9:73 and 66:9. Some emphasize armed confrontation in their discussion of the passages; others distinguish forms of verbal, social, and physical resistance. It would be premature to declare that every explanation has the same textual basis. An interpretation also needs to contend with the explicit fighting language of 9:123 and the prohibition of aggression in 2:190. Historical practices cannot silently become words that the verse itself never states.

A contemporary claim worth examining is that strong moral conviction always produces better moral conduct. A review of research on moral conviction reports a more complicated picture: deeply moralized attitudes are also associated, in some settings, with intolerance of disagreement and resistance to procedural resolutions of conflict (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32886586/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Skitka and colleagues, 2021</a>). This is not a scientific test of 5:54. It is a separate psychological reason to distinguish confidence from the refusal to examine one's actions.

Research on dehumanization offers another caution. A recent systematic review reports that dehumanization occurs in a variety of intergroup settings, while stronger relationships can help reduce it (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42096985/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Karantzas and Simpson, 2026</a>). The underlying studies have their own measures and limitations, and they cannot determine the meaning of revelation. They may, however, illuminate why a social identity label is a dangerous substitute for judging a person's actual conduct.

One extreme account would say that every call to sternness must mean unrestricted cruelty. Another would make every stern passage into nothing more than gentle persuasion. Neither does justice to the whole set of passages. There is genuine language of struggle, severity, and fighting; there are also explicit instructions concerning mercy, security, justice, noncoercion, and peace. Outside interpretations do not form the foundation of my reflection. The words of the verses remain its starting point and its limit.

Verse references: 5:54; 9:73; 9:123; 2:190; 60:8; 5:8; 8:61.

## Conclusion

### Verse

> **5:54** — O  you  who  believe,  if  you  revert from  your  religion,  then  GOD  will substitute  in  your  place  people whom  He loves and who love Him. They  will  be  kind  with  the  believers, stern  with  the  disbelievers,  and  will strive  in  the  cause  of  GOD  without fear  of  any  blame.  Such  is  GOD’s blessing;  He  bestows  it  upon  whomever  He  wills.  GOD  is  Bounteous, Omniscient.

> **5:8** — O  you  who  believe,  you  shall  be  absolutely  equitable,  and  observe GOD,  when  you  serve  as  witnesses. Do  not  be  provoked  by  your  conflicts  with  some  people  into  committing  injustice.  You  shall  be  absolutely  equitable,  for  it  is  more  righteous. You  shall  observe  GOD.  GOD  is fully  Cognizant  of  everything  you do.

> **8:61** — If they resort to peace, so shall you, and put your trust in GOD. He is the Hearer, the Omniscient.

### Reflection on the Verse

I began by asking what it means to be *amansız*—stern, unyielding, giving no respite. Now I see not a single emotion, but several kinds of conduct that must remain distinct. Verse 5:54 begins with love and speaks of striving without fear of blame. Verses 9:73 and 66:9 contain a demanding call to the prophet. Verse 9:123 refers to fighting attackers. Verse 48:29 places severity alongside compassion. Verse 66:6 describes powerful angels carrying out what they are commanded. A recurring adjective does not make all these scenes the same.

Other passages make the picture more demanding. Verse 2:190 forbids aggression. Verse 9:4 protects a treaty; 9:6 requires safe passage for an opponent seeking protection. Verse 60:8 preserves room for equity and friendship across religious difference. Verse 2:256 forbids compulsion in religion. Verse 5:8 refuses to let conflict justify injustice. Verse 3:159 values compassion and consultation. Verse 8:61 requires an answer of peace to a move toward peace. I do not use these passages to erase the severe ones. I read them together because they are all part of the text.

This does not supply a simple answer to every war, political conflict, or personal dispute. Human beings can misclassify events. We mistake criticism for attack, defense for revenge, fidelity to GOD for protecting our own status. For that reason, firmness must be measured by justice before it is celebrated. A severity that cannot admit wrongdoing on its own side has weakened its claim to stand for truth.

Perhaps the most dangerous form of unyieldingness is the one that silences conscience. A person who knows that he is acting wrongly but refuses to stop because he fears appearing weak has a deeper question to answer: whom does his determination serve? Resisting injustice can be difficult. Refusing to become unjust while resisting it may be harder still.

The thought I want to carry forward is this: **The moral value of firmness cannot be separated from what it opposes and the boundaries it respects.** Not yielding to oppression is not the same as forgetting another person's humanity. Invoking GOD's name does not cancel that distinction; it makes my responsibility toward it more serious.

The following 19 points are not new religious rules. They are questions and practical checks I can bring back to the verses considered here.

1. **Begin with the direction of love.** Does this severity arise from fidelity to GOD or the desire to protect my ego? (5:54)
2. **Do not confuse gentleness with weakness.** Keep the gentle side of the verse in view. (5:54)
3. **Do not use fearlessness to escape correction.** Rejecting unjust blame does not mean rejecting evidence. (5:54; 17:36)
4. **Identify the addressee.** Do not claim for yourself a power to punish merely because a verse addresses the prophet. (9:73; 66:9)
5. **Distinguish striving from fighting.** Pay attention to the actual action named in the verse. (9:73; 9:123)
6. **Do not erase the condition of attack.** Difference of belief alone is not the fighting condition stated here. (9:123; 2:190)
7. **Do not become an aggressor while defending yourself.** A just beginning does not remove every limit. (2:190; 2:193)
8. **Know when to stop.** Reconsider action once the grounds for it have changed. (2:193)
9. **Keep your word when angry.** Treaty obligations do not disappear with affection. (9:4)
10. **Respect a request for safety.** An opponent's protection can remain a duty. (9:6)
11. **Do not turn sternness and mercy into enemies.** Read both sides of the description. (48:29)
12. **Do not borrow the angels' assignment.** A heavenly task is not your personal authority to punish. (66:6)
13. **Do not make difference of faith equal hostility.** Examine conduct and the reality of attack. (60:8–9)
14. **Do not coerce conscience.** Preserve the difference between inviting belief and forcing compliance. (2:256)
15. **Do not praise a hard heart.** Remember compassion and consultation when making decisions. (3:159)
16. **Be just to those who oppose you.** Include your own failures in the accounting. (5:8; 4:135)
17. **Do not mistake insults for struggle.** The manner of your objection matters. (6:108; 49:11)
18. **Leave the door to peace open.** Consider proportion, patience, a better response, and the possibility of peace. (16:126; 42:40; 41:34; 8:61)
19. **Do not make a human being your lord.** Neither a leader's command nor your own interpretation becomes GOD's word. (9:31; 3:18)
