## Introduction

Peace to you.

When we say that someone committed a crime, we often think of a court and a public rule. When we say that someone committed a sin, we may think of conscience, faith, and accountability before GOD. The words carry different associations in ordinary speech. But those associations alone do not prove that the Quran divides every wrong into two separate categories.

The Turkish translation of 4:48 uses “suç” twice. A little later, 4:111 uses “günah.” When I look at the Arabic, I find that the two verses employ a word from the same root. That discovery changes the question. Am I examining the Quran’s language, or asking the Quran to endorse a distinction I already made in my own language?

This is not merely a vocabulary problem. If I reduce wrongdoing to what a human court can punish, I may overlook choices hidden from public view. If I treat every sin as a crime that I am entitled to punish, I may begin to claim authority over another person’s conscience. I do not want to smuggle either conclusion into the verses.

In <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/the-fine-line-between-proclaiming-and-proclaiming-yourself/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The Fine Line Between Proclaiming and Proclaiming Yourself</a>, I reflected on the difference between proclaiming what one knows and exalting oneself. That concern returns here. Translation choices can open an investigation; they do not by themselves finish it.

My purpose is to reflect on these verses together and ask how testimony to GOD’s oneness affects knowledge, justice, truthfulness, and submission. I am not writing to hand down a final judgment about particular people. I want to see the word clearly, take the act seriously, and then examine my own responsibility.

## 1. Two Uses of “Offense” in One Verse: Where Should the Question Begin?

### Verse

> **4:48**
>
> Arabic: إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَا يَغْفِرُ أَن يُشْرَكَ بِهِۦ وَيَغْفِرُ مَا دُونَ ذَٰلِكَ لِمَن يَشَآءُ وَمَن يُشْرِكْ بِٱللَّهِ فَقَدِ ٱفْتَرَىٰٓ إِثْمًا عَظِيمًا
>
> English: GOD  does  not  forgive  idolatry,* but  He  forgives  lesser  offenses  for whomever  He  wills.  Anyone  who sets  up  idols  beside  GOD,  has  forged a horrendous offense.
>
> Türkçe: TANRI putperestliği bağışlamaz,* fakat O irade ettiği kimse için daha küçük suçları bağışlar. TANRI’nın yanında put edinen kimse korkunç bir suç uydurmuş olur.

Source: <a href="https://kuranteyit.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">kuranteyit.com</a>

Translation: Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation; the Turkish text is the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation.

### Reflection on the Verse

When I approach 4:48 asking whether crime and sin are the same thing, I first need to resist placing my own vocabulary over the verse. In Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation, the verse uses the language of “offenses.” The Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation uses “suç,” or offense, twice. It does not use the Turkish word “günah” in that verse itself. The question is still worth asking, but the first task is to see what the text actually says.

4:48 is not presented as a comprehensive statute book for human courts. Its subject is GOD’s forgiveness, setting up idols beside Him, and the gravity of doing so. If I read “offense” solely through the modern criminal-law meaning of the word, I risk moving the focus away from the verse. Monotheism and accountability come before my preferred definitions.

I therefore want to begin with a different question: What Arabic expression stands behind the translated word, and how is that expression used elsewhere? The feeling a word evokes in my own language cannot become a more certain authority than the text I am trying to understand.

Verse references: 4:48.

## 2. The Same Arabic Word: “Offense” Here, “Sin” There

### Verse

> **4:111** — Anyone  who  earns  a  sin,  earns  it  to the  detriment  of  his  own  soul.  GOD is Omniscient, Most Wise.

> **4:48** — GOD  does  not  forgive  idolatry,* but  He  forgives  lesser  offenses  for whomever  He  wills.  Anyone  who sets  up  idols  beside  GOD,  has  forged a horrendous offense.

### Reflection on the Verse

The Arabic noun **إِثْم** in 4:111 belongs to the same root as **إِثْمًا** in 4:48. Yet the Turkish translation calls it “günah,” or sin, in 4:111, while using “suç,” or offense, in 4:48. The Authorized English Translation similarly chooses words suited to the sentence. That comparison unsettles any easy claim that the two modern labels form two sealed Quranic categories.

4:111 also directs responsibility back to the person who acts. Even if a wrong seems hidden, or nobody lodges a complaint, the verse does not describe the action as morally weightless. Its consequences reach the self. Whether another person has been harmed is an important question, but it cannot be the only question.

I cannot conclude that every Arabic term in the Quran means precisely the same thing. Nor can I treat a translator’s different English or Turkish word choices as an infallible taxonomy. My responsibility is to read words in their sentences, not to make a doctrine out of an index entry.

Verse references: 4:111, 4:48.

## 3. Placing the Blame on the Innocent: A Wrong That Creates Another Wrong

### Verse

> **4:112** — Anyone  who  earns  a  sin,  then  accuses an innocent person thereof, has  committed  a  blasphemy  and  a gross offense.

### Reflection on the Verse

4:112 moves from wrongdoing to the act of placing it on an innocent person. Its Arabic text contains **خَطِيئَة** (*khati’ah*) and **إِثْم** (*ithm*), then uses *ithm* again in describing the burden of the false accusation. This is a particularly revealing passage because the relations between the words matter more than a rigid English or Turkish chart.

An initial wrong can be compounded by a lie told to protect oneself. The innocent person then bears the social cost of someone else’s evasion: reputation, trust, and the possibility of a just hearing may all be damaged. It would be strange to discuss the fine distinctions between sin and crime while overlooking the person actually harmed.

So I ask myself something practical. When the truth would make me uncomfortable, whose name do I put forward instead of my own? In a family dispute, a workplace disagreement, or a public accusation, can I preserve the truth even when doing so exposes my own mistake? This is not a new legal procedure; it is the moral weight I hear in 4:112.

Verse references: 4:112.

## 4. Fabricating Statements about GOD: An Offense of Speech

### Verse

> **4:50** — Note  how  they  fabricate  lies  about GOD; what a gross offense this is!

### Reflection on the Verse

In 4:50 an offense is connected with fabricating lies about GOD. That moves the reflection beyond visibly harmful physical actions. Speech and claims to religious knowledge can bear serious moral weight. A person may be speaking with confidence while attributing to revelation a statement that came from their own imagination.

This warning especially concerns me when writing about the Quran. Studying an ayah does not give me permission to turn every interpretation I produce into a divine ruling. Even years of research do not erase the boundary between the words of a verse and the thought of the person reading it.

I want these reflections to be useful and open to careful examination. The danger would be to make my own authority part of the message. The question “What does GOD say?” must not quietly become “What have I decided GOD must have said?” Keeping those questions separate is itself a form of truthfulness.

Verse references: 4:50.

## 5. The Subtle Wrong of Declaring Oneself Pure

### Verse

> **4:49** — Have  you  noted  those  who  exalt themselves?  Instead,  GOD  is  the One  who  exalts  whomever  He  wills, without the least injustice.

> **53:32** — They avoid gross sins and transgressions,  except  for  minor  offenses. Your Lord’s forgiveness is immense. He has been fully aware of you since He initiated you from the earth, and while you were embryos in your mothers’ bellies. Therefore, do not exalt yourselves; He is fully aware of the righteous.

### Reflection on the Verse

It is remarkably easy to identify other people’s faults while building a private certificate of innocence for myself. 4:49 challenges self-exaltation, and 53:32 warns against exalting ourselves. I may be able to name an action that is wrong without thereby becoming the final judge of my own righteousness.

This matters in a reflection about sin because a classification can become a means of superiority. I may label another person’s behavior as a serious offense, then minimize a comparable weakness of my own. I may be quicker to condemn than to investigate whether the warning also applies to me.

The corrective is not to avoid moral distinctions altogether. It is to remember whose knowledge is complete. GOD’s judgment and my confidence about myself are not interchangeable. A serious investigation should make me more truthful, not more satisfied with my own rank.

Verse references: 4:49, 53:32.

## 6. Reading the Warning about Forgiveness Alongside Its Companion Verse

### Verse

> **4:116** — GOD  does  not  forgive  idol  worship (if  maintained  until  death),*  and  He forgives  lesser  offenses  for  whomever  He  wills.  Anyone  who  idolizes any  idol  beside  GOD  has  strayed  far astray.

> **4:48** — GOD  does  not  forgive  idolatry,* but  He  forgives  lesser  offenses  for whomever  He  wills.  Anyone  who sets  up  idols  beside  GOD,  has  forged a horrendous offense.

### Reflection on the Verse

If I read 4:48 in isolation, I might turn the warning about unforgiven idolatry into a declaration of hopelessness for every person at every stage of life. But 4:116 returns to the same issue, and the Turkish translation supplied with the Authorized English Translation explicitly includes the parenthetical condition about continuing until death. In the Authorized English Translation, that clarification also appears in 4:116.

This comparison does not weaken the warning against idolatry. It helps me resist claiming knowledge I have not been given. Forgiveness belongs to GOD; another person’s final condition is not something I can announce through a label.

A verse that speaks so seriously about setting up idols deserves careful reading with the other verses that bear on repentance. The strongest sounding isolated sentence is not always the most responsible complete reading. I would rather slow down than claim an authority that the text has not assigned to me.

Verse references: 4:116, 4:48.

## 7. Divine Accountability Does Not Equal Human Coercion

### Verse

> **2:256** — There shall be no compulsion in religion: the right way is now distinct from the wrong way. Anyone who denounces the devil and believes in GOD  has  grasped  the  strongest bond; one that never breaks. GOD is Hearer, Omniscient.

> **4:48** — GOD  does  not  forgive  idolatry,* but  He  forgives  lesser  offenses  for whomever  He  wills.  Anyone  who sets  up  idols  beside  GOD,  has  forged a horrendous offense.

### Reflection on the Verse

4:48 concerns GOD’s forgiveness, whereas 2:256 says that there is no compulsion in religion. Read together, they warn me against turning a statement about divine accountability into a license for one human being to force another person’s belief.

The gravity of idolatry in 4:48 does not itself give a detailed earthly penalty for a person whose beliefs differ from mine. The judgment of belief and the obligations people owe one another should not be collapsed into a single impulse to punish. Otherwise, I could end up claiming to defend monotheism while treating my own judgment as an authority over another person’s conscience.

When persuasion fails, the temptation to compel can follow. For me, 2:256 is a limit on that temptation. This is not a full theory of law; it is a careful refusal to extract from these two verses a power they do not explicitly grant.

Verse references: 2:256, 4:48.

## 8. The Breadth of Mercy: Hope Without Evasion

### Verse

> **39:53** — Proclaim: “O My servants who exceeded the limits, never despair of GOD’s mercy. For GOD forgives all sins. He is the Forgiver, Most Merciful.”

> **4:48** — GOD  does  not  forgive  idolatry,* but  He  forgives  lesser  offenses  for whomever  He  wills.  Anyone  who sets  up  idols  beside  GOD,  has  forged a horrendous offense.

### Reflection on the Verse

I do not want to erase the gravity of 4:48 when reading 39:53. Nor do I want to erase the breadth of mercy in 39:53 when reading 4:48. The challenge is to let both passages address me, rather than selecting one to justify fear or the other to guarantee comfort.

What is my hope actually for? Do I hope so that I can turn away from my wrongdoing, or so that I can continue it with less anxiety? The verses direct my attention to GOD as the One who forgives. They do not grant me the power to declare my own eventual acceptance, but neither do they invite me to make a final judgment of despair.

The words “crime” and “sin” can help us ask ethical questions. They cannot by themselves close the question of mercy. That belongs in the much larger context of responsibility, repentance, and GOD’s knowledge.

Verse references: 39:53, 4:48.

## 9. The Timing of Turning Back Matters More Than the Label

### Verse

> **4:17** — Repentance is acceptable by GOD from those who fall in sin out of ignorance,  then  repent  immediately thereafter.  GOD  redeems  them. GOD is Omniscient, Most Wise. 

> **4:18** — Not acceptable is the repentance of those who commit sins until death comes to them, then say, “Now I repent.”  Nor  is  it  acceptable  from those who die as disbelievers. For these,  we  have  prepared  a  painful retribution.

> **39:54** — You shall obey your Lord, and submit to Him totally, before the retribution  overtakes  you,  then  you cannot be helped.

### Reflection on the Verse

Calling something a “lesser offense” does not give me a promise that I can postpone confronting it forever. 4:17 and 4:18 require us to attend to the time of repentance; 39:54 presses the call to return before retribution overtakes a person. This is a very different concern from finding the most favorable label for a deed.

I can imagine a future version of myself who finally changes. But I do not know how much future I have. If I use word distinctions to delay responsibility, my study has become a defense mechanism rather than a path to truth.

None of this authorizes me to decide whether another particular person’s repentance has been accepted. The verses are sufficient to make me examine the time available to me now, without claiming to know the final judgment of another soul.

Verse references: 4:17, 4:18, 39:54.

## 10. Wronging the Self: The Unseen Side of Responsibility

### Verse

> **4:110** — Anyone  who  commits  evil,  or wrongs  his  soul,  then  implores  GOD for  forgiveness,  will  find  GOD  Forgiving, Most Merciful.

> **42:25** — He  is  the  One  who  accepts  the  repentance  from  His  servants,  and remits  the  sins.  He  is  fully  aware  of everything you do.

### Reflection on the Verse

4:110 speaks of committing evil or wronging one’s own soul, and 42:25 speaks of GOD accepting repentance and remitting sins. These verses make it difficult for me to restrict moral wrongdoing to actions that attract a public complaint. There is also the way a person deals with their own life before GOD.

This does not mean that suffering is proof of sin, or that every painful event must have been deserved. The verses do not make such a universal accusation. I am speaking about choices for which a person bears responsibility, not about turning misfortune into a verdict.

The question I want to put to myself is not simply “Was I caught?” It is whether I made a choice that I need to acknowledge and change. Concealment from others does not settle the question; the possibility of seeking forgiveness remains part of the Quranic picture.

Verse references: 4:110, 42:25.

## 11. Major Sins and Minor Offenses: Distinctions Without a Private Ranking System

### Verse

> **4:31** — If you refrain from committing the gross sins that are prohibited for you, we will remit your sins, and admit you an honorable admittance.

> **53:32** — They avoid gross sins and transgressions,  except  for  minor  offenses. Your Lord’s forgiveness is immense. He has been fully aware of you since He initiated you from the earth, and while you were embryos in your mothers’ bellies. Therefore, do not exalt yourselves; He is fully aware of the righteous.

### Reflection on the Verse

4:31 refers to gross sins, and 53:32 places gross sins, transgressions, and minor offenses within the same passage. So it would be inaccurate to say that the Quran makes no distinctions of gravity. But it would also be inaccurate to assume that my own list of ranks is itself the Quran’s complete classification.

There is a reason I keep returning to the end of 53:32. The warning not to exalt ourselves checks the way I might use the beginning of that verse. “My mistake was minor; theirs was major” can be an attractive argument when I want to feel innocent.

I want to hold two truths together: actions are not automatically identical in weight, and the person weighing them must be humble about their own knowledge. Calling a wrong “minor” is not a reason to celebrate it or to stop seeking correction.

Verse references: 4:31, 53:32.

## 12. Necessity and Intention: Similar Acts, Different Circumstances

### Verse

> **2:173** — He  only  prohibits  for  you  the  eating of  animals  that  die  of  themselves (without  human  interference),  blood, the  meat  of  pigs,  and  animals  dedicated  to  other  than  GOD.  If  one  is forced  (to  eat  these),  without  being malicious  or  deliberate,  he  incurs  no sin.  GOD  is  Forgiver,  Most  Merciful.

### Reflection on the Verse

2:173 addresses a particular prohibition and a case of necessity in which malice and deliberateness are absent. I should not transform this specific passage into a universal exemption for every situation. Yet it reminds me that outward appearances do not always reveal all the morally relevant circumstances.

Two actions may look similar while the conditions under which they occurred were very different. Coercion, knowledge, and available choices can matter. I cannot know another person’s hidden circumstances simply because I know the outward description of an act.

The opposite caution matters too. I must not invent coercion to excuse a choice I made freely. The verse should make me more careful with evidence, not more inventive in explaining away my own conduct.

Verse references: 2:173.

## 13. Taking Another Person’s Rights: From Vocabulary to Harm

### Verse

> **2:188** — You  shall  not  take  each  others’ money illicitly, nor shall you bribe the officials to deprive others of some of their rights illicitly, while you know.

### Reflection on the Verse

2:188 addresses taking another person’s money illicitly and bribing officials to deprive people of their rights. It is striking how quickly a discussion of terminology can become an excuse to look away from someone who has actually been harmed. Finding the perfect word for a wrong does not return the money or restore the right.

This passage puts honesty, authority, and material interests into the same frame. An act may be hidden behind paperwork or defended through procedure, but the question of knowingly depriving another person remains. I am not saying every disputed commercial transaction is the same as the conduct described in 2:188. I am saying that a formal appearance should not end my ethical inquiry.

My practical question is simple and uncomfortable: What belongs to someone else that I am treating as mine? If I have taken a right, what can I do to make it good? A root dictionary cannot perform that work for me.

Verse references: 2:188.

## 14. Life and Dignity: Where Sin and Offense Touch Human Experience

### Verse

> **17:31** — You  shall  not  kill  your  children  due to  fear  of  poverty.  We  provide  for them,  as  well  as  for  you.  Killing  them is a gross offense.

> **17:32** — You  shall  not  commit  adultery;  it  is a gross sin, and an evil behavior.

### Reflection on the Verse

17:31 describes the killing of children as a gross offense. The Turkish translation of 17:32 uses both the language of offense and gross sin in speaking about adultery. These passages make it even harder to imagine that the two Turkish terms identify wholly separate, mutually exclusive realms. The subject of each verse matters as much as the selected word.

I do not derive a complete criminal procedure or a right to punish particular people from these two quotations. To label an act is not to reduce a person’s entire life to that label. The protection of life, the moral seriousness of relationships, and the obligation to avoid unjust assumptions must not disappear behind the argument over terminology.

An ethical reading should keep the people involved visible. If the vocabulary becomes more important than human life, truth, and justice, I have lost the very responsibility I was trying to investigate.

Verse references: 17:31, 17:32.

## 15. Bearing Witness Against Oneself: The Cost of Truthfulness

### Verse

> **4:135** — O  you who  believe,  you  shall  be absolutely  equitable,  and  observe GOD,  when  you  serve  as  witnesses, even  against  yourselves,  or  your parents,  or  your  relatives.  Whether the  accused  is  rich  or  poor,  GOD takes  care  of  both.  Therefore,  do not  be  biased  by  your  personal wishes.  If  you  deviate  or  disregard (this  commandment),  then  GOD  is fully  Cognizant  of  everything  you do.

### Reflection on the Verse

4:135 brings justice into the place where it hurts most: testimony that may go against my own interest. Can I disclose the detail that makes me look wrong? Can I stop defending myself long enough to admit what actually happened? These are more difficult tests than simply learning how to define “sin.”

The case of the falsely accused innocent person in 4:112 comes back to mind. When my interests are at stake, bending the facts can become a second wrong. Whether the person involved is wealthy or poor, close to me or distant, should not replace truthful testimony.

I do not have to pretend that I possess information I do not have. Justice may require admitting uncertainty as much as speaking confidently. The practical point is not a new judicial rule: the call to fairness should reach me even when I stand to lose.

Verse references: 4:135.

## 16. Anger Does Not Rewrite Justice

### Verse

> **5:8** — O  you  who  believe,  you  shall  be  absolutely  equitable,  and  observe GOD,  when  you  serve  as  witnesses. Do  not  be  provoked  by  your  conflicts  with  some  people  into  committing  injustice.  You  shall  be  absolutely  equitable,  for  it  is  more  righteous. You  shall  observe  GOD.  GOD  is fully  Cognizant  of  everything  you do.

### Reflection on the Verse

5:8 insists on fairness even where relationships are marked by conflict. It makes me think about how easily the word “criminal” or “sinner” can become a weapon in the hands of anger. Even when I know that someone has done wrong, I am not thereby granted the freedom to distort evidence or deny that person fair treatment.

A label can sometimes be the beginning of no longer listening. I want to avoid that shortcut. Truthfulness means acknowledging wrong conduct without adding imaginary charges. Justice means not reserving basic fairness only for people I like.

These verses do not settle every practical dispute. They do, however, give a clear boundary for my conduct: hostility does not make injustice acceptable. Another person’s wrong does not automatically authorize mine.

Verse references: 5:8.

## 17. No One Carries the Burden of Another

### Verse

> **6:164** — Say,  “Shall  I  seek  other  than  GOD as a lord, when He is the Lord of all things?  No  soul  benefits  except from  its  own  works,  and  none  bears the  burden  of  another.  Ultimately, you  return  to  your  Lord,  then  He informs  you  regarding  all  your  disputes.”

> **4:111** — Anyone  who  earns  a  sin,  earns  it  to the  detriment  of  his  own  soul.  GOD is Omniscient, Most Wise.

### Reflection on the Verse

6:164 returns responsibility to each soul, and 4:111 likewise describes sin as acquired against oneself. I hear a warning against two contrary habits: placing my own burden on another person and hiding behind another person to escape it.

A leader, teacher, or family member may have influenced me. That influence can be real without making my own decisions vanish. At the same time, I cannot pretend to know every person’s degree of understanding, freedom, or constraint. These verses are not a license for sweeping judgments about lives I have not lived.

The personal question is worth keeping: when a mistake becomes visible, whose name do I mention first? If I always begin with someone else, perhaps I have not yet examined my own share honestly.

Verse references: 6:164, 4:111.

## 18. Declaring Something Forbidden Without Knowledge: The Boundary of Religious Authority

### Verse

> **17:36** — You  shall  not  accept  any  information,  unless  you  verify  it  for  yourself. I  have  given  you  the  hearing,  the eyesight,  and  the  brain,  and  you  are responsible for using them.

> **16:116** — You shall not utter lies with your own tongues stating: “This is lawful, and this is unlawful,” to fabricate lies and attribute them to GOD. Surely, those who fabricate lies and attribute them to GOD will never succeed.

### Reflection on the Verse

17:36 calls for verification; 16:116 warns against fabricating permissions and prohibitions and attributing them to GOD. Together, they put a moral limit on this very study. I cannot turn my chosen vocabulary for sin and crime into an unquestionable religious decree.

One search result may give me the word I wanted. Another translation may use a different term. It is tempting to stop there and use the match as a verdict. But the sentence, neighboring verses, and the limits of my own knowledge still matter.

In an earlier reflection, <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/the-fine-line-between-proclaiming-and-proclaiming-yourself/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The Fine Line Between Proclaiming and Proclaiming Yourself</a>, I explored the distance between making a message clear and proclaiming one’s own authority. The same boundary applies here. A quotation is not my interpretation, and my interpretation must not masquerade as a quotation from GOD.

Verse references: 17:36, 16:116.

## 19. The Record and the Possibility of Turning: From Words to Life

### Verse

> **18:49** — The record will be shown, and you will see the guilty fearful of its contents. They will say, “Woe to us. How come this book leaves nothing, small or large, without counting it?” They will find everything they had done brought forth. Your Lord is never unjust towards anyone.

> **25:70** — Exempted  are  those  who  repent, believe, and lead a righteous life. GOD  transforms  their  sins  into credits. GOD is Forgiver,  Most Merciful.

### Reflection on the Verse

18:49 pictures an accounting that leaves neither the small nor the large unexamined; 25:70 speaks of repentance, belief, and righteous living in connection with transformation. Together they keep the reflection from becoming either a word game or a declaration that change is impossible.

I cannot measure GOD’s assessment of an act solely by the label assigned to it in a human legal system. If I have accused the innocent, I should seek to correct the accusation. If I have withheld a right, I should consider how to restore it. If I have built my righteousness out of someone else’s failure, I should reexamine my own heart. These are practical questions linked to the responsibility in the verses, not a complete code of law.

I also recall my earlier essay, <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/wisdom_behind_apparent_evil_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The Wisdom Behind Events That Seem Evil</a>, where I considered the relationship between wisdom and justice. A compelling explanation must not become an excuse to hide an actual wrong. The same is true of linguistic precision: it is valuable only if it helps me become more truthful.

Verse references: 18:49, 25:70.

## On the Arabic Word Roots

### Verse

> **4:48** — GOD  does  not  forgive  idolatry,* but  He  forgives  lesser  offenses  for whomever  He  wills.  Anyone  who sets  up  idols  beside  GOD,  has  forged a horrendous offense.

> **4:111** — Anyone  who  earns  a  sin,  earns  it  to the  detriment  of  his  own  soul.  GOD is Omniscient, Most Wise.

> **4:112** — Anyone  who  earns  a  sin,  then  accuses an innocent person thereof, has  committed  a  blasphemy  and  a gross offense.

> **4:31** — If you refrain from committing the gross sins that are prohibited for you, we will remit your sins, and admit you an honorable admittance.

> **18:49** — The record will be shown, and you will see the guilty fearful of its contents. They will say, “Woe to us. How come this book leaves nothing, small or large, without counting it?” They will find everything they had done brought forth. Your Lord is never unjust towards anyone.

> **39:53** — Proclaim: “O My servants who exceeded the limits, never despair of GOD’s mercy. For GOD forgives all sins. He is the Forgiver, Most Merciful.”

### Reflection on the Verse

The noun **إِثْم** (*ithm*, root **أ ث م**) links **إِثْمًا** in 4:48 and **إِثْمًا** in 4:111. The Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation renders the former as “suç,” or offense, and the latter as “günah,” or sin. A variation in translation vocabulary is not the same as a change of Arabic root.

4:112 places **خَطِيٓـَٔةً** (*khati'atan*, root **خ ط أ**) beside **إِثْمًا**, then uses **إِثْمًا** again later in the sentence. This is a useful warning against assuming a one-to-one mapping between every Arabic term and one modern-language label. These distinctions matter linguistically, but they do not create a complete ranking or punishment system by themselves.

**ٱلذُّنُوبَ** (*dhunub*, root **ذ ن ب**) provides another vocabulary of sins in 39:53. **سَيِّـَٔاتِكُمْ** (*sayyi'at*, root **س و أ**) appears in the Arabic of 4:31. **ٱلْمُجْرِمِينَ** (*mujrimin*, root **ج ر م**) appears in 18:49, where the Authorized English Translation speaks of the guilty. The roots overlap in moral discussion without becoming identical in every sentence.

The verb **ٱفْتَرَىٰٓ** (*iftara*, root **ف ر ي**) in 4:48 concerns fabrication, while **يَغْفِرُ** (*yaghfiru*, root **غ ف ر**) anchors the statement about forgiveness. Neither word, isolated from its syntax, can substitute for the verse as a whole.

For linguistic cross-checking, see the <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/treebank.jsp?chapter=4&verse=48" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Quranic Arabic Corpus on 4:48</a>, its <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/wordbyword.jsp?chapter=4&verse=112" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">4:112 analysis</a>, and its <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/wordbyword.jsp?chapter=39&verse=53" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">39:53 analysis</a>. These are linguistic aids, not a replacement for Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation.

Verse references: 4:48; 4:111–112; 4:31; 18:49; 39:53.

## Other Claims

### Verse

> **4:48** — GOD  does  not  forgive  idolatry,* but  He  forgives  lesser  offenses  for whomever  He  wills.  Anyone  who sets  up  idols  beside  GOD,  has  forged a horrendous offense.

> **4:116** — GOD  does  not  forgive  idol  worship (if  maintained  until  death),*  and  He forgives  lesser  offenses  for  whomever  He  wills.  Anyone  who  idolizes any  idol  beside  GOD  has  strayed  far astray.

> **2:256** — There shall be no compulsion in religion: the right way is now distinct from the wrong way. Anyone who denounces the devil and believes in GOD  has  grasped  the  strongest bond; one that never breaks. GOD is Hearer, Omniscient.

> **39:53** — Proclaim: “O My servants who exceeded the limits, never despair of GOD’s mercy. For GOD forgives all sins. He is the Forgiver, Most Merciful.”

### Reflection on the Verse

A common modern distinction separates crime, understood in terms of enforceable criminal law, from sin, understood in terms of religious or moral responsibility. It can be a useful distinction for a particular purpose. For instance, the <a href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/crime" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Cornell Legal Information Institute</a> defines crime with reference to conduct punishable under law. But that legal definition describes a human institutional framework, not the full meaning of every instance of **إِثْم** in the Quran. Laws themselves can differ from one jurisdiction to another.

Other approaches assume either that every legally punishable wrong must be a sin, or that every sin must become an offense for humans to punish. Neither sweeping claim follows simply from the vocabulary of 4:48. The passage concerns divine forgiveness; 2:188 addresses illicit taking and bribery; 2:256 rejects compulsion in religion. Conflating all these passages into one rule about human punishment would be a very large additional claim.

Some traditional classifications assign fixed levels or definitions to terms such as *ithm, dhanb, khati'ah, sayyi'ah,* and *jurm*. Such classifications may be useful when their limits are acknowledged. They should not replace the actual relation of the words in 4:48, 4:111, and 4:112. The passages invite attention to usage, not confidence in a ready-made table.

Another debate concerns how 4:48 relates to 39:53. Some readings stress the apparent absoluteness of the warning; others consider repentance before death and the clarification appearing in 4:116. My reflection rests instead on reading the supplied verses alongside one another without taking control of another person’s final judgment. I cannot turn a theological discussion into knowledge of the unseen.

Verse references: 4:48; 4:111–112; 4:116; 2:188; 2:256; 4:17–18; 39:53–54; 25:70.

## Conclusion

### Verse

> **4:48** — GOD  does  not  forgive  idolatry,* but  He  forgives  lesser  offenses  for whomever  He  wills.  Anyone  who sets  up  idols  beside  GOD,  has  forged a horrendous offense.

> **4:111** — Anyone  who  earns  a  sin,  earns  it  to the  detriment  of  his  own  soul.  GOD is Omniscient, Most Wise.

> **4:112** — Anyone  who  earns  a  sin,  then  accuses an innocent person thereof, has  committed  a  blasphemy  and  a gross offense.

> **39:53** — Proclaim: “O My servants who exceeded the limits, never despair of GOD’s mercy. For GOD forgives all sins. He is the Forgiver, Most Merciful.”

> **25:70** — Exempted  are  those  who  repent, believe, and lead a righteous life. GOD  transforms  their  sins  into credits. GOD is Forgiver,  Most Merciful.

### Reflection on the Verse

I began with two labels—crime and sin—and an impulse to draw a line between them. But the relationship between *ithm* in 4:48 and 4:111 makes that line less simple than it first appeared. The words in 4:112 reinforce the need to follow usage in context. The Quran speaks of wrongdoing with different degrees and emphases; it does not have to ratify every modern vocabulary chart I create.

There is the gravity of setting up idols beside GOD. There is the wrong of accusing an innocent person, depriving someone of their rights, bending testimony, letting hostility displace justice, and claiming religious certainty without knowledge. Each passage confronts another dimension of responsibility. Choosing one English label over another does not erase what I owe the truth.

I find myself lingering between 4:111 and 4:135. One returns the burden of sin to the person who acts; the other asks for justice even when testimony works against oneself. That seems a much more demanding measure of life than simply deciding who deserves which label.

And then there are 39:53 and 4:116. The final judgment concerning forgiveness is not mine to issue. I do not want to minimize wrongdoing or despair of mercy. I want hope to lead to an honest change of direction, not to an excuse for delay.

So my answer is deliberately careful. **Crime and sin can describe different kinds of responsibility in modern language, but the use of “offense” in 4:48 and “sin” in 4:111 does not establish two sealed, unchanging Quranic categories.** The enduring task is to become more truthful, more just, and more willing to submit to GOD.

The following nineteen prompts are not new religious rules. They are questions I am leaving for myself after reading the verses.

1. **Check the word before constructing a verdict.** (4:48; 4:111)
2. **Do not mistake a translation choice for a divine taxonomy.** (4:48; 4:112)
3. **Take responsibility for your own wrong.** (4:111)
4. **Do not shift blame onto the innocent.** (4:112)
5. **Do not claim knowledge about GOD that you lack.** (4:50; 16:116)
6. **Do not exalt yourself at another person’s expense.** (4:49; 53:32)
7. **Do not claim authority over forgiveness.** (4:48; 4:116)
8. **Do not try to compel belief.** (2:256)
9. **Do not despair of mercy.** (39:53)
10. **Do not postpone change to an unknown tomorrow.** (4:17–18; 39:54)
11. **Recognize the wrong you can do to yourself.** (4:110)
12. **Do not make a “minor” wrong into a permission.** (4:31; 53:32)
13. **Do not confuse compulsion with free choice.** (2:173)
14. **Respect other people’s property and rights.** (2:188)
15. **Protect life and human dignity.** (17:31–32)
16. **Give fair testimony even against yourself.** (4:135)
17. **Do not let anger rewrite justice.** (5:8)
18. **Do not hide behind others to avoid your own burden.** (6:164)
19. **Choose a concrete step toward correction.** (18:49; 25:70)
