# [28:59] Annihilation: Why Are Communities Destroyed, and What Warnings Do We Ignore?

**Author:** Berk KÖKSAL  
**Date:** November 19, 2026  
**Place:** Mersin

Peace be upon you.

When a community is annihilated, what remains? Ruins, perhaps. A name. A future that never arrived. I can feel the temptation to move past this subject quickly, because thinking about disappearance also brings me face to face with my own mortality. Yet the Quranic passages about annihilation do not merely describe the magnitude of destruction. They ask what human beings do when they are warned.

I begin with 28:59. As I ask why GOD would annihilate a community, I must be careful not to appoint myself the one who judges every life. I do not possess the complete account of people I have never known. I can contemplate the specific events described in the verses without turning the suffering of a living person into evidence of a secret divine verdict I have not been given.

Two kinds of ending need to remain distinct: the destruction of communities described in connection with their conduct, and the mortality shared by everyone on earth. The first does not make every death proof of guilt. The second does not make every ending an arbitrary punishment. Unless I preserve this distinction, the verses about the Hereafter, mercy, justice, and rescue become easy to misunderstand.

Bearing witness to the oneness of GOD should also matter for how I handle knowledge, truthful speech, justice, and submission. I want to read this subject in that spirit, not as an opportunity to claim superiority over the fate of others.

For me, the hardest question is not when someone else might be destroyed. It is more personal: **While I still have time, which warning am I ignoring, and which truth am I postponing living?**

## 1. Annihilation: What Must Happen Before a Community Is Destroyed?

### Verse

> **28:59 — العربية**
> وَمَا كَانَ رَبُّكَ مُهْلِكَ ٱلْقُرَىٰ حَتَّىٰ يَبْعَثَ فِىٓ أُمِّهَا رَسُولًا يَتْلُوا۟ عَلَيْهِمْ ءَايَٰتِنَا وَمَا كُنَّا مُهْلِكِى ٱلْقُرَىٰٓ إِلَّا وَأَهْلُهَا ظَٰلِمُونَ

> **28:59 — English**
> For your Lord never annihilates any community without sending a messenger in the midst thereof, to recite our revelations to them. We never annihilate any community, unless its people are wicked.

> **28:59 — Türkçe**
> Zira Rabbin, onun ortasında vahiylerimizi onlara okuması için bir elçi göndermeden, hiçbir topluluğu asla imha etmez. Biz halkı kötü kimseler olmadıkça hiçbir topluluğu asla imha etmeyiz.

Source: <a href="https://kuranteyit.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">kuranteyit.com</a>

### Reflection on the Verse

When I hear the word *annihilation*, I first imagine disappearance: a town without voices, homes without inhabitants, streets that no longer lead to everyday life. Then a harder question takes shape. What brings a community to such an end? As I ask it, I am not merely looking back at strangers in a distant century. I am looking at the life in which I am presently taking part. Could some of the things I now call normal become warnings in the memory of those who come after me?

Verse 28:59 places a **moral limit** before the image of destruction. It speaks of a messenger, revelations being recited, and wrongdoing among the people. Annihilation is not presented as an arbitrary display of power against an uninformed community. The verse directs attention to what people were given and what they did with it. But I cannot turn that statement into knowledge of the hidden causes behind every historical catastrophe. The divine judgment described in revelation and my judgment about a contemporary tragedy are not interchangeable.

It is easy to ask why *they* were destroyed. It is much harder to ask what I am doing with the warnings available to *me*. When I postpone a truth I have recognized, defend an injustice as custom, or remain comfortable because a whole community approves of something wrong, this passage stops being a weapon for accusing people in the past. It begins to expose my own responsibility.

The repeated insistence in 28:59 that a community is not annihilated without these conditions makes me pause. The verse is not simply about power. It refuses to sever power from justice. My own speech about other people's fate should show some awareness of that restraint.

Verse references: 28:59.

## 2. When Blessings Become Ordinary and Gratitude Disappears

### Verse

> **28:58** — Many a community we annihilated for  turning unappreciative  of  their lives. Consequently, here are their homes,  nothing  but  uninhabited ruins after them, except a few. We were the inheritors.

> **6:6** — Have they not seen how many generations before them we have annihilated? We established them on earth more than we did for you, and we showered them with blessings, generously, and we provided them with flowing streams. We then annihilated them because of their sins, and we substituted another generation in their place.

### Reflection on the Verse

One of the saddest images in 28:58 is what remains: houses with no one living in them. There were once meals, conversations, children, unfinished plans, and hopes for tomorrow. The passage ties that emptiness to communities becoming unappreciative of their lives. I begin to wonder how a human being learns to treat life itself as property that carries no responsibility toward the One who grants it.

Verse 6:6 sharpens the question. Earlier generations had resources, abundant provision, and flowing streams. Then the verse speaks of their sins and their annihilation. Prosperity, then, is not a certificate of righteousness. A society may be productive, powerful, and admired without being just. And these verses do not authorize me to accuse a person who has lost possessions of deserving that loss. Wealth is not proof of innocence; deprivation is not proof of guilt.

In my own life, ingratitude does not always announce itself dramatically. It can appear as entitlement, as forgetting another person's work, or as getting used to someone else's suffering. Do I still seek what is right when circumstances are comfortable? If I use what I have to make others feel smaller, I have misunderstood the gift.

A building may remain when its inhabitants are gone. That possibility unsettles every scale of prestige I could invent. If someone encounters the traces I leave behind, will they find a record of gratitude, justice, or merely the evidence that I once possessed things?

Verse references: 28:58; 6:6.

## 3. While People Are Righteous: Destruction and Justice

### Verse

> **11:117** — Your  Lord  never  annihilates  any community  unjustly,  while  its  people are righteous.

> **11:116** — If  only  some  of  those  among  the previous  generations  possessed enough  intelligence  to  forbid  evil! Only  a  few  of  them  deserved  to  be saved  by  us.  As  for  the  transgressors,  they  were  preoccupied  with their  material  luxuries;  they  were guilty.

### Reflection on the Verse

Read beside 28:59, verse 11:117 adds another careful boundary: a community whose people are righteous is not annihilated unjustly. Verse 11:116 then asks why so few among earlier generations had the understanding to forbid evil. Together these passages bring my attention beyond private virtue toward the moral character of life shared with others.

Forbidding evil is not a convenient slogan. It may require accurate testimony when a right is violated, refusing to celebrate dishonest gain, or not lying merely because someone powerful expects it. It also requires knowing the limits of my information and recognizing that a careless intervention can cause harm. The verses do not appoint me judge over every person. They ask me to confront the cost of becoming indifferent to wrongdoing.

I have to ask myself whether my responsibility ends with saying that I did not personally commit a particular injustice. People's knowledge, abilities, and circumstances differ, and I cannot pretend otherwise. Still, 11:116 does not treat the absence of a moral voice as insignificant. Its mention of transgressors absorbed in material luxuries is particularly uncomfortable. Which truth do I avoid because I do not want my comfort disturbed?

I do not learn from these verses to calculate another person's final fate. I learn that if I care about living in a just community, righteousness cannot be reduced to a secret preference that never shapes the way I live with others.

Verse references: 11:117; 11:116.

## 4. Can Judgment Come Without Warning?

### Verse

> **6:131** — This  is  to  show  that  your  Lord  never annihilates  any  community  unjustly, while its people are unaware.

> **17:15** — Whoever is guided, is guided for his own good, and whoever goes astray does so to his own detriment. No sinner will bear the sins of anyone else. We never punish without first sending a messenger.

> **10:44** — GOD  never  wrongs  the  people;  it  is the  people  who  wrong  their  own souls.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 6:131 connects the absence of unjust annihilation with whether people were unaware. In 17:15, responsibility is personal: no sinner bears another's sins, and punishment is not described as arriving before a messenger is sent. Verse 10:44 states that GOD does not wrong people. These are not small details in a discussion about catastrophe. They are safeguards against careless claims of certainty.

From the outside, I do not know everything another person knew, what warning reached them, or how it was understood. Declaring the guilt of a whole population, then applying a specific revealed judgment to random people living today, goes beyond what I have been told. The principle that no one bears another person's sins should make me exceptionally cautious with collective accusations.

Sometimes the desire to explain suffering turns into an excuse to speak without compassion. After seeing a report of disaster, someone may declare that the victims deserved it. But that does not reveal the meaning of their suffering. It merely places an unsupported verdict over lives the speaker does not know. If I take 28:59 and 6:131 seriously, I must resist this impulse.

This does not mean refusing to recognize wrongdoing. I can name injustice, look for evidence, protect those who have been harmed, and distinguish an aggressor from a victim. What I cannot do is claim to know the hidden divine judgment of every person caught in a catastrophe. Knowing when not to pretend that I know is part of justice.

Verse references: 6:131; 17:15; 10:44.

## 5. Every Community Has a Term: Is Perishing the Same as Punishment?

### Verse

> **17:58** — There  is  not  a  community  that  we will  not  annihilate  before  the  Day  of Resurrection,  or  inflict  severe  retribution  upon  them.  This  is  already written down in the book.

> **7:34** — For each community, there is a predetermined life span. Once their interim comes to an end, they cannot delay it by one hour, nor advance it.

### Reflection on the Verse

My first reaction to 17:58 is unease. The verse speaks of every community facing annihilation or severe retribution before the Day of Resurrection. Verse 7:34 speaks of an appointed term for each community. I must not force these statements into a single mechanism, a single cause, or a single pattern of ending. In 17:58 itself, annihilation and severe retribution are named as distinct possibilities.

Cities change, governments fall, generations pass, institutions disappear, and individuals die. It is tempting to fasten one religious explanation to every ending. Yet not every historical event comes with the particular revealed account given for an earlier community. The sweeping language of 17:58 does not turn me into someone who can identify GOD's private reason for every calamity.

There is another question here that touches me deeply: why do I assume that what I have built will remain? A family name, an organization, a place, a way of life—all can feel permanent while I am inside them. Verse 7:34 confronts that illusion of control. My task may not be to learn how to stop time, but to ask what I am doing with the time I have.

This question also meets the one I raised in <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/destiny_free_will_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">“The Great Mystery of Destiny”</a>: does an appointed term dissolve personal responsibility? Verse 7:34 speaks of the boundary of a term; it does not give me permission to excuse every choice I make within that term.

I do not know the appointed end of a place or a generation. I can, however, choose how I behave before that end: resist normalizing injustice, deal fairly with people, and refuse to treat the temporary arrangements of my world as absolute.

Verse references: 17:58; 7:34.

## 6. Why Not Immediately? The Misleading Silence of Respite

### Verse

> **18:58** — Yet,  your  Lord  is  the  Forgiver,  full of  mercy.  If  He  called  them  to  account  for  their  deeds,  He  would  annihilate  them  right  there  and  then. Instead,  He  gives  them  a  respite until  a  specific,  predetermined time; then they can never escape.

> **18:59** — Many  a  community  we  annihilated because  of  their  transgressions,  we designated  a  specific  time  for  their annihilation.

> **29:53** — They  challenge  you  to  bring  the retribution!  If  it  were  not  for  a predetermined  appointment, the retribution  would  have  come  to them  immediately.*  Certainly,  it  will come  to  them  suddenly,  when  they least expect it.

### Reflection on the Verse

What holds me in 18:58 is the relationship between delay and mercy. If people were called to account for their deeds immediately, the verse says, annihilation could come then and there. Instead, a specific time is granted. Verse 18:59 describes an appointed time for earlier communities, while 29:53 shows people challenging the messenger to bring retribution. Read together, the verses expose two mistakes: assuming I must be right because consequences have not yet appeared, and demanding that someone else's judgment arrive on my schedule.

There is a contradiction in me that I do not enjoy admitting. I hope for time after my own mistakes. Yet I may want someone who has harmed me to be given no time at all. The times described in these verses do not move according to my anger. I cannot claim to know every purpose behind a delay. I can, however, receive the time available to me as an opportunity to change rather than turn it into a wish for someone else's destruction.

In my earlier reflection <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/what_will_life_in_hell_be_like_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">“What Will Life in Hell Be Like?”</a>, I considered the weight of a condition in which changing course no longer changes the outcome. Here I am looking at the time before that point. If I can still correct a lie, restore someone's right, or leave behind a form of arrogance, why am I postponing it?

Respite is not a certificate of innocence. It does not prevent me from responding justly to harm. But if I treat the time granted to me as meaningless, I may have failed to understand the mercy described in 18:58.

Verse references: 18:58; 18:59; 29:53.

## 7. At the Edge of Mercy: Hope Without Postponement

### Verse

> **35:45** — If  GOD  punished  the  people  for their  sins,  He  would  not  leave  a single  creature  on  earth.  But  He respites  them  for  a  predetermined interim.  Once  their  interim  is  fulfilled, then GOD is Seer of His servants.

> **39:53** — Proclaim: “O My servants who exceeded the limits, never despair of GOD’s mercy. For GOD forgives all sins. He is the Forgiver, Most Merciful.”

> **39:54** — You shall obey your Lord, and submit to Him totally, before the retribution  overtakes  you,  then  you cannot be helped.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 35:45 does not let me speak of some distant guilty population as though I stand outside the problem. If human beings were punished immediately for their sins, it says, not a single creature would remain on earth. My first response should not be to count other people's failures. I, too, have been given time. Why am I so eager to question the time available to others while assuming that my own is natural?

Yet fear cannot be the end of this reflection. Verse 39:53 addresses servants who have exceeded the limits and tells them not to despair of GOD's mercy. The next verse, 39:54, calls them to submit before retribution overtakes them. One verse opens hope; the other gives urgency to that hope. If I separate them, I may either deny myself the possibility of returning or pretend that returning can be postponed forever.

It is easy to speak grandly about repentance. It is harder to call someone I have hurt, correct a lie that benefited me, stop taking what is not mine, or apologize to a person I humiliated. When I speak of GOD's mercy, am I allowing it to change my behavior? Or am I using the promise of mercy to protect habits I do not yet wish to surrender?

Fear of destruction is understandable. But if I direct that fear into an examination of my own way of life rather than a wish for other people to be ruined, the verses have become a moral summons. Hope should not delay truth. Hope should give me reason to begin.

Verse references: 35:45; 39:53; 39:54.

## 8. Hardship and Prosperity Can Both Become Tests

### Verse

> **7:94** — Whenever  we  sent  a  prophet  to  any community,  we  afflicted  its  people with  adversity  and  hardship,  that they may implore.

> **7:95** — Then  we  substituted  peace  and prosperity  in  place  of  that  hardship. But  alas,  they  turned  heedless  and said,  “It  was  our  parents  who  experienced  that  hardship  before prosperity.”  Consequently,  we  punished  them  suddenly  when  they  least expected.

> **7:96** — Had  the  people  of  those  communities  believed  and  turned  righteous,  we  would  have  showered  them with  blessings  from  the  heaven  and the  earth.  Since  they  decided  to  disbelieve,  we  punished  them  for  what they earned.

### Reflection on the Verse

Reading 7:94–96, I notice the sequence of changing circumstances. There is hardship, then peace and prosperity, then a form of heedlessness in which the earlier experience loses its capacity to teach. The issue is not merely that suffering occurred. It is how the people interpreted what happened and what they did once comfort returned.

I recognize something similar in myself. During difficult days, I think I have learned the value of health, justice, friendship, and provision. When the difficulty passes, I can drift back into habits that contradict what I said I had learned. Circumstances may improve while the inner person remains unchanged. Prosperity can be a blessing, yet it can also become the excuse I use to stop examining my life.

Verse 7:96 relates faith and righteousness to blessings. That does not give me a mechanical formula in which every wealthy person is approved and every poor person condemned. The passage speaks about communities and the consequences of their responses. It does not disclose the hidden reason for every individual condition I encounter.

I can contemplate these verses without accusing those who suffer. How do we treat one another during hardship? Do we keep our promises when comfort returns? Have we learned anything that changes our conduct? Those are questions I can honestly answer. Claiming to have solved the divine meaning of another person's disaster is not.

Verse references: 7:94; 7:95; 7:96.

## 9. Does Suffering a Disaster Make Someone Guilty?

### Verse

> **30:41** — Disasters  have  spread  throughout the land and sea, because of what the people have committed. He thus lets them taste the consequences of some of their works, that they may return (to the right works).

> **42:30** — Anything  bad  that  happens  to  you  is a  consequence  of  your  own  deeds, and  He  overlooks  many  (of  your sins).

> **8:25** — Beware of a retribution that may not be limited to the evildoers among you.* You should know that GOD’s retribution is severe.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 30:41 links widespread disasters to human actions. Verse 42:30 speaks of the relationship between misfortune and deeds while also saying that many sins are overlooked. Verse 8:25 warns of a retribution that may not remain confined to evildoers. Taken together, these statements make one simplistic claim untenable: that everyone caught in a disaster must be equally guilty for the same reason.

A community's choices can harm people who did not make those choices. Decisions, lies, exploitation, and neglected responsibilities have consequences. But the visible result does not usually tell me each person's share of responsibility. The harm suffered by a victim is not proof against that victim. It is a reason to consider what help, fairness, and care require of me. The warning that consequences may not remain neatly confined should enlarge my conscience, not diminish it.

There is a very practical test here. Looking at families who have lost homes or loved ones, will my first words be a verdict about what they supposedly deserved? Or will I ask what truthful information, help, and justice demand? Revelation supplies specific divine accounts of certain communities. It does not place an equivalent explanation in my hands for every destruction I see today.

A reflection on annihilation loses its center the moment it makes other people's pain into a spectacle. If the subject does not make me more careful, compassionate, and just, what has been gained by repeating the word *disaster*?

Verse references: 30:41; 42:30; 8:25.

## 10. When Corruption Becomes Normal, What Do Leaders Do?

### Verse

> **17:16** — If we are to annihilate any community, we let the leaders commit vast corruption therein. Once they deserve retribution, we annihilate it completely.

> **11:116** — If  only  some  of  those  among  the previous  generations  possessed enough  intelligence  to  forbid  evil! Only  a  few  of  them  deserved  to  be saved  by  us.  As  for  the  transgressors,  they  were  preoccupied  with their  material  luxuries;  they  were guilty.

> **5:8** — O  you  who  believe,  you  shall  be  absolutely  equitable,  and  observe GOD,  when  you  serve  as  witnesses. Do  not  be  provoked  by  your  conflicts  with  some  people  into  committing  injustice.  You  shall  be  absolutely  equitable,  for  it  is  more  righteous. You  shall  observe  GOD.  GOD  is fully  Cognizant  of  everything  you do.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 17:16 connects a community's destruction with vast corruption committed by its leaders. That should prevent me from reducing social ruin to a collection of isolated private failures. Those who exercise power, make decisions, and shape other people's daily conditions bear responsibilities that extend beyond themselves. The wrong choice of a leader may become an entire system of wrong choices imposed on others.

Verse 11:116 asks why so few people resisted evil. Verse 5:8 then establishes an indispensable limit: hostility toward others must not provoke injustice. I cannot oppose corruption by treating my anger as a license to become corrupt. Nor does accepting a powerful person's words make them true. Sometimes the hardest part of speaking truthfully is recognizing what it may cost.

I find a smaller version of this test in my own circumstances. I may not govern a city, but I can influence a family, a workplace, or a circle of friends. If someone trusts my judgment and I manipulate that trust, my wrongdoing is not merely a private mistake. I have misused a measure of responsibility entrusted to me.

This gives the contemplation of destruction an urgent direction. Rather than spending my energy predicting the next catastrophe, I can look at where injustice begins and where it might still be stopped. I do not need to claim the authority of the final Judge to take an ordinary, demanding step toward fairness.

Verse references: 17:16; 11:116; 5:8.

## 11. Aad: Does Being Powerful Mean Being Safe?

### Verse

> **41:15** — As for ‘Ãd, they turned arrogant on earth, opposed the truth, and said, “Who is more powerful than we?” Did they not realize that GOD, who created them, is more powerful than they? They were unappreciative of our revelations.

> **69:6** — As for ‘Aad, they were annihilated by a persistent, violent storm.

> **69:7** — He  unleashed  it  upon  them  for seven  nights  and  eight  days,  violently.  You  could  see  the  people  tossed around like decayed palm trunks.

> **69:8** — Can you find any trace of them?

### Reflection on the Verse

In 41:15, Aad asks a revealing question: who could be more powerful than they were? The arrogance comes before the destruction. I hear in it more than confidence in physical strength. It is the dangerous assumption that strength itself determines truth. When people have power, it becomes easier to imagine that their success confirms the justice of everything they do. The verse recalls the power of the One who created them.

Verses 69:6–8 describe Aad's end through a violent storm lasting seven nights and eight days. The question about whether any trace of them remains becomes an unsettling image of the fragility of human power. But a particular revealed account of a past community is not a license to identify every storm today as punishment for its victims. Similar weather does not give me identical divine knowledge.

Power can make a community louder while making its self-criticism weaker. A large audience, abundant resources, and the ability to intimidate others do not make anyone independent of GOD. I see smaller versions of this temptation in myself. When I achieve something difficult, earn money, or become someone others listen to, I may begin to mistake competence for moral authority.

The lasting lesson I want from Aad is not paralysis through fear. It is a sober scale for strength. Power may be a resource I have been given. It is not permission to claim ownership of truth or to dismiss those I can overpower.

Verse references: 41:15; 69:6; 69:7; 69:8.

## 12. Thamoud: Violating a Warning That Was Heard

### Verse

> **91:11** — Thamoud’s  disbelief  caused  them  to transgress. 

> **91:12** — They  followed  the  worst  among them. 

> **91:13** — GOD’s  messenger  said  to  them, “This  is  GOD’s  camel;  let  her drink.” 

> **91:14** — They  disbelieved  him  and  slaughtered  her.  Their  Lord  then  requited them  for  their  sin  and  annihilated them. 

> **91:15** — Yet,  those  who  came  after  them remain heedless. 

### Reflection on the Verse

The account of Thamoud in 91:11–15 is brief but carefully ordered: disbelief, transgression, following the worst among them, a warning through the messenger, and the slaughter of the camel. Then comes annihilation. The verses do not attempt to give an exhaustive social history. They show an act performed after an explicit warning. If I hurry past that sequence, I risk concentrating on the ending while overlooking the responsibility that preceded it.

What strikes me is not only that a warning was violated but that people followed the worst among them. I may not have initiated a harmful action. Yet supporting it, cheering for it, or remaining silent out of convenience can still raise questions about my own responsibility. Not every observer necessarily carries identical guilt; the passage does not give me such a chart. But 'I did not do it myself' can become a way of refusing to examine whom I choose to follow.

It costs very little to condemn people who lived long ago. The harder test is what I do with a wrong I can see now. What if speaking truthfully threatens my income, my friendships, or the approval of someone I admire? At such a moment, a story from the past stops being safely distant.

The closing warning about heedlessness matters, too. Knowing the story is not proof that I have learned from it. Memorizing its conclusion and living differently because of it are two separate things. That distance cannot be crossed by commentary alone.

Verse references: 91:11; 91:12; 91:13; 91:14; 91:15.

## 13. Noah: A Long Warning and the Illusion of an Easy Escape

### Verse

> **29:14** — We sent Noah to his people, and he stayed  with  them  one  thousand years, less fifty.* Subsequently, they incurred the flood because of their transgressions.

> **29:15** — We saved him and those who accompanied him in the ark, and we set it up as a lesson for all the people.

> **11:42** — As it sailed with them in waves like hills, Noah called his son, who was isolated: “O my son, come ride with us; do not be with the disbelievers.”

> **11:43** — He said, “I will take refuge on top of a hill, to protect me from the water.” He said, “Nothing can protect anyone today from GOD’s judgment; only those worthy of His mercy (will be saved).” The waves separated them, and he was among those who drowned.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verses 29:14–15 speak of Noah living among his people for a very long time, followed by the flood and the rescue of those with him on the ship. The account does not begin with a sudden spectacle. It includes an extended period in which a warning and a response mattered. If I see only rising water, I remove the final scene from the years that preceded it.

In 11:42–43, Noah calls to his son. That moment moves me for another reason. Love and closeness are real, yet they do not function as a guarantee of rescue in this scene. The son imagines a mountain will protect him, and the waves separate them. The contrast between confidence in a familiar refuge and the judgment described in the verse is painful. But I must not use the scene to declare all practical precautions wrong. The very account of the ship also includes acting on a task that was given.

In my own life I sometimes confuse wanting someone I love to change with possessing the right to decide for them. I can speak truthfully, help, accompany, and care. I cannot claim to hold all of another person's choices, the full knowledge of GOD about them, or the final outcome of their life.

I do not want to read the flood only as a picture of destruction. It is also an account of rescue. The necessary question is not simply who perished, but how people responded to warning and what the narrative says about mercy in the middle of judgment.

Verse references: 29:14; 29:15; 11:42; 11:43.

## 14. Pharaoh: Oppression, Power, and Words Spoken at the End

### Verse

> **28:4** — Pharaoh  turned  into  a  tyrant  on earth,  and  discriminated  against some  people.  He  persecuted  a  helpless  group  of  them,  slaughtering their  sons,  while  sparing  their daughters. He was indeed wicked.

> **28:40** — Consequently,  we  punished  him and  his  troops,  by  throwing  them into  the  sea.  Note  the  consequences for the transgressors.

> **10:90** — We delivered the Children of Israel across  the  sea.  Pharaoh  and  his troops pursued them, aggressively and  sinfully.  When  drowning  became a reality for him, he said, “I believe that there is no god except the One in whom the Children of Israel have believed; I am a submitter.”

> **10:91** — “Too late!* For you have rebelled already, and chose to be a transgressor.

> **10:92** — “Today, we will preserve your body, to set you up as a lesson for future  generations.”*  Unfortunately, many people are totally oblivious to our signs.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 28:4 does not leave Pharaoh's wrongdoing vague. It describes discrimination, oppression of a vulnerable group, and the killing of sons. A reflection on annihilation cannot begin and end with imagining the fate of those who died. It must also consider how power behaves when it imagines it has the right to determine whose life is protected and whose can be destroyed. The oppression in this passage is not merely a disagreement over ideas. It is violence against people.

Verse 28:40 describes Pharaoh and his troops being punished in the sea. Verses 10:90–92 record his declaration as drowning becomes unavoidable, followed by the answer given to him. I read this alongside my own tendency to postpone moral change. Speaking differently once the consequences arrive does not erase the need to ask why I defended an injustice while I still had the power to stop it.

I should not transform this particular revealed account into an automatic verdict about every modern political event. The verses name a figure and describe his conduct. My responsibility is not to label people casually as 'Pharaoh.' It is to take the ethical questions seriously: What does power do to the vulnerable? What does discrimination require me to confront? When have I put my own safety or reputation above someone else's right?

Power can end suddenly. The suffering caused by its misuse may endure long after that. Reflection on destruction should therefore awaken concern not only about an eventual ending, but also about the oppression that could still be resisted before an ending arrives.

Verse references: 28:4; 28:40; 10:90; 10:91; 10:92.

## 15. Qaroon: Wealth, Cleverness, and a Confidence That Collapses

### Verse

> **28:76** — Qãroon (the slave driver) was one of Moses’ people who betrayed them and oppressed them. We gave him so  many  treasures  that  the  keys thereof were almost too heavy for the strongest band. His people said to him, “Do not be so arrogant; GOD does not love those who are arrogant.

> **28:78** — He said, “I attained all this because of my own cleverness.” Did he not realize that GOD had annihilated before him  generations  that were much stronger than he, and greater in number? The (annihilated) transgressors were not asked about their crimes.

> **28:81** — We then caused the earth to swallow him and his mansion. No army could  have  helped  him  against GOD; he was not destined to be a winner.

> **28:82** — Those who were envious of him the day before said, “Now we realize that GOD is the One who provides for  whomever  He  chooses  from among His servants, and withholds. If it were not for GOD’s grace towards us, He could have caused the earth to swallow us too. We now realize  that  the  disbelievers  never succeed.”

### Reflection on the Verse

In 28:78, Qaroon attributes his wealth to his own cleverness. The verse asks whether he had failed to notice that earlier generations stronger and more numerous than himself had been annihilated. Achievement, it seems, is not immunity. The size of a fortune, the admiration it generates, and the confidence of its owner do not by themselves tell us whether it was gained or used justly.

Verse 28:81 describes the earth swallowing him and his mansion. What holds me almost as strongly is the next verse: people who envied him the day before reconsider what they had admired. What do I measure when I call someone's life enviable? The building? The money? The audience? That reversal exposes how easily I confuse visible wealth with an unseen verdict of success.

This is not permission to accuse every wealthy person. Qaroon is the subject of a specific account, and its wider passage also speaks about betrayal and oppression. I cannot use his ending to declare that every misfortune suffered by a prosperous person is divine punishment. Neither wealth nor poverty gives me full access to someone's moral condition.

The danger I recognize in myself is quieter: once I begin to treat my achievements as evidence of personal superiority, I may stop asking how I use them. Qaroon's fate places a question mark over every achievement that refuses examination. The things I own may be temporary; my responsibility for what I do with them cannot be made to disappear by changing the subject.

Verse references: 28:76; 28:78; 28:81; 28:82.

## 16. Do Not Overlook Those Who Were Saved

### Verse

> **10:103** — We  ultimately  save  our  messengers and  those  who  believe.  It  is  our  immutable  law  that  we  save  the  believers.

> **21:9** — We  fulfilled  our  promise  to  them; we saved them together with whomever  we  willed,  and  annihilated  the transgressors.

> **12:110** — Just when the messengers despair, and  think  that  they  had been  rejected, our victory comes to them. We then save whomever we choose, while our retribution for the guilty people is unavoidable. 

### Reflection on the Verse

It is easy for the imagination to become absorbed by destruction. Yet 10:103 speaks of messengers and believers being saved. Verse 21:9 describes the fulfillment of a promise, the rescue of some, and the annihilation of transgressors. In 12:110, help arrives when messengers are close to despair. If I omit these passages, I create a distorted impression that the Quran speaks only of eradication.

Neither can I turn rescue into a personal guarantee of a trouble-free life. The precise way and timing of deliverance are not described as one identical earthly experience for every believer. Claiming that a righteous person must never suffer, or that help must always arrive in the form I expect, would add details not established by these verses.

What matters is that the passages do not portray destruction as the whole story. There is warning, human response, judgment, mercy, and rescue. A book that speaks seriously about accountability also speaks seriously about hope. I miss something essential if I choose to hear only the frightening word.

For my own reflection, this creates a demanding simplicity. While examining how earlier people perished, I should not forget to ask what I am doing with the possibilities open to me now. I can seek righteousness, practice mercy, trust GOD, and refuse injustice without claiming to know every mystery of final deliverance.

Verse references: 10:103; 21:9; 12:110.

## 17. Everyone on Earth Perishes: Mortality Is Not a Verdict of Guilt

### Verse

> **55:26** — Everyone on earth perishes.

> **55:27** — Only  the  presence  of  your  Lord lasts.  Possessor  of  Majesty  and Honor.

> **28:88** — You shall not worship beside GOD any other god. There is no other god beside Him. Everything perishes except His presence. To Him belongs all sovereignty, and to Him you will be returned.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 55:26 opens a different door: everyone on earth perishes. This is no longer simply an account of a wicked community being annihilated. It is a statement about the mortality of earthly life. Verse 55:27 says that the presence of the Lord alone lasts. Verse 28:88 likewise speaks of everything perishing except GOD's presence, and of all sovereignty belonging to Him. The language of perishing therefore cannot be reduced to an accusation of wrongdoing. It also exposes the limits of created existence.

If every human life ends, the fact that someone dies cannot automatically prove personal guilt. Collapsing the difference between the punitive annihilation of a particular community and ordinary human mortality would turn a universal condition into an unjust allegation. The breadth of 55:26 does not erase the specific accounts in other verses, but it prevents me from forcing all endings into one cause.

The monotheism of 28:88 is particularly strong. If all sovereignty belongs to GOD, I must not assign absolute status to my institutions, powers, achievements, or even my carefully constructed identity. That my loved ones and I are mortal does not make love worthless. It reveals the value of the time in which love can be expressed. Why postpone a needed word while it can still be said?

Perhaps the most personal meaning of this reflection appears here: I will not remain. My work, my name, my home, and the sound of my voice may outlast my body in some form. But mortality does not make life empty. It makes me ask what direction I give this finite opportunity: toward the endless claims of my ego, or toward truthfulness and submission to GOD?

Verse references: 55:26; 55:27; 28:88.

## 18. Does Annihilation Mean That Death Ends Everything?

### Verse

> **35:36** — As  for  those  who  disbelieve,  they have  incurred  the  fire  of  Hell,  where they  are  never  finished  by  death,  nor is  the  retribution  ever  commuted  for them.  We  thus  requite  the  unappreciative.

> **20:74** — Anyone  who  comes  to  his  Lord guilty  will  incur  Hell,  wherein  he never dies, nor stays alive.

> **18:49** — The record will be shown, and you will see the guilty fearful of its contents. They will say, “Woe to us. How come this book leaves nothing, small or large, without counting it?” They will find everything they had done brought forth. Your Lord is never unjust towards anyone.

### Reflection on the Verse

The words *annihilation*, *death*, *perishing*, and *punishment in the Hereafter* are often spoken as though they describe the same event. These verses resist that simplification. Verse 35:36 says that death does not finish those in Hell and that their retribution is not reduced. Verse 20:74 speaks of the guilty person who neither dies nor stays alive there. Verse 18:49 presents the record of human actions. The annihilation of an earthly community is therefore not described as the erasure of all moral accountability.

There are details I do not know. I cannot claim to explain the physics of the Hereafter, the experience of time there, or the exact mechanism of bodily existence. But 'they died, so the account is gone' would disregard the scene in which people encounter the record of what they did.

In my earlier reflection <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/what_will_life_in_hell_be_like_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">“What Will Life in Hell Be Like?”</a>, I considered death no longer being a way out. Here I place that subject within a larger distinction: the ending of earthly life does not necessarily erase the moral significance of everything that occurred within it. A city may become uninhabited. The actions of its former inhabitants still matter in the Quran's account of judgment.

This is frightening, but it is also a summons to take truthfulness seriously. If a wrong I can temporarily conceal will not thereby become morally nonexistent, I should not build my life on concealment. Likewise, an act of kindness that goes unseen by other people need not be regarded as empty or lost.

Verse references: 35:36; 20:74; 18:49.

## 19. Arabic Roots: Are Annihilation, Perishing, Punishment, and Rescue the Same?

### Verse

> **28:58** — Many a community we annihilated for  turning unappreciative  of  their lives. Consequently, here are their homes,  nothing  but  uninhabited ruins after them, except a few. We were the inheritors.

> **28:59** — For your Lord never annihilates any community without sending a messenger in the midst thereof, to recite our revelations to them. We never annihilate any community, unless its people are wicked.

> **25:36** — We  said,  “Go,  both  of  you,  to  the people  who  rejected  our  revelations,”  and  subsequently,  we  utterly annihilated the rejectors.

> **55:26** — Everyone on earth perishes.

> **28:88** — You shall not worship beside GOD any other god. There is no other god beside Him. Everything perishes except His presence. To Him belongs all sovereignty, and to Him you will be returned.

> **17:58** — There  is  not  a  community  that  we will  not  annihilate  before  the  Day  of Resurrection,  or  inflict  severe  retribution  upon  them.  This  is  already written down in the book.

> **29:15** — We saved him and those who accompanied him in the ark, and we set it up as a lesson for all the people.

### Reflection on the Verse

In English, I may use *annihilate*, *destroy*, *perish*, and *pass away* as though they were a single term. The Arabic of these verses shows why that would be careless. Several distinct roots appear, and even one root can be used in sentences with different scopes. The task is not to win a dictionary argument. It is to hear what each sentence actually says before extracting a doctrine from a familiar word.

In 28:58, **أَهْلَكْنَا** (*ahlaknā*), in 28:59 **مُهْلِكَ** (*muhlik(a)*), and in 28:88 **هَالِكٌ** (*hālikun*) belong to the **ه-ل-ك (h-l-k)** root family. Yet one context speaks of annihilated communities, another of the conditions under which a community is not annihilated, and the third of everything perishing except GOD's presence. Sharing a root does not make punitive destruction and universal mortality identical moral events.

Verse 25:36 contains **فَدَمَّرْنَاهُمْ تَدْمِيرًا** (*fa-dammarnāhum tadmīrā*), from **د-م-ر (d-m-r)**, in an account of a people who rejected revelations. Verse 55:26 uses **فَانٍ** (*fānin*), from **ف-ن-ي (f-n-y)**, for the perishing of everyone on earth. The difference in the roots makes me read the specific judgment of a community separately from the shared transience of earthly life.

In 17:58, **مُعَذِّبُوهَا** (*muʿadhdhibūhā*) and **عَذَابًا** (*ʿadhāban*) derive from **ع-ذ-ب (ʿ-dh-b)**, the family associated here with retribution. Their appearance beside the expression for annihilation matters: the verse itself does not use the same word for both alternatives. In 29:15, **فَأَنجَيْنَٰهُ** (*fa-anjaynāhu*) belongs to **ن-ج-و (n-j-w)** and expresses rescue. The grammar of the narratives carries more than one outcome.

Verse 28:58 also uses **بَطِرَتْ** (*baṭirat*), from **ب-ط-ر (b-ṭ-r)**, in speaking of the communities becoming unappreciative of their lives. In 28:59, **ظَٰلِمُونَ** (*ẓālimūn*) belongs to **ظ-ل-م (ẓ-l-m)**, the root family of wrongdoing. In 28:88, **وَجْهَهُۥ** (*wajhahu*) appears in the Authorized English Translation as “His presence”; I would not turn that phrase into a physical description of a human-like face. Roots can clarify relationships between words, but context determines how far an interpretation may go.

My method therefore needs to remain simple and disciplined: hear the full verse, inspect its language, and refuse to let a root replace the sentence. The textual reference here is **Rashad Khalifa's Authorized English Translation** and, for Turkish passages, **the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation**. Word study helps only when it makes my conclusions more careful rather than more absolute.

Verse references: 28:58; 28:59; 25:36; 55:26; 28:88; 17:58; 29:15.

## Other Claims

Some approaches attempt to explain the destruction of earlier communities wholly through natural events. Other readings search for contemporary equivalents of every storm, flood, or upheaval mentioned in scripture and then claim to identify the same divine punishment today. Historical or scientific inquiry and the moral account presented in a verse can address different questions. But the verses do not grant me the authority to declare the victims of a present catastrophe individually guilty. The name of an ancient community should not become a label carelessly attached to people living now.

Another traditional assumption is that the worldly annihilation of a community automatically tells us the identical final fate of every individual associated with it. Other readings strongly distinguish social consequences from personal accountability. I do not want to force 17:15, 18:49, 6:131, and 8:25 into an overly simple formula. Participation in a shared calamity does not establish identical intentions or responsibilities. The Quran also speaks of rescue and individual accounting.

Some interpretations turn 55:26 and 28:88 into an extensive theory of the physical erasure of the universe. Others try to read their language as merely symbolic. Each position may require assumptions beyond what these particular verses explicitly state. What the passages clearly place before me is the contrast between created transience and GOD's enduring presence, and the statement that sovereignty belongs to Him.

Historical discussions may also offer different locations, timelines, and proposed archaeological identifications for communities mentioned in the Quran. Those claims can be investigated on their own merits. The present contemplation does not rely on an uncertain site identification, an archaeological hypothesis, or a claim that someone knows the secret purpose of a modern disaster. The moral distinctions in the verses are sufficiently serious without such additions.

## Conclusion

I began with a severe question: why does GOD annihilate a community? Reading 28:59 alongside 6:131 and 11:117, I cannot ignore warning, awareness, wrongdoing, and justice. Reading 18:58–59 and 35:45, I cannot ignore the time granted before an ending. These verses do not invite me to pass immediate sentences over other people. They confront me with responsibility for the time in which I am still able to act.

Aad's strength, Thamoud's warning, Noah's long encounter with his people, Pharaoh's oppression, and Qaroon's wealth should not become a catalog of catastrophes. Each brings a different human failure into view: arrogance, heedlessness, following wrongdoing, abusing authority, and treating material success as proof of personal superiority. These are not merely the failures of people in the past. They are dangers I need to look for in my own life.

And yet I am not authorized to place a definitive accusation over someone who suffers disaster. Verse 17:15 emphasizes that no one carries another's sins. Verse 8:25 warns that a retribution may not be limited to evildoers. Held together, these verses should make me more attentive to victims, not more eager to judge them. I need justice and compassion more than I need the illusion of knowing a verdict withheld from me.

Verses 55:26–27 and 28:88 take the reflection to its widest boundary: I, too, am mortal. Nothing I possess is everlasting. Awareness of that transience could lead to despair, or it could make the time still available to me more precious. Verses 39:53–54 suggest the latter direction: never despair of GOD's mercy, but do not postpone returning to Him. The passages about rescue remind me that destruction is not the only word spoken.

Verse 13:11 connects the condition of a people with their own willingness to change. Verse 41:46 recalls the personal consequences of good and evil action, while 4:135 demands justice even when it goes against those closest to me.

The houses of an annihilated community may remain when the inhabitants are gone. Its power, wealth, and noise may no longer mean anything. For me the more demanding measure is not what others have left behind, but **which injustice I resisted, which truth I honored, how I responded to what I was given, and which wrong I was willing to leave behind while I could**.

One day, I, too, will leave this world. I do not know how that day will arrive, and I do not know the final fate of others. What I can do now is speak truthfully, act justly, refuse to cooperate with wrongdoing, and take seriously the opportunity still available to me. The question *Annihilation?* finally returns me to the life I am living: **a life I must answer for by practicing what is right, not by pretending to predict GOD's judgment.**
