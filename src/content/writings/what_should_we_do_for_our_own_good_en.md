# [41:46] What Should We Do for Our Own Good? The Quiet Reckoning of What We Do for Our Souls

**Author:** Berk KÖKSAL  
**Date:** November 18, 2026  
**Place:** Mersin

Peace be upon you.

I am not writing this to tell somebody else how to live. I am putting a question in front of myself: **What should I do for my own good today?** I can cling to things I believe will make life better while overlooking the harm I may be doing to my own soul. Achievement, recognition, winning an argument, seeing a plan succeed—none of these is automatically evil. Yet none of them, by itself, tells me that I have lived rightly.

I can spend considerable care shaping a piece of work, a sentence, or an idea. Do I give the same care to my conduct? When I see a mistake, do I correct it? When I help someone, do I preserve their dignity? Can I value being truthful before GOD above appearing admirable before people? I do not want to answer every question with an easy yes.

Verse 41:46 directs attention to the good and harm that return to the person who acts. I want to consider it without turning self-examination into either self-condemnation or self-praise. Seeking **my own good** might mean refusing to forget GOD, the rights of other people, or the actual character of what I do.

## 1. Who Benefits First When I Do Good?

### Verse

> **41:46**
>
> **العربية:** مَّنْ عَمِلَ صَٰلِحًا فَلِنَفْسِهِۦ وَمَنْ أَسَآءَ فَعَلَيْهَا وَمَا رَبُّكَ بِظَلَّٰمٍ لِّلْعَبِيدِ
>
> **English:** Whoever  works  righteousness,  does so  for  his  own  good,  and  whoever works  evil  does  so  to  his  own  detriment.  Your  Lord  is  never  unjust towards the people.
>
> **Türkçe:** Kim doğruluğa çalışırsa, bunu kendi iyiliği için yapar ve kim kötülüğe çalışırsa, bunu kendi zararına yapar. Senin Rabbin insanlara karşı asla adaletsiz değildir.

Source: <a href="https://kuranteyit.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">kuranteyit.com</a>

### Reflection on the Verse

When I ask what I should do for my own good, I notice how easily I look for the answer in other people's behavior. If someone understood me better, if circumstances improved, if a hope were fulfilled, perhaps I would feel better. Some of those things truly matter. Yet 41:46 brings me back to a responsibility I cannot hand over: *What am I doing?*

The verse has two directions. Working righteousness is for one's own good; working evil is to one's own detriment. I cannot take the first half as comfort and silently leave the second behind. And the statement about GOD's justice matters just as much. This is not an arbitrary exchange in which I can demand immediate worldly success in return for every good deed.

So the question becomes more uncomfortable. Do I want to do good because of what I expect to receive, or because I want to live truthfully before GOD? Those motives may be mixed, and I do not want to pretend otherwise. Perhaps the first act of kindness toward my own soul is to examine that mixture without congratulating myself for noticing it.

Verse references: 41:46.

## 2. Is Everything I Desire Actually Good for Me?

### Verse

> **41:49** — The  human  being  never  tires  of  imploring for good things. And when adversity  befalls  him,  he  turns  despondent, desperate.

> **17:4** — We  addressed  the  Children of Israel in the scripture: “You will commit gross evil on earth, twice. You are destined to fall into great heights of arrogance.

> **17:7** — “If you work righteousness, you work righteousness for your own good, but if you commit evil you do so to your own detriment. Thus, when the second time comes to pass, they will defeat you and enter the masjid, just as they did the first time. They will wipe out all the gains you had accomplished.”

> **17:8** — Your Lord showers you with His mercy. But if you revert to transgression, we will counter with retribution.  We  have  designated Gehenna as a final abode for the disbelievers.

### Reflection on the Verse

I can grow attached to the thing I want before I have understood what it means. A quick solution. A door that opens. Recognition for work that has taken time. Verse 41:49 speaks of the human being who never tires of asking for good things and then falls into despair when hardship arrives. Wanting something is not the same as knowing it is truly good.

In 17:7, righteousness is said to benefit the one who practices it, and evil to harm the one who practices it. Yet the passage also speaks of defeat, a sanctuary, and the destruction of what had been gained; 17:8 continues the warning about returning to transgression. Verse 17:4 explicitly places the account in an address to the Children of Israel. These verses belong to that specific account. I must not erase that setting and manufacture a personal guarantee that every righteous act will bring a visible reward by tomorrow.

This distinction may protect me from one of my own traps: confusing faithfulness with the success of my plans. I can hope for something, work for it, and still refuse to make that outcome the measure of whether it was worthwhile to do what is right. Otherwise I may discover that I was devoted less to righteousness than to the result I wanted.

Verse references: 41:49; 17:4; 17:7; 17:8.

## 3. The Effort I Cannot Delegate

### Verse

> **29:6** — Those who strive, strive for their own good. GOD is in no need of anyone.

> **53:39** — Every human being is responsible for his own works.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 29:6 says that those who strive do so for their own good, and that GOD needs no one. That second sentence is deeply humbling. When a person does something useful, the sense of being indispensable can quietly grow. But the one who needs to learn and to be transformed is me, not GOD.

Verse 53:39 draws my attention to responsibility for my own actions. I may receive guidance, friendship, criticism, and practical help. Those can all matter. None of them allows me to outsource my decisions or place the responsibility for my life entirely in another person's hands.

Even when I finish a piece of work, the completed thing does not prove that my motives were pure. I want to examine the work beneath the result. Did the effort make me more attentive, more honest, less willing to take shortcuts at another person's expense? If I never ask this, I may begin to treat activity itself as righteousness. Effort matters. But the direction of effort matters too.

Verse references: 29:6; 53:39.

## 4. Where Does Caring for My Soul Begin?

### Verse

> **91:7** — The soul and Him who created it. 

> **91:8** — Then showed it what is evil and what is good.

> **91:9** — Successful is one who redeems it. 

> **91:10** — Failing is one who neglects it. 

### Reflection on the Verse

Verses 91:7–10 speak of the soul, of the knowledge of right and wrong given to it, and of success or failure in relation to what a person does with it. They do not hand me a scale for ranking other people's souls. They turn the question inward.

Caring for myself, then, cannot simply mean approving every feeling or desire. Sometimes what is good for me may be precisely what I resist: acknowledging envy, checking pride, or refusing to turn anger into a principle. These are my own questions before the verses, not new rulings I can impose on others.

There is a part of this work that attracts no audience. No one sees the moment I stop making excuses in my own mind. No one applauds a temptation that I refuse to feed. I cannot claim to know that every such moment is accepted by GOD. I can only take the warning seriously: neglecting my soul while maintaining an admirable exterior is not the same as taking care of it.

Verse references: 91:7; 91:8; 91:9; 91:10.

## 5. Advising Everyone Except Myself

### Verse

> **2:44** — Do  you  exhort  the  people  to  be righteous,  while  forgetting  yourselves,  though  you  read  the  scripture? Do you not understand?

> **18:104** — “They  are  the  ones  whose  works  in this  life  are  totally  astray,  but  they think that they are doing good.”

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 2:44 asks a question that is difficult to keep directed at somebody else. How can I encourage others to be righteous and forget myself while reading scripture? It is possible to write accurately, speak persuasively, or explain a principle and still fail to live by it.

Verse 18:104 speaks of those whose efforts have gone astray while they believe they are doing good. I do not want to flatten that verse's particular context into a verdict against every imperfect person. Yet it reminds me that sincere confidence and actual correctness are not identical.

This touches a question I explored in [*The Most Argumentative Creature: The Difference Between Seeking Truth and Needing to Be Right*](https://berkkoksal.com/writings/the_most_argumentative_creature_en/). Am I looking for truth, or for arguments that protect my self-image? If I used some of the energy spent defending myself to correct one real mistake, would that not be a more meaningful form of self-respect?

Verse references: 2:44; 18:104.

## 6. Stopping Before I Believe a Claim

### Verse

> **17:36** — You  shall  not  accept  any  information,  unless  you  verify  it  for  yourself. I  have  given  you  the  hearing,  the eyesight,  and  the  brain,  and  you  are responsible for using them.

> **39:18** — They  are  the  ones  who  examine  all words,  then  follow  the  best.  These are  the  ones  whom  GOD  has  guided;  these  are  the  ones  who  possess intelligence.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 17:36 places responsibility upon hearing, sight, and the mind. Information does not become verified because it gives me comfort. If a claim happens to confirm what I already want to believe, that may be a reason to examine it more carefully, not less.

Verse 39:18 describes those who examine words and then follow what is best. There is both attentiveness and a decision here. I need not pretend I know everything; neither should I use uncertainty as an excuse never to act on what I have understood.

I return to this question in connection with my earlier reflection [*Is Comparing Quran Translations Doubt or Responsibility?*](https://berkkoksal.com/writings/is-comparing-quran-translations-doubt-or-responsibility/). The point is not to collect more and more material until truth disappears beneath it. It is to remain answerable for what I accept and repeat. A careful pause can itself be an act of kindness toward my future self—and toward anyone who may trust my words.

Verse references: 17:36; 39:18.

## 7. What Do I Do When No One Is Watching?

### Verse

> **35:18** — No soul can carry the sins of another soul. If a soul that is loaded with sins implores another to bear part of its load, no other soul can carry any part of it, even if they were related. The only people to heed your warnings are those who reverence their Lord, even when alone in their privacy, and observe the Contact Prayers (Salat). Whoever purifies his soul, does so for his own good. To GOD is the final destiny.

> **53:32** — They avoid gross sins and transgressions,  except  for  minor  offenses. Your Lord’s forgiveness is immense. He has been fully aware of you since He initiated you from the earth, and while you were embryos in your mothers’ bellies. Therefore, do not exalt yourselves; He is fully aware of the righteous.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 35:18 speaks of people who reverence their Lord even in private, while also stating that no soul can carry another's burden. It unsettles the comfortable distinction between my public image and my private choices. If my kindness requires witnesses, how sturdy is it?

Verse 53:32 warns against exalting ourselves and reminds us that GOD knows who is righteous. My self-examination must not become another occasion for self-praise. Saying “I am sincere” is not evidence that I am. I may even turn my awareness of my faults into a reason to feel superior to people who do not speak about theirs.

There are decisions made beyond the reach of applause: an error admitted quietly, a wrong not repeated, a kindness that cannot be displayed. I am not tallying those things to issue myself a certificate of purity. I am trying to remember that being responsible before GOD cannot depend on whether another human being has noticed me.

Verse references: 35:18; 53:32.

## 8. Returning Instead of Defending My Mistake

### Verse

> **7:23** — They  said,  “Our  Lord,  we  have wronged  our  souls,  and  unless  You forgive  us  and  have  mercy  on  us,  we will be losers.”

> **4:110** — Anyone  who  commits  evil,  or wrongs  his  soul,  then  implores  GOD for  forgiveness,  will  find  GOD  Forgiving, Most Merciful.

### Reflection on the Verse

The words of 7:23 do not blame someone else. They admit wrongdoing against one's own soul and ask for mercy. I find that directness difficult and beautiful. I can explain my mistakes in great detail—fatigue, pressure, the behavior of other people. Some circumstances may be relevant, but they cannot be allowed to turn a wrong into a right.

Verse 4:110 speaks of a person who has done wrong and then asks GOD for forgiveness. The possibility of forgiveness does not make the wrongdoing trivial. Nor does admitting a mistake automatically repair its consequences. Where I have harmed someone or neglected a duty, I must take seriously the need to change what can still be changed.

What concerns me most is the habit of defending a wrong until it feels reasonable. Once that habit settles in, repentance becomes harder to imagine. Perhaps doing good to myself sometimes begins with an unadorned confession: I have wronged my soul. I do not need to invent a grand spiritual identity before I can turn back.

Verse references: 7:23; 4:110.

## 9. Not Mistaking Despair for the Truth

### Verse

> **39:53** — Proclaim: “O My servants who exceeded the limits, never despair of GOD’s mercy. For GOD forgives all sins. He is the Forgiver, Most Merciful.”

> **20:82** — I  am  surely  Forgiving  for  those  who repent,  believe,  lead  a  righteous  life, and steadfastly remain guided.

### Reflection on the Verse

A person may tire of confronting the same weakness. It is easy to turn disappointment into a final verdict: “I will never change.” Verse 39:53 tells us not to despair of GOD's mercy. I do not read this as permission to minimize wrongdoing. Verse 20:82 joins repentance with faith, righteous living, and remaining steadfastly guided.

Despair can present itself as realism, as though I had enough knowledge to close every door to the future. I do not. The verses direct me away from issuing the final sentence upon myself and back toward GOD.

This does not make regret painless. Nor is hope a substitute for responsibility. The hope I find here is demanding: it invites me to acknowledge the harm, seek forgiveness, and alter my conduct. Doing good to myself means neither excusing everything nor declaring myself beyond mercy.

Verse references: 39:53; 20:82.

## 10. At the Edge of My Strength: Steadfastness and Prayer

### Verse

> **2:153** — O  you  who  believe,  seek  help through  steadfastness  and  the  Contact  Prayers  (Salat).  GOD  is  with those who steadfastly persevere.

> **2:286** — GOD never burdens a soul beyond its means: to its credit is what it earns, and against it is what it commits. “Our Lord, do not condemn us if we forget or make mistakes. Our Lord, and protect us from blaspheming against You, like those before us have done. Our Lord, protect us from sinning until it becomes too late for us to repent. Pardon us and forgive us. You are our Lord and Master. Grant us victory over the disbelieving people.”

### Reflection on the Verse

Some days knowing what is right does not make it easy to do. Verse 2:153 calls believers to seek help through steadfastness and the Contact Prayers. It does not require me to look strong at every moment. Asking for help belongs inside a life of submission.

Verse 2:286 speaks of a burden within a soul's capacity and continues with a prayer about mistakes, forgetting, forgiveness, and protection. The prayer itself reminds me that human limits are not something to conceal. At the same time, I do not know the full weight of another person's suffering. I would not want to wield this verse against someone by telling them that their pain must be easy.

At times I may need to slow down, continue with what I can actually do, and bring what I cannot carry to GOD. Prayer is not the abandonment of effort. It may be the place where effort and the acknowledgment of my limitations finally meet.

Verse references: 2:153; 2:286.

## 11. Gratitude Is Also a Responsibility to Myself

### Verse

> **31:12** — We  have  endowed  Luqmãn  with wisdom:  “You  shall  be  appreciative of  GOD.”  Whoever  is  appreciative is  appreciative  for  his  own  good.  As for  those  who  turn  unappreciative, GOD is in no need, Praiseworthy.

> **2:152** — You  shall  remember  Me,  that  I  may remember  you,  and  be  thankful  to Me; do not be unappreciative.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 31:12 explicitly says that the grateful person is grateful for their own good; GOD needs nothing. Gratitude cannot be a service that supplies something GOD lacks. It changes the direction in which I look at what I have been given.

Verse 2:152 brings remembrance and gratitude together. If remembrance is not limited to desperate moments, it must also find a place when life is going well. I would rather let a blessing lead to responsibility than turn it into proof that I have become better than somebody else.

What can I do with what I have received? A skill, a relationship, an opportunity, even the ability to begin again—each invites that question. Gratitude does not require me to pretend that every experience has been pleasant. It asks me not to claim every good thing as though I were its ultimate source.

Verse references: 31:12; 2:152.

## 12. Refusing to Lie in My Own Favor

### Verse

> **4:135** — O  you who  believe,  you  shall  be absolutely  equitable,  and  observe GOD,  when  you  serve  as  witnesses, even  against  yourselves,  or  your parents,  or  your  relatives.  Whether the  accused  is  rich  or  poor,  GOD takes  care  of  both.  Therefore,  do not  be  biased  by  your  personal wishes.  If  you  deviate  or  disregard (this  commandment),  then  GOD  is fully  Cognizant  of  everything  you do.

> **5:8** — O  you  who  believe,  you  shall  be  absolutely  equitable,  and  observe GOD,  when  you  serve  as  witnesses. Do  not  be  provoked  by  your  conflicts  with  some  people  into  committing  injustice.  You  shall  be  absolutely  equitable,  for  it  is  more  righteous. You  shall  observe  GOD.  GOD  is fully  Cognizant  of  everything  you do.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 4:135 becomes especially difficult when justice speaks against me. It demands fairness in testimony even against ourselves, our parents, or relatives. Remaining silent about what I know is wrong may bring temporary relief. It is not necessarily an act of kindness toward my own soul.

Verse 5:8 places another limit on my excuses: conflict with people must not tempt me into injustice. Being hurt does not give me the right to distort their actions. I can defend what is mine without corrupting what is true.

I do not want to confuse winning a dispute with becoming more righteous. If I know I was mistaken, can I say so without surrounding the admission with another defense? If another person's right interrupts my preferred narrative, can I let it interrupt me? A truthful account may wound my pride for a moment and still be far better for me than a comfortable lie.

Verse references: 4:135; 5:8.

## 13. Can Giving Be Good for the Giver Too?

### Verse

> **2:272** — You are not responsible for guiding anyone. GOD is the only one who guides whoever chooses (to be guided). Any charity you give is for your own good. Any charity you give shall be for the sake of GOD. Any charity you  give  will  be  repaid  to  you, without the least injustice.

> **64:16** — Therefore,  you  shall  reverence GOD as much as you can, and listen, and  obey,  and  give  (to  charity)  for your  own  good.  Anyone  who  is protected  from  his  own  stinginess, these are the successful ones.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 2:272 says that what we give in charity is for our own good. Verse 64:16 connects giving for our own benefit with reverencing GOD according to our ability and being protected from our own stinginess. I hear a warning against treating the person receiving help as inferior. The giver is not simply a benefactor; the giver also stands in need of what generosity may teach.

Giving can appear as subtraction: less money, less time, less ease. The verses do not deny that practical cost. They ask me to consider a wider measure. The insistence that charity be for GOD also calls my motives into question. Am I helping someone, or purchasing their admiration and loyalty?

After offering help, do I keep the recipient in debt to me? Does my goodwill turn bitter when nobody thanks me? These are not questions for the person I help to answer. They are questions for me. A form of giving that diminishes another's dignity cannot be the whole meaning of doing good to myself.

Verse references: 2:272; 64:16.

## 14. Turning Good Intentions Into Actual Deeds

### Verse

> **2:177** — Righteousness  is  not  turning  your faces  towards  the  east  or  the  west. Righteous  are  those  who  believe  in GOD,  the  Last  Day,  the  angels,  the scripture,  and  the  prophets;  and they  give  the  money,  cheerfully, to  the  relatives,  the  orphans,  the needy,  the  traveling  alien,  the  beggars,  and  to  free  the  slaves;  and  they observe  the  Contact  Prayers  (Salat) and  give  the  obligatory  charity (Zakat);  and  they  keep  their  word whenever  they  make  a  promise;  and they steadfastly persevere in the face  of  persecution,  hardship,  and war.  These  are  the  truthful;  these are the righteous.

> **90:14** — Feeding,  during  the  time  of  hardship. 

> **90:15** — Orphans who are related.

> **90:16** — Or the poor who is in need.

> **90:17** — And being one of those who believe, and  exhorting  one  another  to  be steadfast,  and  exhorting  one  another to be kind. 

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 2:177 refuses to reduce righteousness to an outward direction. It joins faith with generosity, keeping promises, the Contact Prayers, and steadfastness in hardship. The verse leaves me little room for saying that an inward claim of goodness makes the practical obligations of life irrelevant.

Verses 90:14–17 describe feeding people in hard times, caring for orphans and people in need, and encouraging one another toward patience and mercy. The people in those verses are not abstractions. Hunger, need, and isolation are lived realities. A reflection that never moves beyond reflection could leave them untouched.

So I need a question more concrete than “Did I think correctly today?” Whose burden did I lighten? Which promise did I keep? What did I actually give? People do not all possess the same resources or capacity. Yet this difference should deepen my care, not turn righteousness into a beautiful idea with no one on the receiving end.

Verse references: 2:177; 90:14; 90:15; 90:16; 90:17.

## 15. What My Anger and My Forgiveness Cost

### Verse

> **3:134** — who  give  to  charity  during  the  good times, as well as the bad times. They are  suppressors  of  anger,  and  pardoners of the people. GOD loves the charitable.

> **24:22** — Those among you who are blessed with resources and wealth shall be charitable  towards  their  relatives, the poor, and those who have immigrated for the sake of GOD. They shall treat them with kindness and tolerance; do you not love to attain GOD’s forgiveness? GOD is Forgiver, Most Merciful.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 3:134 connects giving in ease and hardship with restraining anger and forgiving people. It does not say that anger never arises. It asks what happens when anger does arise: how much authority will I allow it to have over my next word and action?

Verse 24:22 speaks of kindness and tolerance, then asks whether we do not love to attain GOD's forgiveness. I cannot ask that forgiveness be available to me while treating mercy toward others as an alien idea. Still, I do not think these verses authorize the conclusion that a person must discard every boundary or treat injustice as acceptable. Forgiveness and the denial of a wrong are not the same.

Perhaps one good thing I can do for myself is leave certain arguments without demanding the feeling of victory. Not because the hurt never mattered, but because I do not want to let it govern me indefinitely. This is not a balance I can claim to have perfected. It is one I need to keep examining.

Verse references: 3:134; 24:22.

## 16. How Am I Treating the People Closest to Me?

### Verse

> **17:23** — Your  Lord  has  decreed  that  you shall not worship except Him, and your parents shall be honored. As long as one or both of them live, you shall never say to them, “Uff” (the slightest gesture of annoyance), nor shall you shout at them; you shall treat them amicably.

> **17:24** — And  lower  for  them  the  wings  of humility,  and  kindness,  and  say, “My  Lord,  have  mercy  on  them,  for they have raised me from infancy.”

### Reflection on the Verse

Verses 17:23–24 describe respect for parents in strikingly ordinary terms: not even an impatient expression, no harsh shouting, kind words, humility, and a prayer for mercy. The scale is small enough to enter an ordinary conversation.

I may become absorbed in distant goals and forget that my tone today also belongs to my moral life. In close relationships it is tempting to say, “They know what I mean,” and stop examining the words I actually use. But the verse is attentive to words and behavior as well as intention.

This does not make every family situation simple, nor does it give me knowledge of another family's difficulties. It gives me a clear direction for the conduct that is mine to choose. Doing good to myself may sometimes begin by lowering my voice, delaying a harsh reply, or expressing gratitude while there is still time.

Verse references: 17:23; 17:24.

## 17. Not Despising a Small Good Deed

### Verse

> **99:7** — Whoever does an atom’s weight of good will see it. 

> **99:8** — And whoever does an atom’s  weight of evil will see it.

> **4:40** — GOD does not inflict an atom’s weight of injustice. On the contrary, He multiplies the reward manifold for the righteous work, and grants from Him a great recompense.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verses 99:7–8 say that even an atom's weight of good or evil will be seen. Their brevity unsettles the way my mind keeps track of life. I tend to remember spectacular failures and achievements. The small decision disappears from my memory. Not from the horizon described by these verses.

Verse 4:40 says that GOD does not commit even the smallest injustice and multiplies the recompense for a righteous deed. I cannot turn that into an exact calculation of reward or presume to know how any specific action will be judged. But I can refuse to call a small kindness meaningless because no one noticed it.

An honest correction, a word withheld before it can wound, attention to a quiet need—these may be modest beginnings. I do not want to turn them into a spiritual scorecard. I want to stop waiting for a dramatic transformation before I perform the good that is already within reach.

Verse references: 99:7; 99:8; 4:40.

## 18. What Can I Change Today for Tomorrow?

### Verse

> **59:18** — O  you  who  believe,  you  shall  reverence  GOD,  and  let  every  soul  examine  what  it  has  sent  ahead  for tomorrow.  You  shall  reverence GOD;  GOD  is  fully  Cognizant  of everything you do.

> **18:110** — Say,  “I  am  no  more  than  a  human like  you,  being  inspired  that  your god is  one god.  Those who hope to meet  their  Lord  shall  work  righteousness,  and  never  worship  any other god beside his Lord.”

> **45:15** — Whoever  works  righteousness  does so  for  his  own  good,  and  whoever works  evil  does  so  to  his  own  detriment.  To  your  Lord  you  will  be returned.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 59:18 asks every soul to look at what it has sent ahead for tomorrow. There is a danger in living as if nothing lasts beyond today, but there is another danger in imagining that tomorrow is entirely under my control. This verse gives me something more modest and demanding: examine what I am sending forward now.

Verse 18:110 connects the hope of meeting one's Lord with righteous work and refusing to worship any other god alongside Him. That is why seeking my own good must not become a refined form of self-worship. I do not want to turn GOD into a tool for securing my plans. I want my plans to become answerable to GOD.

Verse 45:15 brings me back to the opening: righteousness benefits the one who practices it, evil harms the one who practices it, and we return to our Lord. I would rather end without promising myself a magnificent new identity. One smaller question is enough: *Did I live even one truth that I already knew today?* If I did not, the work before me is clear.

Verse references: 59:18; 18:110; 45:15.

## 19. Arabic Roots: The Language of Doing Good to Oneself

### Verse

> **41:46** — Whoever  works  righteousness,  does so  for  his  own  good,  and  whoever works  evil  does  so  to  his  own  detriment.  Your  Lord  is  never  unjust towards the people.

> **29:6** — Those who strive, strive for their own good. GOD is in no need of anyone.

> **91:9** — Successful is one who redeems it. 

> **31:12** — We  have  endowed  Luqmãn  with wisdom:  “You  shall  be  appreciative of  GOD.”  Whoever  is  appreciative is  appreciative  for  his  own  good.  As for  those  who  turn  unappreciative, GOD is in no need, Praiseworthy.

> **64:16** — Therefore,  you  shall  reverence GOD as much as you can, and listen, and  obey,  and  give  (to  charity)  for your  own  good.  Anyone  who  is protected  from  his  own  stinginess, these are the successful ones.

### Reflection on the Verse

In 41:46, **عَمِلَ** (*ʿamila*) is connected to the root **ʿ-m-l (ع-م-ل)**, the language of doing and acting. The word **صَٰلِحًا** (*ṣāliḥan*) belongs to the root **ṣ-l-ḥ (ص-ل-ح)**, associated with what is sound or righteous. The phrase **لِنَفْسِهِۦ** (*li-nafsihi*) includes **n-f-s (ن-ف-س)** and makes the relation to one's own self explicit. These are not merely feelings being praised; the verse is speaking about the character of actual work.

Verse 29:6 uses **جَٰهَدَ** (*jāhada*) and **يُجَٰهِدُ** (*yujāhidu*), from **j-h-d (ج-ه-د)**, in the context of striving. I am not collapsing every possible use of this root into a single English definition; here the verse expressly says that the striving is for the one who strives. In 91:9, **زَكَّىٰهَا** (*zakkāhā*) is associated with **z-k-w (ز-ك-و)** and the purification of the soul.

In 31:12, **يَشْكُرْ** (*yashkur*) belongs to **sh-k-r (ش-ك-ر)**, the language of gratitude. In 64:16, **أَنفِقُوا۟** (*anfiqū*) is connected to **n-f-q (ن-ف-ق)**, the act of spending or giving. Taken together within their verses, the words bring effort, action, purification, gratitude, and generosity into the discussion of one's own good. A root, however, cannot by itself settle the meaning of a whole verse. I return to the sentence and its context rather than asking the dictionary to become a religious authority.

Verse references: 41:46; 29:6; 91:9; 31:12; 64:16.

## Other Claims

Some readings of “one's own good” focus on immediate worldly success: do the right thing and expect a visible reward at once. Yet 41:46 and 45:15 do not assign a date, amount, or worldly form to every outcome. And the specific setting of 17:7–8 should not be converted into a universal schedule of guaranteed prosperity.

Another approach may treat the phrase as permission to put self-interest above other people's needs. But 2:272, 64:16, 2:177, and 90:14–17 place generosity and mercy within the same moral account. This does not establish a promise that every gift will produce a material gain for the giver.

It is also possible to treat the purification of the soul as something that can be definitively measured by outward religious appearance. The respect shown to GOD in private in 35:18 and the warning against exalting oneself in 53:32 make me cautious about that kind of certainty. These are possible approaches to examine, not conclusions I can declare on the authority of the verses.

## Conclusion

What should I do for my own good? I do not have a magnificent one-line answer. Before 41:46, I can see what belongs to me: work righteousness. Before 29:6, I cannot delegate my effort. Before 91:9–10, I cannot neglect my soul. Before 7:23 and 39:53, I must not confuse admitting wrongdoing with shutting the door on mercy.

None of this appoints me the judge of other people. Verse 4:135 demands justice even against my own interest; 2:272 asks me to consider what giving does to my soul; 17:23–24 brings the inquiry into my closest relationships. And 59:18 asks what I have sent ahead for tomorrow.

Today I can speak more truthfully. Apologize for something. Stop repeating a claim I have not examined. Notice someone in need. Refuse to claim every good thing as my own achievement. None of these actions gives me authority to guarantee my own place in Paradise. The verses do not put that judgment in my hands. But I need not wait for a grand public moment to begin turning toward what they ask of me.

**Perhaps the most important thing I can do for my own good is stop trying to prove that I am right and begin working to do what is right.** I do not possess the full knowledge of the outcome. I remain responsible for my choice today.
