# [77:30] Shade and Shadows in the Quran: Refuge, Failed Refuge, Prostration, and 19 Reflections

*Not everything called shade offers shelter; meaning emerges from the place in which a word is used.*

**Author:** Berk KÖKSAL  
**Date:** November 26, 2026  
**Place:** Mersin

## Introduction

**Greetings. Peace be upon you.**

When I step into the shade on a hot day, I think first of coolness. The shadow of a wall, the space beneath a tree, or the brief cover of a cloud all speak to me through the same word. Reading the passages about shade in the Quran side by side has unsettled that habit. In one place shade belongs to comfort and provision; in another it cannot protect. Another verse speaks of shade that is itself hot. Elsewhere shadows appear within creation's submission to GOD.

The contrast within Sura 77, *Dispatched (Al-Mursalaat)*, first brought the question into focus. Verses **77:30–31** describe a shade of three different densities that offers no relief. Verse **77:41** speaks of the righteous enjoying shade and springs. The word is familiar in both places, but the reality described is not the same. I cannot responsibly interpret the image without reading its neighbors.

Other passages complicate and enrich the question. Verses 25:45–46 draw attention to moving shade; 16:81 presents shade as an everyday gift; 13:15 and 16:48 speak about shadows and submission; 28:24 shows a tired man withdrawing to the shade after helping others. Verse 26:189 names the Day of the Canopy in a different narrative. Treating all these passages as though they depicted one scene would erase the distinctions the text makes. The first discipline of this inquiry is to hear each occurrence in its own context before drawing connections.

Shade also brings me to the human desire for safety. I want relief from pain, a secure place for people I love, and some continuity in a changing life. But a place called safe is not necessarily safe. Something that benefits me may harm someone else. I can take an ordinary blessing for granted until another person has none of it. These are personal questions prompted by the verses, not social judgments secretly embedded in each use of the word.

I am not claiming to possess every concealed meaning of shade. I want to understand what the sentences explicitly say, where their contexts meet and differ, and where an honest reader must stop short of certainty. Writing about shadows should not darken the text itself. The point, for me, is not to produce an impressive metaphor. It is to become a more careful reader and a more responsible human being.

## 1. A Shade of Three Densities: Does Every Shade Protect?

### Verse

> **77:30**
>
> **Arabic:** <span lang="ar" dir="rtl">ٱنطَلِقُوٓا۟ إِلَىٰ ظِلٍّۢ ذِى ثَلَـٰثِ شُعَبٍۢ</span>
>
> **English — Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation:** Go to a shade of three different densities.
>
> **Turkish — the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation:** Üç farklı yoğunluktaki bir gölgeye gidin.

Source: <a href="https://kuranteyit.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">kuranteyit.com</a>

> **77:29** — Vaktiyle inkâr ettiğiniz şeye gidin.
> **77:31** — Yine de o ne serinlik sağlar, ne de sıcaktan koruma.

### Reflection on the Verse

One word can quietly shape my expectation before I have even finished reading the sentence: shade. Under the summer sun, I associate shade with relief, rest, and a measure of safety. That is why 77:30 initially sounds almost reassuring. Yet 77:31 immediately removes that reassurance. The thing called shade offers neither coolness nor protection from heat. Its name tells me what it resembles, not what it accomplishes.

The sequence matters. Verse 77:29 speaks of facing what was previously denied. Verse 77:30 names the shade to which the hearers are directed, and 77:31 tells us what this shade cannot do. Reading the middle sentence without its neighbors would turn an unsettling passage into a false promise of refuge. The Authorized English Translation speaks of three different densities, but these verses do not supply a physical analysis of each density. I may ask what they are; I cannot claim the text has already told me their composition or exact structure.

This passage also leaves me with an uncomfortable question about ordinary life. I sometimes call a person, an institution, or an idea a safe place before asking whether it actually deserves my trust. That application is my reflection, not a hidden social rule contained in 77:30. The verse concerns a future scene of judgment, and I do not want to replace it with a convenient metaphor. Still, its stark contrast between a familiar name and a failed function challenges my readiness to be reassured by appearances. **What am I calling protection without examining whether it truly protects?**

## 2. Sparks Beneath the Shade: When Refuge Is Reversed

### Verse

> **77:32** — O, köşkler kadar büyük kıvılcımlar saçar.
> **77:33** — Develerin rengi kadar sarıdır.
> **77:34** — O gün, reddedicilerin vay hâline.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 77:32 describes enormous sparks, and 77:33 adds a comparison of color. Then 77:34 returns to the warning addressed to the rejectors. The preceding sentence had already told me that this was no ordinary refuge from the sun. Now the whole picture resists the idea of safety. Shade and sparks appear together, almost undoing the expectation that made the word shade comforting in the first place.

I need to be careful with the descriptions. Size and color are part of the verses. A chemical formula for Hell, a measurable temperature, or a complete diagram of the three densities is not. Adding such details to make the scene more vivid would not strengthen the revelation; it would substitute my imagination for what is actually said. A serious reading must be capable of leaving some questions unanswered. Curiosity is not the problem. Pretending that a guess has become certainty is.

The repeated warning in 77:34 prevents me from treating these images as an exotic spectacle. Sura 77 is concerned with the Day of Decision and with human denial. The real force of this passage does not depend on my being able to reconstruct the scenery. It asks me to take seriously the possibility that a denied reality may eventually be encountered. I can let that warning challenge my complacency without claiming to know the final condition of the people around me. The verse is grave enough without my borrowing its authority to judge them.

## 3. Another Shade in the Same Sura: The Righteous at Rest

### Verse

> **77:41** — Doğrular gölgenin ve nehirlerin keyfini çıkaracaklar.
> **77:42** — Ve arzuladıkları meyvelerin.
> **77:43** — İşlerinizin karşılığında mutlulukla yiyin ve için.
> **77:44** — Biz erdemlileri işte böyle ödüllendiririz.

### Reflection on the Verse

At 77:41 the very same image opens onto a radically different scene. The righteous enjoy shade and springs. Fruits appear in 77:42; 77:43 relates happiness to their works, and 77:44 speaks of rewarding the virtuous. A few verses earlier there was shade without relief. Here there is shade together with water, abundance, and welcome. The contrast belongs to the sura itself. I do not have to import a story to create it.

It would be too narrow to turn the difference into a question of temperature alone. The words about works in 77:43 give the scene an ethical dimension. The promised shade is presented in a context of righteousness and its reward. Yet this does not authorize me to calculate another person's final standing. The text speaks about the righteous and the virtuous; it does not give me complete access to anyone's conscience or their ultimate destination.

I am left considering the life that I am living now. What am I making with the abilities entrusted to me? What harm am I willing to acknowledge? Can I examine my actions without using someone else's apparent failure to congratulate myself? The shade and springs of 77:41–44 offer more than a pleasing landscape. They place a vision of rest alongside the seriousness of what human beings do. My questions about daily conduct are a personal response to that relationship. They are not a new law hidden inside the vocabulary of shade.

## 4. A Shade of Happiness: Faith Joined to Righteous Conduct

### Verse

> **4:57** — İnanan ve doğru bir hayat sürenlere gelince, biz onları akan nehirler bulunan bahçelere kabul edeceğiz; orada sonsuza dek kalırlar. Orada tertemiz eşleri olacaktır. Onları saadet dolu bir gölgeye kabul edeceğiz.

### Reflection on the Verse

The terms in 4:57 belong together: belief, a righteous life, gardens, rivers, and a shade filled with happiness. The shade is part of a larger promise, not an isolated object. I cannot turn the verse into a guarantee that a believer will always enjoy physical ease in this world. It describes a future reception and reward. That boundary matters, especially when I am tempted to treat comfort as a reliable measurement of spiritual success.

The pairing of belief and righteous living brings my speech and behavior into the same frame. I can easily say that I believe in GOD. But what happens if, during that very day, I break a promise, dismiss another person's labor, or speak with certainty about something I have not investigated? The verse does not list my examples. I bring them to it because the reference to a righteous life makes a merely verbal profession feel incomplete.

There is another difference I need to preserve: comfort is not identical with goodness. A fortunate circumstance might allow me to ignore the people I hurt. The happy shade of 4:57 is not described simply as a luxury that anyone can purchase. It belongs to a promise tied to belief and conduct. I can receive that promise as an invitation to live carefully now without assigning myself an assured place in its final scene. I also need not despise ordinary material blessings; I only need to stop treating them as sufficient evidence that my life has been just.

## 5. Cool Shade and Unending Provision: Respecting the Allegory

### Verse

> **13:35** — Doğrular için söz verilen Cennetin alegorisi akan nehirler, tükenmez rızıklar ve serin gölgedir. İşte doğruluğu gözetenler için varış noktası böyledir, oysa inkârcılar için varış noktası Cehennemdir.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 13:35 makes an especially important distinction by calling its picture of Paradise an allegory. Rivers, provision that does not run out, and cool shade are presented as elements of the promised destination for the righteous. Because the verse identifies an allegorical description, I should not assume that I can translate every image into the measurements of an earthly physics experiment. I know the relief of shade and the need for water. I do not therefore know the complete nature of the promised life.

Calling the passage an allegory does not make it empty or arbitrary. The images are specific. Water, provision, and coolness have recognizable value. A person exhausted by summer heat might hear this promise differently from someone who has never lacked shelter. Such human responses can deepen attention, but they do not license unlimited additions to what is written. I must distinguish between the qualities named by the verse and the landscape I supply from my imagination.

The same verse contrasts different destinations. I do not want to translate that contrast into a license to label living strangers with final verdicts. I do not know the entirety of their struggles, choices, or last moment. Studying a promise should increase my seriousness without eroding justice toward the people before me. If cool shade is part of the reward described in 13:35, humility about my knowledge is part of the responsibility I carry while trying to understand it.

## 6. Shade Shared With Others: Rest Is More Than an Object

### Verse

> **36:55** — Cennet sakinleri, o gün, mutlu bir biçimde meşgul olacaklar.
> **36:56** — Rahat mobilyaların keyfini çıkararak eşleriyle beraber güzel bir gölgede kalırlar.
> **36:57** — Orada meyvelere sahip olacaklar; diledikleri her şeye sahip olacaklar.
> **36:58** — En Merhametli bir Rabden esenlik selamları.

### Reflection on the Verse

The picture in 36:56 is not of a solitary figure occupying a patch of shade. Companions are present, and the verse speaks of rest together. Verse 36:55 sets a mood of happiness; 36:57 adds fruit and desires fulfilled; 36:58 brings a greeting of peace from a merciful Lord. Shade is one feature in a scene whose significance is larger than temperature or furniture.

I inevitably bring my own experiences of longing and human closeness to this passage. Someone who has lived through separation, loneliness, or a difficult relationship may hear the idea of resting together very differently from someone surrounded by companionship. But I cannot derive a full map of every afterlife relationship from 36:56. It describes a particular scene of shared rest. To claim detailed knowledge beyond that would be to extend the verse with my expectations.

The greeting of peace in 36:58 arrests me most. The image is not exhausted by desirable objects. There is also a relationship with the One from whom that greeting comes. That complicates my habit of identifying happiness with possessions or flawless surroundings. Returning to my own day, I ask whether people feel safe and respected in my presence. Do my words bring them peace, or do I purchase my own convenience at their expense? This is an ethical question inspired by the passage, not a claim that Paradise is simply an improved version of our present homes.

## 7. Extended Shade and Abundant Water: The Spaciousness of Relief

### Verse

> **56:27** — Sağ taraftakiler, sağ tarafta olacaklar.
> **56:28** — Yemyeşil meyve bahçelerinde.
> **56:29** — Hoş kokulu meyveler.
> **56:30** — Yayılmış gölge.
> **56:31** — Bol su.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 56:30 is strikingly brief: extended shade. Read alone, it could be treated as a small detail. Read with 56:27–31, it belongs to a landscape of green growth, fruit, shade, and abundant water. These elements are related in my earthly experience. Water sustains life; shade makes exertion bearable. Such familiarity helps me receive the imagery, but it does not prove that the environment described functions according to every physical condition I know here.

The word extended suggests to me a relief that is not cramped or precarious. I imagine no desperate competition for a single narrow patch of coolness. Yet the feeling evoked by a phrase is not a measurement. Nothing in 56:30 gives me a surface area, a direction, or the timetable of this shade. The verse offers a description, not an engineering drawing. A serious reader can appreciate the image without pretending that appreciation is complete knowledge.

This scene also returns my attention to ordinary gifts. I can become so accustomed to water that another person's thirst turns into an abstract report. I may rarely notice the heat endured by people working outdoors. Verses 56:27–31 do not enumerate all the practical duties of sharing resources. Nevertheless, reflecting on the value of water and shelter can affect the way I receive the blessings already present in my life. A promised abundance should not make me indifferent to scarcity around me; it can sharpen both gratitude and attention.

## 8. Neither Scorching Sun nor Bitter Cold

### Verse

> **76:11** — Sonuç olarak, TANRI onları o günün kötülüklerinden korur, üstelik onları sevinç ve gönül ferahlığı ile ödüllendirir.
> **76:12** — O onları metanetleri nedeniyle Cennetle ve ipekle ödüllendirir.
> **76:13** — Onlar orada lüks mobilyalarda dinlenirler. Ne güneşin sıcağını çekerler, ne de bir soğuğu.
> **76:14** — Orada gölge onların üzerini örter, meyveler de ulaşılabilir duruma getirilir.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verses 76:13–14 describe something broader than escape from sunlight. The people in the scene experience neither the heat of the sun nor the cold. Shade covers them, and fruit is brought within reach. Relief is presented in both directions. In this world I might escape the heat only to complain about the cold; here the contrast points to a life not defined by either discomfort.

The order of the passage deserves attention. Verses 76:11–12 speak first of protection, joy, and a reward connected with steadfastness. I do not want to rush toward the attractive scenery and overlook what precedes it. If the only thing I notice is the comfort, I miss the moral depth of the sequence. Steadfastness makes me think about how a person responds to hardship, how long a promise is kept, and what does not get abandoned merely because the circumstances become difficult. It would be careless, however, to turn this into a simplistic command for every suffering person to endure every situation without seeking help.

Even when I sit in actual shade, my mind may be restless. Worry, regret, and unfinished decisions can follow me into a comfortable place. I should not transform 76:13–14 into a psychological treatment claim; its immediate subject is a promised life beyond this one. Yet it exposes my habit of expecting a change in surroundings to settle every inner conflict. Can I work to improve the conditions of life while also learning steadiness, gratitude, and responsibility? The passage invites that question without offering easy shortcuts.

## 9. Hot Shade: When a Familiar Word Fails Its Promise

### Verse

> **56:41** — Soldakiler ise solda olacaklar.
> **56:42** — Sefalet ve inferno içinde.
> **56:43** — Onların gölgeleri bile sıcaktır.
> **56:44** — Asla serin değil, asla dayanılır değil.
> **56:45** — Onlar vaktiyle zengindiler.
> **56:46** — Büyük bir küfürde ısrar ettiler.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 56:43 says that even their shade is hot. The same familiar word that was previously linked with water and rest now appears in a painful scene. Taken together, 56:41–44 place it beside misery, inferno, and the absence of relief. The word shade has not become a guarantee of well-being simply because I know what it usually means in ordinary speech.

Verses 56:45–46 then refer to a former condition of wealth and persistence in grave disbelief. I must resist the temptation to convert this into a claim that wealth itself determines a person's final standing. The text links these people with a particular moral course; it does not give me authority to judge everybody belonging to an economic class. A comfortable life cannot prove that someone is righteous. Nor can an uncomfortable life, by itself, prove the opposite.

Everyday experience offers situations that feel like hot shade: an apparent security that breeds anxiety, a convenient arrangement built on someone else's deprivation, a role that looks impressive but conceals a growing loss of integrity. These are my analogies, not the literal content of 56:43. I do not want them to domesticate the force of the passage. Still, the explicit point remains: the existence of something called shade does not guarantee the relief I expect from it. I need to examine substance as carefully as I examine names.

## 10. Cool Shade and the Sun’s Heat: Reading a Sequence of Contrasts

### Verse

> **35:19** — Kör ile gören eşit değildir.
> **35:20** — Karanlık ve aydınlık da değildir.
> **35:21** — Gölgenin serinliği ile güneşin sıcaklığı da değildir.
> **35:22** — Yaşayan ve ölü de değildir; TANRI irade ettiği kimsenin işitmesine yol açar. Sen kabirde olanları işiten kimseler yapamazsın.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verses 35:19–22 unfold through a sequence of contrasts: the blind and the seeing, darkness and light, cool shade and the heat of the sun, the living and the dead. The shade is not introduced as a separate promise of rest. It is one element in a chain of things the passage refuses to equate. I read it as an invitation to notice meaningful differences rather than flatten everything into a reassuring sameness.

This does not entitle me to conclude that shade is always good and sunlight always bad. The sun, too, belongs to the created order. Verse 25:45 speaks of the relationship between shade and the sun, while 35:21 uses their contrast within a different argument. Context changes the function of a word. I have to listen to the surrounding sentences rather than build an absolute theological dictionary from one occurrence.

In disputes I sometimes find it convenient to erase a distinction. I confuse careful inquiry with an instant opinion, verified knowledge with an attractive guess, and honest speech with words designed merely to please. The contrasts of 35:19–22 expose that laziness in my thinking. But they do not appoint me as a judge who may divide every person I meet into light and darkness. A more responsible application begins with the distinctions in my own choices. Can I recognize what is not equivalent without pretending that I possess complete knowledge of everyone involved?

## 11. The Moving Shadow: Learning to Observe Creation Again

### Verse

> **25:45** — Rabbinin gölgeyi nasıl tasarladığını görmedin mi? Eğer O irade etseydi, onu sabit kılabilirdi; o zaman biz güneşi buna göre tasarlardık.
> **25:46** — Ancak onu yavaş hareket edecek şekilde tasarladık.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 25:45 asks me to consider how shade is designed and then mentions the sun. Verse 25:46 speaks of its gradual movement. Here shade does not appear as a reward or a failed refuge; it belongs to the order of creation. The shadow of a wall is in one place during the morning and elsewhere later in the day. I encounter such changes constantly, yet I rarely notice them unless they interfere with my plans. The verse makes observation itself worth my attention.

I cannot extract an entire system of celestial mechanics from these two sentences. They call attention to a relationship between the sun, shade, and movement; they do not provide all the measurements and mechanisms required for a scientific model. Scientific investigation has its own indispensable discipline. Respect for revelation should not require me to pass off a later calculation as a sentence written in the verse. Clear boundaries are a form of intellectual honesty, not a denial of wonder.

The moving shade also makes me conscious of time. As it changes, the day advances while I make small decisions inside a larger order I did not create. I think of unfinished work, an apology I have delayed, or a difficult passage I should read more carefully. None of these tasks is spelled out in 25:45–46. They are personal responses to its invitation to look. Perhaps reflection sometimes begins not with a secret but with recovering the capacity to notice something ordinary.

## 12. Shadows From Right and Left: A Sign of Submission

### Verse

> **16:48** — Onlar her şeyi TANRI’nın yarattığını görmediler mi? Gölgeleri TANRI’ya tam bir teslimiyet içinde ve isteyerek, onları sağdan ve soldan çevreler.
> **16:49** — Göklerdeki her şey ve yeryüzündeki her şey—her yaratık— TANRI’ya secde eder ve melekler de öyle; en ufak bir kibir olmadan.*

### Reflection on the Verse

In 16:48 the shadows of created things appear within the language of submission to GOD. Verse 16:49 widens the scene to creatures in heaven and on earth, and speaks of angels without arrogance. I find it striking that the movement of a shadow and the conscious worship of a human being can both enter the scriptural language of prostration. Yet this does not tell me that shadows have a human nervous system, make personal choices, or belong to a religious community.

Neither can I confidently declare that the verse has been fully explained by the angle of sunlight. The movement of shadows to the right and left is observable, but the verse speaks of submission as well. Reducing every word to geometry would discard an important dimension of its language. At the other extreme, inventing unseen mechanisms that the verse never identifies would claim knowledge I do not possess. Both extremes tempt me away from the text.

The mention of arrogance makes the passage personal. I can defend my opinions so fiercely that admitting a mistake begins to feel like a loss of identity. Verse 16:49 makes me reflect on humility without giving me the right to demand unthinking obedience from others. If I write about shadows bowing to GOD and then treat my interpretation as untouchable, I have learned very little. The first responsibility of my reflection is to recognize the limits of its speaker.

## 13. Shadows Prostrating Morning and Evening: A Recurring Reminder

### Verse

> **13:15** — Göklerdeki ve yeryüzündeki herkes, isteyerek ya da istemeyerek, TANRI’ya secde eder; gölgeleri de sabahları ve akşamları öyle yapar.*
> **22:18** — Göklerdeki ve yeryüzündeki herkesin, güneşin, ayın, yıldızların, dağların, ağaçların, hayvanların ve birçok insanın TANRI’ya secde ettiklerini fark etmiyor musun? İnsanlar arasından diğer birçoğu ise mahvolmaya bırakılmıştır. TANRI kimi küçük düşürürse, onu hiç kimse onurlandırmaz. Her şey TANRI’nın iradesine göredir.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 13:15 places shadows among those described as prostrating to GOD in the mornings and evenings. The references to time make the scene vividly accessible. Shadows lengthen, shift, and change direction as the day passes. No extraordinary event is needed to notice them. And yet their visible motion does not give me exhaustive knowledge of everything the verse means by prostration.

Verse 22:18 gathers the sun, moon, stars, mountains, trees, animals, and many human beings into the language of prostration. I cannot claim to know the precise manner in which every one of these prostrations occurs. A human decision to bow and the movement of a shadow need not be identical processes merely because a common word connects them. The verses broaden my attention, but they do not authorize me to construct an invisible science of worship with imagined mechanisms.

I had explored a related question in <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/you_do_not_understand_their_glorification_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">“You Do Not Understand Their Glorification.”</a> Here that question returns through the ordinary shadow. When I see it in the morning, I do not have to think only about how late I am for work. I can briefly remember that my concerns unfold within an order much larger than myself. This does not place me above others. It can instead make it more difficult to behave as though my own viewpoint were the measure of all creation.

## 14. Shade in Ordinary Life: The Value of a Created Gift

### Verse

> **16:81** — Ve TANRI yarattığı şeyler aracılığıyla size gölge ile dağlarda size sığınaklar sağladı ve sıcaktan sizi koruyan elbiseler ile savaşlarda çarpıştığınızda koruyan giysiler sağladı. O böylece üzerinizdeki nimetlerini tamamlar ki teslim olasınız.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 16:81 places shade alongside shelters in the mountains and clothing that protects people from heat. After the difficult scenes of judgment and the descriptions of Paradise, shade returns to the world I can touch. I think of stepping beneath a tree on a hot afternoon, standing on the cooler side of a rock, or using a covering against the sun. These moments are so ordinary that I rarely notice them as gifts. The verse interrupts that familiarity.

It also matters that shade is provided through created things. A benefit can arrive through concrete features of the world rather than through an event that appears extraordinary. Human labor can work with those features: designing a shelter, making protective clothing, or ensuring that someone does not have to remain in dangerous heat. The verse does not supply architectural blueprints or a detailed public policy. It does make it harder for me to dismiss such practical protection as spiritually irrelevant.

Submission appears at the end of 16:81. For me, acknowledging this gift should not mean despising human effort or forgetting those who provide care. It can mean recognizing that usefulness does not make me the absolute owner of the world. When I enjoy shelter, I can also ask who has little access to it. That is my ethical question arising from the verse, not a comprehensive social program claimed in its name. Gratitude is more serious when it changes the way I notice the needs of others.

## 15. The Shade of Clouds: Why Receiving a Gift Does Not End Responsibility

### Verse

> **2:57** — Sizi (Sina’da) bulutlarla gölgelendirdik ve size kudret helvası ve bıldırcınlar indirdik: “Size temin ettiğimiz iyi şeylerden yiyin.” Onlar (isyan ederek) bize zarar vermediler; onlar sadece kendi nefislerine zarar verdiler.
> **7:160** — Onları on iki kabile topluluğuna ayırdık ve halkı ondan su istediğinde Musa’ya ilham ettik: “Asanla o kayaya vur,” bunun üzerine oradan on iki pınar fışkırdı. Böylece her topluluk kendi suyunu bildi. Onları bulutlarla gölgeledik ve onlara kudret helvası ve bıldırcınlar indirdik: “Size sağladığımız güzel şeylerden yiyin.” Haksızlık ettikleri biz değiliz; kendi nefislerine haksızlık edenler onlardı.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 2:57 describes a people shaded by clouds and provided with food, then speaks of the harm they did to themselves. Verse 7:160 returns to the scene with sources of water, the needs of different groups, and the gifts they received. Shade here belongs to practical provision. Yet being given a gift is not the same as responding to it rightly. The closing parts of these verses keep that distinction clear.

I recognize a similar weakness in myself. When a project succeeds or I escape difficulty, I may begin to feel that every decision I made must have been correct. Relief can become self-approval. But in 2:57 and 7:160, the presence of shade and food does not mean the recipients were incapable of wrongdoing. Provision does not automatically certify character. The verses invite gratitude, but they also resist the notion that receiving help removes moral responsibility.

Nor do I want to turn a passage about a particular community into a weapon against people living today. The scene has its own context, and I cannot casually transfer all its details onto strangers. I can, however, ask myself about the relationship between gifts and conduct. Health, work, a home, a friend's support, even a temporary patch of shade—none of these makes my later actions automatically just. If something has been provided for me, I still have to decide how I will use it and how I will treat the people around me.

## 16. Moses Retreats to the Shade: Helping While Acknowledging Need

### Verse

> **28:23** — Midyan suyuna ulaştığında, orada hayvan sulayan bir insan kalabalığı buldu ve yanda iki kadının beklediğini fark etti. Dedi ki: “İhtiyacınız olan nedir?” Dediler ki: “Kalabalık dağılıncaya kadar hayvan sulayamıyoruz ve babamız yaşlı bir adam.”
> **28:24** — Onlar için hayvan suladı, ardından şunu diyerek gölgeye çekildi, “Rabbim, bana ne nimet gönderirsen gönder, ona aşırı muhtacım.”

### Reflection on the Verse

Shade appears in a remarkably human scene in 28:23–24. Moses notices two women waiting near water, learns about their difficulty, waters their animals, and then withdraws to the shade while acknowledging his need before his Lord. This is neither the reward of Paradise nor the terrifying shade of the Day of Decision. It is the place where a person who has helped others now sits in need himself.

The order is moving: someone else's difficulty is noticed; practical help follows; afterward the helper speaks of his own vulnerability. I cannot turn that sequence into a mandatory formula for every act of generosity. But it exposes a temptation in my own conduct. When I help, I sometimes want to be seen as the strong one, the capable one, the one who needs nothing. Moses in the shade reminds me that being useful to another person does not erase my limitations.

His appeal in 28:24 also makes me consider what I seek in return. Must every good act produce immediate praise, recognition, or indebtedness? A kindness can lose something of its freedom when I treat it as a purchase of influence. The verses do not present my thoughts as a formal ethical code. I am responding to the relationship between the action and the prayer. For me, the shade becomes a moment of reflection after work: **Can I serve someone without pretending that I am no longer a person who needs help?**

## 17. The Day of the Canopy: Does a Related Word Mean the Same Scene?

### Verse

> **26:176** — Orman Halkı, elçileri inkâr etti.
> **26:177** — Şuayb onlara dedi ki: “Doğru kimseler olmayacak mısınız?
> **26:178** — “Ben size gönderilen güvenilir bir elçiyim.
> **26:181** — “Ticaret yaparken tam ölçüyle verin; hile yapmayın.
> **26:182** — “Adil bir teraziyle tartın.
> **26:183** — “İnsanları aldatıp haklarından mahrum bırakmayın ve yeryüzünde bozgunculuk yaparak dolaşmayın.
> **26:188** — Dedi ki: “Yaptığınız her şeyi bilen Rabbimdir.”
> **26:189** — Onu inkâr ettiler ve sonuç olarak Gölgelik Günü’nün azabını üzerlerine çektiler. Bu, müthiş bir günün azabıydı.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 26:189 speaks of the punishment of the Day of the Canopy. The related vocabulary might tempt me to merge it with the shade of 77:30. But the contexts are distinct. Verses 26:176–189 concern the People of the Woods and the message delivered by Shu'aib. In particular, 26:181–183 address honest measures, fair weighing, and the obligation not to deprive people of what is theirs. Verse 26:189 closes that narrative with its severe outcome. I have no basis for claiming that it supplies the missing physical details of the three densities in Sura 77.

This section confronts me with an ethical question that is more immediate than speculation about a future scene. In business or in any exchange, do I demand full value when receiving while giving less than I owe? Do I protect my profit by concealing the cost imposed on another person? The instructions about measure and fairness are concrete. It is surprisingly easy to become fascinated with the dramatic name of a day while passing over the conduct criticized in the preceding verses.

I will not claim exact knowledge of the physical conditions surrounding the event in 26:189. The verse names the punishment and describes it as severe; it does not present a meteorological report. Shared vocabulary does not prove identical events. Preserving this distinction allows me to hear both passages as they stand: the warning in 77:30–31 and the concern for justice in 26:181–189. Perhaps a serious study of the canopy must remember the scales as much as the shade.

## 18. Investigating Shade Without Pretending to Know Everything

### Verse

> **3:7** — O, anlaşılması kolay—kutsal yazının özünü oluşturan—ayetleri de, çok anlamlı veya alegorik ayetleri de içeren bu kutsal yazıyı sana indirdi. Kalplerinde şüphe barındıranlar, karışıklık meydana getirmek ve belirli bir anlamı çekip çıkarmak için çok anlamlı ayetlerin peşine düşerler. Bunların doğru anlamını, TANRI ve bilgide sağlam temelli olan kimselerin dışında hiç kimse bilmez. Onlar derler ki: “Biz buna inanıyoruz—hepsi Rabbimizden gelir.” Sadece akıl sahibi olanlar dikkate alacaklardır.
> **17:36** — Kendin için teyit etmediğin sürece, hiçbir bilgiyi kabul etme. Ben sana işitmeyi, görmeyi ve beyni verdim ve sen onları kullanmaktan sorumlusun.
> **39:18** — Onlar tüm sözleri inceleyen, ardından en güzeline uyanlardır. Bunlar TANRI’nın rehberlik ettiği kimselerdir; bunlar akıl sahibi olanlardır.

### Reflection on the Verse

Placing occurrences of a word side by side can make me feel that I am moving closer to understanding. That feeling is valuable, but understanding is not the same as having a complete explanation. Verse 3:7 distinguishes clear passages from those that are multiple in meaning or allegorical, and warns about forcing a preferred meaning out of them. Verse 17:36 makes verification a responsibility; 39:18 praises careful attention to different words and following what is best. These passages make me accountable for the way I write about shade.

Verse 77:30 mentions three densities, but I do not possess their laboratory specification. Verse 13:15 speaks of the prostration of shadows, but I cannot claim to know every dimension of that language. The Day of the Canopy in 26:189 is not explicitly identified as the very same scene described in 77:30. At the same time, some distinctions are clear: 16:81 presents shade as a gift, while 77:31 denies relief to the shade in that passage. Intellectual humility is not the same as declaring every sentence unknowable.

A person can study for years and still make an error. I am not exempt from that possibility. The chance of being mistaken is not a reason to stop writing; it is a reason to keep the distance between revelation and my commentary visible. If a reader disagrees, I want to ask which verse, word, and connection they find more persuasive. Winning an argument is not the purpose of reading. Perhaps the most difficult result of studying the Quran's shadows is this: **Can I recognize the shadows cast by my own certainty?**

## 19. Which Shade Am I Seeking? Looking Beyond the Name of Refuge

### Verse

> **77:30** — Üç farklı yoğunluktaki bir gölgeye gidin.
> **77:31** — Yine de o ne serinlik sağlar, ne de sıcaktan koruma.
> **77:41** — Doğrular gölgenin ve nehirlerin keyfini çıkaracaklar.
> **16:81** — Ve TANRI yarattığı şeyler aracılığıyla size gölge ile dağlarda size sığınaklar sağladı ve sıcaktan sizi koruyan elbiseler ile savaşlarda çarpıştığınızda koruyan giysiler sağladı. O böylece üzerinizdeki nimetlerini tamamlar ki teslim olasınız.
> **4:57** — İnanan ve doğru bir hayat sürenlere gelince, biz onları akan nehirler bulunan bahçelere kabul edeceğiz; orada sonsuza dek kalırlar. Orada tertemiz eşleri olacaktır. Onları saadet dolu bir gölgeye kabul edeceğiz.

### Reflection on the Verse

I began this inquiry by following a word. It has led me through very different scenes: the practical shade of 16:81, the observable movement in 25:45–46, the shadows associated with prostration in 13:15, the promised happiness of 4:57, the shade without protection in 77:30–31, and the peace of the righteous in 77:41. The word cannot responsibly be compressed into one slogan. Its meaning has to be heard in each setting.

What I have questioned most is my own expectation. I hear shade and imagine safety. Verse 77:31 tells me that this association does not always hold. In contrast, 77:41 and 4:57 describe rest within a context of righteousness and righteous living. I do not know the entirety of anyone's final destination, including my own. But I can investigate whether I have been truthful and fair today. I can ask what my actions have brought to the people around me.

Being grateful for shade as a created gift does not mean treating it as the ultimate source of security. Shade can benefit me, but it is not the owner of final power. As I observe the world more carefully, I also want to examine the claims of safety I hear within it. Having a refuge for today's heat is not the same as holding a guarantee for eternity.

The question that remains is simple, though the answer is not: **Am I merely looking for shade, or am I learning to ask what it truly provides and what kind of life I am building beneath it?** I want to keep that question alive not to elevate my own interpretation but to make my decisions more responsible.

## Arabic Word Roots

English words such as *shade* and *shadow* may make the passages sound uniform. The Arabic vocabulary reveals connections, but it also requires distinctions. Studying a root does not grant its reader mastery over the full meaning of a passage. It gives me another way to check what I am claiming.

**ظِلّ (*ẓill*) and ظِلَال (*ẓilāl*), root ظ-ل-ل:** The family of words appears in descriptions of shade and shadows. Verse 77:30 refers to a shade, while 13:15 and 16:48 speak of shadows. Verse 77:31 denies the protective quality one normally expects. A common root does not make every occurrence function in the same way. The positive descriptions in 4:57, 13:35, and 77:41 remain different in context from 77:30–31 or 56:43.

**ٱنطَلِقُوا (*inṭaliqū*), root ط-ل-ق:** This imperative occurs in both 77:29 and 77:30. The repetition matters because the destination is further specified in the second sentence. It should not be confused with *al-mursalāt*, the name of Sura 77, which belongs to a different root. English or Turkish may use related everyday words for movement, but similarity in a translation does not make the Arabic words identical.

**شُعَب (*shuʿab*), root ش-ع-ب:** The Arabic of 77:30 places this plural word beside the number three. Its lexical range concerns divisions, branches, or separate portions. **Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation** uses the phrase “three different densities,” and **the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation** speaks of three different densities as well. It is important not to hide the linguistic question behind the translation. Knowing the root does not tell me which substances or exact physical structures these three refer to.

**مَدّ (*madd*), root م-د-د:** Terms associated with extending or spreading appear in 25:45 and 56:30. One context draws attention to the design of shade in the observable world; the other uses extended shade in a picture of reward. The relationship in vocabulary is meaningful without making the two scenes identical.

**س-ج-د (*s-j-d*), the root of prostration:** Verses 13:15 and 16:49 use language of prostration in passages concerning creation. The root helps me identify the shared theme. It does not provide a complete account of the manner in which shadows prostrate. Likewise, verses 2:57 and 7:160 describe shade provided by clouds; that does not mean every mention of shade automatically refers to a cloud.

**الظُّلَّة (*al-ẓulla*), also related to ظ-ل-ل:** The expression translated as “Day of the Canopy” in 26:189 belongs to the same broad family. Root kinship alone cannot prove that the event is identical to the shade of 77:30.

The most valuable lesson of this lexical investigation is restraint. A dictionary can illuminate a sentence, but it should not replace the sentence, its context, or the limits of my knowledge. Verse 17:36 remains relevant here: what I know, what I infer, and what I merely imagine must not be presented as the same thing.

## Other Claims

Traditional interpretations of 77:30 sometimes explain the shade as smoke rising from Hell and separating into three branches. Such a reading emphasizes the lexical range of the Arabic word for divisions or branches. The account attributed to <a href="https://quran.com/ar/77%3A30/tafsirs/ar-tafsir-al-tabari" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">al-Tabari</a>, for instance, takes this approach. Other commentaries offer more elaborate pictures of the threefold structure. Those details are not all explicitly stated in 77:30–31 as presented in the Authorized English Translation. I therefore regard them as interpretations to be considered, not as additional words that may be silently inserted into the verse.

The prostration of shadows in 13:15 and 16:48 has also received different explanations. Some emphasize the movement of shadows as an indication of creation's submission to its order; others stress the broader spiritual language of prostration. Neither approach gives me permission to pretend that its preferred mechanism has been fully specified in the verse. The language of creation in 22:18 is wider than any one physical diagram.

Verses 25:45–46 have at times been read through scientific models of sunlight and motion. Such models can be studied on their own merits, but their details should not be presented as though the verses stated them all. Some readings of the Day of the Canopy in 26:189 describe a particular cloud or covering. The brief verse itself supplies neither a complete historical reconstruction nor an exhaustive physical account. Identifying that event with 77:30 also requires more than a common root.

These perspectives can be examined respectfully and critically. None of them becomes a second revelation simply by being old or widely repeated. What matters is keeping visible the point at which the text ends and a commentator's explanation begins.

## Conclusion

I began with the assumption that shade meant relief. The verses have made that easy definition impossible to maintain. Verses 77:30–31 describe shade that does not protect; 77:41–44 place shade and springs among the rewards of the righteous; 56:43–44 speak of hot shade; 4:57 and 13:35 relate shade to happiness and comfort. The word remains recognizable while the surrounding reality changes dramatically. That difference has challenged not only my vocabulary but also my habit of drawing conclusions from a single line detached from its context.

Turning to creation opened a different field of attention. Verses 25:45–46 returned me to the changing shadow I encounter every day. Verses 16:48 and 13:15 associated shadows with submission and prostration. Verse 16:81 recalled shade as a practical gift. These are not meanings I need to set against one another. They are also not interchangeable. Observation, submission, and shelter each belong to the particular sentences that express them. Respecting that variety has taught me to read more patiently.

Moses withdrawing to the shade in 28:24 remains one of the most human scenes in this study. He helps people in difficulty and then acknowledges his own need. A person can act generously without becoming self-sufficient. Sometimes I am eager to appear capable of everything. That short passage gently exposes the limits of such an image. Helping another person does not end my dependence on GOD.

The Day of the Canopy in 26:189 has reminded me not to combine separate events merely because related words appear in both. The call to honest measure and justice in 26:181–183 should not disappear beneath my fascination with the name of a day. I may lack certainty about the physical appearance of a described event while still hearing a clear warning against depriving people of their rights. Unknown details and known responsibilities can stand beside each other.

Shade, then, is neither automatically a symbol of salvation nor always a sign of punishment. **What matters is the sentence, the surrounding passage, and the human response that the passage addresses.** When I read of shadows prostrating, I want to grow less arrogant. When I encounter shade as provision, I want to be grateful. When I read of shade that cannot protect, I want to question misleading assurances. When the promised rest of the righteous is described, I want to take righteous living seriously. These are my reflections, not new commandments I can add to the verses.

As the sun moves, my own shadow changes. I too am capable of change, error, and learning. Each time I think I have understood a passage, I need to guard against letting the shadow of my certainty fall over its words. The Quran's varied shades have taught me to consider refuge, failed refuge, movement, prostration, and promise in their proper places. My responsibility today is to listen carefully and to try to live with greater fairness, humility, and gratitude.
