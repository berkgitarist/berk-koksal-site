# Does Every Question Seek the Truth? The Ethics of Asking in the Quran

**Author:** Berk KÖKSAL  
**Date:** 04.10.2026  
**Place:** Mersin

The main purpose of this article is to reflect on the verses together with other related verses and to explore, in a sincere and profound language, what witnessing the oneness of GOD means in human life in terms of knowledge, justice, truthfulness, and submission.

Peace. Peace be upon you.

A question can open the door to knowledge, but not every question arises from the same place. One question may seek understanding; another may postpone responsibility, prolong an argument, satisfy curiosity without purpose, or avoid facing an answer already made clear. When the Quranic examples are read together, the issue is not a simple choice between asking and remaining silent.

5:101 warns against prematurely pressing for matters whose disclosure could cause harm, while 16:43 tells those who do not know to ask those who know. Several passages answer concrete questions directly. 17:85 answers a question by reminding the human being how little knowledge has been given. Questioning, then, is neither automatically virtuous nor automatically blameworthy.

The deeper issue is the ethics of inquiry: Why am I asking? What will I do with the answer? Will I verify what I hear? Can I accept the limits of my knowledge? Can the answer correct me rather than merely confirm me?

### Reflection on the Verse

5:101 does not abolish questioning. It warns against pressing for matters which, if disclosed prematurely, could harm the questioner, while the same verse says that matters asked about in the light of the Quran may become clear.

The distinction matters. Curiosity is not condemned in itself, but curiosity has timing and proportion. Sometimes a person wants not the responsibility of knowledge but the sensation of possessing an answer.

The closing description of GOD as Forgiver and Clement also restrains the impulse to fill every unaddressed space with a new religious rule. What GOD has left without an imposed burden need not be turned into one by human insistence.

**Verse references:** 5:101.

## 2. When Repeated Questions Turn Away from Truth

### Verse

> **5:102** — Others  before  you  have  asked  the same  questions,  then  became  disbelievers therein.

### Reflection on the Verse

5:102 states that others before asked the same kinds of questions and then became disbelievers therein. The verse does not expose every inner motive, so it would be wrong to label every persistent question as insincere. Yet the warning is clear: questioning can move a person away from truth rather than toward it.

A person may keep asking not because no answer exists, but because every answer is met with a new condition. Inquiry then becomes a mechanism for postponing commitment.

The verse makes me ask a harder question about my own questions: if an answer becomes clear, am I willing to live by it?

**Verse references:** 5:102.

## 3. A Good Question Opens into a Livable Answer

### Verse

> **2:189** — They ask you about the phases of the moon! Say, “They provide a timing device  for  the  people,  and  determine  the time of Hajj.”  It  is not righteous  to  beat  around  the  bush;* righteousness  is  attained  by  upholding  the  commandments  and  by being  straightforward.  You  shall  observe GOD, that you may succeed.

### Reflection on the Verse

When people ask about the phases of the moon, 2:189 directs the answer toward their human function: they provide a timing system and determine the time of Hajj. The verse then turns to straightforwardness and righteousness.

The answer does not indulge curiosity for its own sake. It connects knowledge to life, worship, and truthful conduct.

This suggests that a question grows in value when its answer helps order life more truthfully. Knowledge is not merely collected; it becomes a measure for action.

**Verse references:** 2:189.

## 4. Some Questions Bring Responsibility More Than Information

### Verse

> **2:215** — They ask you about giving: say, “The charity you give shall go to the parents, the relatives, the orphans, the poor, and the traveling alien.” Any good  you  do,  GOD  is  fully  aware thereof.

### Reflection on the Verse

2:215 answers a question about charity by directing attention to parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, and the traveling alien. The question moves from abstraction toward relationships and obligations.

This is a demanding form of knowledge. The answer can touch money, priorities, and the way vulnerable people are seen.

The sincerity of inquiry is therefore tested by whether I am ready not only to know the answer, but also to carry what the answer requires.

**Verse references:** 2:215.

2:217 answers a question about fighting during the Sacred Months but widens the moral frame to include greater violations: repelling from GOD's path, violating sanctity, expelling people, and oppression.

A narrow question can miss a larger injustice. The answer can therefore preserve the question while correcting its scale.

Truth-seeking inquiry must allow for the possibility that the problem is not only my lack of information, but the way I framed the issue in the first place.

**Verse references:** 2:217.

Knowledge separated from justice can preserve the form of an answer while losing its purpose. A good question should help protect, repair, and act rightly.

## 7. Intimate Questions Still Require Measure

### Verse

> **2:222** — They  ask  you  about  menstruation: say, “It is harmful; you shall avoid sexual  intercourse  with  the  women during  menstruation;  do  not  approach  them  until  they  are  rid  of  it. 2:222 answers a question about menstruation directly, without turning a bodily and intimate matter into either shame or spectacle. The answer speaks in terms of harm, restraint, purification, and relationship.

The Quranic presence of such a question reminds me that religious inquiry includes ordinary embodied life, not only abstract theology.

Yet openness does not justify endless intrusion. There remains a difference between necessary clarity and curiosity that violates proportion or dignity.

**Verse references:** 2:222.

## 8. The Question of What Is Lawful: Principle Before Endless Detail

### Verse

> **5:4** — They  consult  you  concerning  what is  lawful  for  them;  say,  “Lawful  for you  are  all  good  things,  including what  trained  dogs  and  falcons  catch for  you.”  You  train  them  according to  GOD’s  teachings.  You  may  eat what  they  catch  for  you,  and  mention  GOD’s  name  thereupon.  You shall  observe  GOD.  GOD  is  most efficient in reckoning.

### Reflection on the Verse

5:4 answers a question about what is lawful by first giving a broad principle: all good things. It then addresses a specific practical matter and closes with reverence for GOD and accountability.

This order matters. A person can become lost in hundreds of small details while forgetting the governing principle.

Questions about religion should not become a machine for multiplying rules beyond what GOD has given. Detail has a place, but it remains under the larger moral frame.

**Verse references:** 5:4.

## 9. If You Do Not Know, Ask

### Verse

> **16:43** — We did not send before you except men whom we inspired. Ask those who know the scripture, if you do not know.

### Reflection on the Verse

16:43 tells those who do not know to ask those who know the scripture. Read beside 5:101, the balance becomes clear: unnecessary probing can be harmful, but refusing to ask when one does not know is not a virtue.

Humility before knowledge begins with the ability to say, “I do not know.” Asking can prevent ignorance from hardening into confident error.

Yet asking an informed person does not surrender one's own responsibility. 17:36 still requires verification, and 39:18 still praises examination and following what is best.

**Verse references:** 16:43.

## 10. Some Questions Teach the Limits of Human Knowledge

### Verse

> **17:85** — They  ask  you  about  the  revelation. Say,  “The  revelation  comes  from  my Lord.  The  knowledge  given  to  you  is minute.” 

### Reflection on the Verse

17:85 responds to a question about revelation by reminding the hearer that only a little knowledge has been given to human beings.

This is an answer that teaches limits. Not possessing exhaustive knowledge does not mean truth is defective; the limitation may be ours.

Inquiry is healthiest when it expands knowledge and humility at the same time. The more one learns, the less credible the illusion of intellectual self-sufficiency becomes.

**Verse references:** 17:85.

## 11. Questions About What We Do Not Control

### Verse

> **7:187** — They ask you about the end of the world (the Hour),* and when it will come to pass. Say, “The knowledge thereof is with my Lord. Only He reveals its time.* Heavy it is, in the heavens and the earth. It will not come  to  you  except  suddenly.”** They ask you as if you are in control thereof. Say, “The knowledge thereof is with GOD,” but most people do not know. 

### Reflection on the Verse

7:187 answers the question about the Hour by stating that its knowledge belongs to the Lord and that its timing is disclosed only by Him. The verse notes that people question the messenger as if he controlled it.

Here inquiry can become entangled with the desire for control. Knowing an exact time can feel like mastery over uncertainty.

Submission appears precisely at this boundary: not in killing curiosity, but in recognizing that some knowledge and authority belong to GOD alone.

**Verse references:** 7:187.

## 12. Preparing for the Hour Instead of Possessing Its Date

### Verse

> **79:42** — They  ask  you  about  the  Hour,  and when it will take place!

### Reflection on the Verse

79:42 records the repeated question about when the Hour will occur. The persistence of the question reveals how strongly human beings desire a date.

But the moral weight of the Hereafter is greater than its timetable. If accountability is certain, the decisive question becomes not “when?” but “what am I sending ahead?”

This connects with the reflection in *What Did You Send Ahead for Tomorrow?*: the future is not ours to possess, but today's action is ours to answer for.

**Verse references:** 79:42.

## 13. An Answer Need Not Exhaust Every Possible Detail

### Verse

> **18:83** — They  ask  you  about  Zul-Qarnain. Say,  “I  will  narrate  to  you  some  of his history.” 

### Reflection on the Verse

When asked about Zul-Qarnain, 18:83 says that only part of his history will be narrated. The verse openly establishes proportion in answering.

Modern information culture often assumes that more detail always means better knowledge. Yet excess can obscure what matters.

Truthfulness includes knowing where to stop: saying what is given, refusing to manufacture what is absent, and leaving unprovided detail unfilled.

**Verse references:** 18:83.

## 14. A Short Answer Can Be a Complete Answer

### Verse

> **20:105** — They  ask  you  about  the  mountains Say, “My Lord will wipe them out.

### Reflection on the Verse

20:105 gives a direct answer to the question about the mountains. Not every question requires an elaborate theory.

Human beings can confuse length with depth. A clear revelation can become less clear when surrounded by speculation that the text itself does not authorize.

The discipline of inquiry therefore includes the discipline of ending the answer where the evidence ends.

**Verse references:** 20:105.

## 15. Asking Does Not Remove the Duty to Verify

### Verse

> **17:36** — You  shall  not  accept  any  information,  unless  you  verify  it  for  yourself. I  have  given  you  the  hearing,  the eyesight,  and  the  brain,  and  you  are responsible for using them.

### Reflection on the Verse

17:36 commands the person not to accept information without verification and ties hearing, sight, and the mind to responsibility.

Receiving an answer from an authority does not automatically make the answer true. Asking someone else does not outsource my moral responsibility to think and verify.

This creates an important limit around religious authority. Teachers and scholars can inform, but they do not become owners of another person's conscience.

**Verse references:** 17:36.

## 16. Investigate Before Information Becomes Injustice

### Verse

> **49:6** — O you who believe, if a wicked person brings any news to you, you shall first investigate, lest you commit injustice towards some people, out of ignorance, then become sorry and remorseful for what you have done.

### Reflection on the Verse

49:6 requires investigation when unreliable news arrives, because unverified information can produce injustice and later regret.

The ethics of knowledge therefore protects not only the inquirer but other people whose honor, safety, or rights can be harmed by careless belief and speech.

A truth-seeking question asks “Is this verified?” before accusation, repetition, and judgment. Research is not merely intellectual hygiene; it can be an act of justice.

**Verse references:** 49:6.

## 17. Examining All Words: Inquiry Does Not End with the First Answer

### Verse

> **39:18** — They  are  the  ones  who  examine  all words,  then  follow  the  best.  These are  the  ones  whom  GOD  has  guided;  these  are  the  ones  who  possess intelligence.

### Reflection on the Verse

39:18 praises those who examine all words and then follow the best. Listening is followed by discernment.

A question is only the beginning. Answers must be compared, tested, and weighed. If I collect only what confirms what I already wanted to believe, I may ask many questions without conducting any real inquiry.

The search for truth becomes serious when my own preferred conclusion is also allowed to be tested.

**Verse references:** 39:18.

## 18. The Manner of the Question Is Part of Its Ethics

### Verse

> **16:125** — You shall invite to the path of your Lord  with  wisdom  and  kind  enlightenment, and debate with them in the best possible manner. Your Lord knows best who has strayed from His path, and He knows best who are the guided ones.

### Reflection on the Verse

16:125 commands invitation with wisdom and kind enlightenment and debate in the best possible manner. The same moral atmosphere applies to questioning.

A question can be weaponized to humiliate, trap, or display another person's ignorance. Such questioning may be clever without being wise.

Truth-seeking inquiry cares about both the answer and the relationship in which the answer is sought. Wisdom, kindness, and justice shape the tone as well as the content.

**Verse references:** 16:125.

## 19. Arabic Root Reflections: Asking, Knowing, Clarifying, Verifying

### Verse

> **5:101** — O  you  who  believe,  do  not  ask  about matters  which,  if  revealed  to  you prematurely,  would  hurt  you.  If  you ask  about  them  in  light  of  the  Quran, they  will  become  obvious  to  you. GOD  has  deliberately  overlooked them. GOD is Forgiver, Clement.
>
> **16:43** — We did not send before you except men whom we inspired. Ask those who know the scripture, if you do not know.
>
> **17:36** — You  shall  not  accept  any  information,  unless  you  verify  it  for  yourself. I  have  given  you  the  hearing,  the eyesight,  and  the  brain,  and  you  are responsible for using them.

### Reflection on the Verse

The Quranic language of asking is connected with **س أ ل (s-ʾ-l)**, a root used for requesting and questioning. The root itself does not decide whether a question is sincere; context and purpose do.

Knowledge is repeatedly expressed through **ع ل م (ʿ-l-m)**. In 16:43, not knowing becomes a reason to ask; in 17:85, the human being is reminded how little knowledge has been given. Inquiry and humility therefore belong together.

The language of becoming clear in 5:101 is connected with the **ب ي ن (b-y-n)** field, carrying senses of distinction and clarification. A fruitful question should move toward clarity rather than merely multiplying speech.

17:36 finally places verification under moral responsibility. Hearing, sight, and mind are gifts that must be used; asking a question never removes the responsibility to evaluate the answer.

**Verse references:** 5:101; 16:43; 17:36.

## Other Claims

Historical interpreters have offered different contextual accounts for 5:101-102. Some restrict the warning to particular questions asked in an early community, while others draw a broader principle against manufacturing unnecessary religious burdens. Those discussions can be studied within their own sources.

They do not determine the main reflection here. The direct Quranic balance is sufficient: 5:101 warns about harmful questioning, 16:43 commands asking when one does not know, 17:85 teaches epistemic limits, and 17:36 together with 49:6 requires verification and investigation.

It would therefore be too simple to say either “the Quran discourages questions” or “every question is virtuous in every circumstance.” The verses present a more demanding ethics of inquiry.

## Conclusion

In the Quran, questioning is neither automatically praised nor automatically condemned. Some questions open into clarity and responsibility; some expose the limits of knowledge; some can create unnecessary burdens; and some can become a path of avoidance.

The measure that remains with me is this: **if my question seeks truth, I must also be willing to let the answer correct me.**

5:101 teaches restraint, 16:43 teaches the humility to ask, 17:85 teaches the poverty of human knowledge, 17:36 teaches verification, 49:6 teaches investigation before harm, and 39:18 teaches examination and following what is best.

A question can therefore become an act of truthfulness, justice, humility, and submission. But it can also become an endless corridor through which the ego escapes every answer. The difference is not merely in the words of the question; it is in what the person is willing to do when clarity arrives.
