# [43:40] Can You Make the Deaf Hear? Hearing, Understanding, and the Limits of Guidance

**Nineteen Quran Reflections on Listening, Understanding, Choice, Justice, and the Responsibility of the Messenger**

**Author:** Berk KÖKSAL

**Date:** November 07, 2026

**Location:** Mersin

## Introduction

Peace. Peace be upon you.

Sometimes I say something again and again. I am convinced that the person in front of me is not hearing it. I find another example, try a clearer explanation, raise my voice. Yet the feeling of not being understood remains. Eventually a question forms: What more can I possibly say?

The Quran places a more unsettling question before me: Can you make the deaf hear? At first it seems to concern the unresponsive listener. But reading what comes before and after 43:40 changes the direction of my attention. The people who believe themselves guided, the turning away from the message, the person entrusted with communicating it, and the limits of human authority all appear together.

In my earlier reflection <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/quran_heart_more_than_an_organ_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The Secrets of the Heart in the Quran: Is the Heart Merely an Organ?</a>, I examined 22:46 and the language of understanding. In <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/the-fine-line-between-proclaiming-and-proclaiming-yourself/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The Fine Line Between Proclaiming and Proclaiming Yourself</a>, I questioned the temptation to turn the act of carrying a message into authority over others. Both questions return here: Am I listening to the truth, or listening only for the echo of my own voice?

This reflection will not equate a physical disability with a spiritual fault. Deafness does not make a person unreceptive to truth; functioning ears do not guarantee understanding. Reading these verses attentively is also a matter of refusing to degrade the people to whom the imagery does not literally apply.

My purpose is to consider the verses together and ask what bearing witness to the oneness of GOD means for knowledge, fairness, truthful speech, and submission, both in the person who speaks and the person who listens. Perhaps the most valuable result will not be a better classification of other people but a more honest examination of myself.

## 1. Can You Make the Deaf Hear? The Limit the Question Places on Me

### Verse

> **43:40**
>
> Arapça: أَفَأَنتَ تُسْمِعُ ٱلصُّمَّ أَوْ تَهْدِى ٱلْعُمْىَ وَمَن كَانَ فِى ضَلَٰلٍ مُّبِينٍ
>
> English: Can  you  make  the  deaf  hear;  can you  make  the  blind  see,  or  those  who are far astray?
>
> Türkçe: Sen sağırlara işittirebilir misin; körlerin ya da çok uzak sapanların görmelerini sağlayabilir misin?

Source: <a href="https://kuranteyit.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">kuranteyit.com</a>

Translation: Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation. The Turkish text is the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation.

### Reflection on the Verse

When I first encounter this question, my mind almost immediately looks for other people: those who do not understand what I say, those who reject an argument I consider convincing, those with whom I have had the same conversation many times. Yet the question stops me before I turn it into an accusation. It addresses the limits of the person carrying a message. I can speak to another human being; I cannot remake that person's inward life by my own will.

There is no disgrace in admitting this boundary. A statement can be true without producing the same response in every listener. If I forget the difference, I may begin by sharing something I believe is right and end by claiming authority over someone else's conscience. When I feel certain, I can raise my voice; when that fails, I can become contemptuous. Perhaps the verse examines not only the listener but also the role the speaker imagines for himself.

The Authorized English Translation says “far astray.” Yet the Arabic wording deserves its own careful examination; I will return to that in the discussion of roots. I do not want an emphatic expression to become my personal scale for measuring living people. My first task is simpler: distinguish bringing a message from trying to control another person's response.

**Verse references:** 43:40.

## 2. Feeling Guided While Being Led Elsewhere

### Verse

> **43:36** — Anyone  who  disregards  the  message  of  the  Most  Gracious,  we  appoint  a  devil  to  be  his  constant  companion.*

> **43:37** — Such  companions  will  divert  them from  the  path,  yet  make  them  believe that they are guided.

> **43:38** — When  he  comes  before  us  he  will say,  “Oh  I  wish  you  were  as  far  from me  as  the  two  easts.*  What  a  miserable companion!”

> **43:39** — It  will  not  console  you  on  that  day, as  transgressors,  that  both  of  you will share in the retribution.

### Reflection on the Verse

The passage immediately preceding 43:40 makes me think that hearing is not merely about sound. It describes disregarding the message, being accompanied by a misleading companion, and nevertheless believing that one is guided. The frightening possibility is not only that I may be wrong. It is that I may become comfortable enough in my error to stop noticing it.

I cannot treat that possibility as something that belongs only to other people. If I surround myself with approval, if every screen shows me only the arguments I already enjoy, if I take every disagreement as an insult, my certainty may grow while my understanding remains untested. I am not equating every digital feed or friendly voice with the companion described in the verse. The passage simply prompts me to examine what is feeding my confidence.

To listen sincerely may mean giving a fair hearing to the objection I least want to hear. If I can absorb hundreds of affirmations yet refuse one justified criticism, what exactly makes me so certain that I am guided? I want that question directed toward myself before I apply it to anyone else.

**Verse references:** 43:36, 43:37, 43:38, 43:39.

## 3. Listening Is Not the Same as Understanding

### Verse

> **10:42** — Some  of  them  listen  to  you,  but can  you  make  the  deaf  hear,  even though they cannot understand?

> **10:43** — Some  of  them  look  at  you,  but  can you  guide  the  blind,  even  though they do not see?

> **10:44** — GOD  never  wrongs  the  people;  it  is the  people  who  wrong  their  own souls.

### Reflection on the Verse

The contrast is unusually sharp here: some people listen, yet understanding does not follow. Some look, yet seeing does not follow. Access to words and engagement with their meaning are not identical. This is not presented as a mere technical failure of the speaker's voice.

As a musician, I can hear a recording and identify individual notes without understanding how the music holds together. That is only an analogy from my own experience, not a claim that the verse is about music. The point is that a signal reaching my ears does not finish the work of comprehension. Attention has to take place inside the listener.

The following verse, 10:44, matters to me too. When I see failure to understand, I should not rush to accuse GOD of injustice or claim to know everything occurring in another person's mind. The verse places human responsibility in the foreground. Still, I have no access to another person's complete intentions. I can hear someone's words without knowing everything that shaped them. The same limitation applies when others listen to me.

**Verse references:** 10:42, 10:43, 10:44.

## 4. Turning Away: The Call and the Decision Not to Receive It

### Verse

> **27:79** — Therefore,  put  your  trust  in  GOD; you are following the manifest truth. 

> **27:80** — You  cannot  make  the  dead,  nor  the deaf, hear the call, if they turn away.

> **27:81** — Nor  can  you  guide  the  blind  out  of their  straying.  The  only  ones  who will  hear  you  are  those  who  believe in  our  revelations,  and  decide  to  be submitters.

### Reflection on the Verse

In 27:80, the inability to make the call heard is linked to turning away. This changes the question I bring to the passage. Instead of asking how a speaker can produce an ever more impressive demonstration, I begin to ask whether the listener is prepared to consider what is offered. A call may be present without a receptive response.

When I do not want to hear someone, I can manufacture sophisticated excuses. I can attack their manner, change the subject, or make a minor detail the center of a new dispute. Of course, criticism of someone's manner can be legitimate, and disagreement is not automatically a moral failure. I cannot use these verses to condemn every refusal. But I can ask myself whether I actually examined a claim or searched for ways not to examine it.

Verse 27:79 also turns my attention toward the speaker. Reliance is directed toward GOD, not toward applause or a favorable response. That reliance does not entitle me to despise an audience. It should loosen my desire to own the outcome.

**Verse references:** 27:79, 27:80, 27:81.

## 5. Why Does the Warning Return in Almost the Same Words?

### Verse

> **30:52** — You cannot make the dead, nor the deaf, hear the call, once they turn away.

> **30:53** — Nor can you guide the blind out of their  straying.  You  can  only  be heard by those who believe in our revelations, and decide to become submitters.

### Reflection on the Verse

Reading these verses beside 27:80–81, I notice the return of nearly the same warning in another chapter. The call can be made, but no human speaker can take over the listener's inward decision. Repetition does not invite me simply to speak louder; it urges me to recognize the boundary.

When a conversation fails, I can move toward either extreme. I may press the same argument until the other person yields, or I may decide never to speak again. The verses do not turn either impulse into a universal rule. There is a difference between reminding someone and trying to force the desired answer. Whether another conversation is appropriate also calls for patience and fairness.

The choice to believe and become a submitter is present in 30:53. I cannot make that choice for someone else. I may offer a space for reflection, but I cannot believe on another person's behalf. That same thought returns to me as a challenge: am I as open to examination as I wish others to be?

**Verse references:** 30:52, 30:53.

## 6. Saying “We Hear” Is Not Enough

### Verse

> **8:21** — Do not be like those who say, “We hear,” when they do not hear.

> **8:22** — The worst creatures in the sight of GOD are the deaf and dumb, who do not understand.

> **8:23** — Had GOD known of any good in them, He would have made them hearers.  Even  if  He  made  them hearers, they still would turn away in aversion.

### Reflection on the Verse

The warning about people who say they hear when they do not hear is unsettling because it could describe a performance of attention. I can recite a verse accurately, sit through a discussion, and even take notes while refusing to let a justified correction reach my conduct. An outward gesture of agreement is not a certificate of understanding.

Verse 8:22 uses stark language. I must not take it as permission to degrade people with hearing or speech disabilities. The wording itself connects the description to a failure to understand; the surrounding verses speak about the claim to hear and the act of turning away. To turn that ethical context into contempt for a physical disability would add a license the passage does not give me.

Nor do I want to dissolve the severity of 8:23. There is a real warning about aversion to guidance. But the verse does not appoint me to inspect another person's hidden condition. The more immediate test is whether I have confused appearing attentive with being answerable to what I hear.

**Verse references:** 8:21, 8:22, 8:23.

## 7. Those Who Respond: Hearing Beyond Reception

### Verse

> **6:36** — The  only  ones  to  respond  are  those who  listen.  GOD  resurrects  the dead; they ultimately return to Him.

> **25:73** — When reminded of their Lord’s revelations, they never react to them as if they were deaf and blind.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 6:36 connects hearing with responding. I take this to mean that the subject is not exhausted by passive sensory reception. What happens after I encounter a truth matters. If I acknowledge something only in conversation but never allow it to affect my decisions, knowledge may have stopped at the threshold.

Verse 25:73 also directs my attention to the way a person reacts when reminded of revelation. It does not lead me toward uncritical acceptance of every claim presented under a religious label. It invites me not to pass by revelation without serious engagement. A thoughtful response requires looking at what I am accepting, rather than letting a familiar phrase do the work for me.

Some words remain with me for years; long speeches can leave little trace. I cannot turn that observation into a ranking of people's spiritual worth. It does remind me that the length and volume of a message are not the same as the honesty of my attention. Hearing matters; what I do with it matters too.

**Verse references:** 6:36, 25:73.

## 8. Where Is Real Blindness? Keeping the Metaphor in Context

### Verse

> **22:46** — Did they not roam the earth, then use their minds to understand, and use their ears to hear? Indeed, the real blindness is not the blindness of the eyes, but the blindness of the hearts inside the chests.

### Reflection on the Verse

This verse slows me down because it explicitly considers eyes, ears, and understanding. Its distinction about real blindness helps determine how I should read the other images. Instead of treating a bodily condition as an ethical fault, I must examine the use I make of the capacities I have.

I do not read this as a scientific claim about the anatomy of thought. The subject concerns how human beings engage with what they encounter. Seeing events, traveling, hearing testimony, and gathering evidence do not necessarily mean that I have understood or acted responsibly. What have I learned? Have I ignored what would require me to change?

My eyes may function well while pride blinds my judgment. My ears may receive every word while anger prevents me from listening fairly. These are my moral analogies, not medical descriptions of other people. The distinction in 22:46 guards me against placing an ethical stigma on physical blindness or deafness.

**Verse references:** 22:46.

## 9. The Blind Man Who Came Eagerly: Whose Attention Matters?

### Verse

> **80:1** — He  (Muhammad)  frowned  and turned away.

> **80:2** — When the blind man came to him.

> **80:3** — How  do  you  know?  He  may  purify himself.

> **80:4** — Or  he  may  take  heed,  and  benefit from the message.

> **80:7** — Even  though  you  could  not  guarantee his salvation.

> **80:8** — The one who came to you eagerly.

> **80:9** — And is really reverent.

> **80:10** — You ignored him.

### Reflection on the Verse

This passage challenges any careless use I might make of the language of hearing and blindness. A blind man comes seeking a message, and the failure to give him due attention is criticized. His physical condition does not diminish the seriousness of his search. The concern is with the attention offered by the one responsible for speaking.

It makes me reconsider whom I listen to. Am I more attentive to someone with status, a large following, or wealth? Do I overlook the person who arrives quietly and genuinely wants to learn? I do not claim that this passage settles every modern communication dilemma. Yet it clearly prevents me from equating social visibility with worthiness of attention.

A deaf person is not intellectually or spiritually unreachable because of deafness. Nor does a hearing person necessarily receive what is said with understanding. After reading 80:1–10, the word “deaf” becomes impossible for me to use as an easy insult. My responsibility may begin with making communication accessible and approaching the person before me without prejudice.

**Verse references:** 80:1, 80:2, 80:3, 80:4, 80:7, 80:8, 80:9, 80:10.

## 10. Can Repeating the Sound Become Understanding?

### Verse

> **2:171** — The  example  of  such  disbelievers  is that  of  parrots  who  repeat  what  they hear  of  sounds  and  calls,  without understanding.  Deaf,  dumb,  and blind; they cannot understand.

### Reflection on the Verse

This verse draws a sharp distinction between repeating what is heard and understanding it. A human being may reproduce the words of a message without allowing the thought behind them to take shape. That warning is uncomfortable for speakers as well as listeners. Could I pronounce the right sentences beautifully while resisting the work those sentences demand of me?

A musician can perform a melody flawlessly without asking why it belongs where it does in the larger piece. That analogy does not explain the entire verse; it helps me keep repetition and comprehension apart. Memorization is not worthless. It simply cannot stand in for every aspect of reflection.

There is also a social danger here. When enough people repeat a claim in the same words, its familiarity may begin to resemble proof. Volume and agreement are not substitutes for judgment. I need to ask what a statement means and then what I have actually done with it.

**Verse references:** 2:171.

## 11. If Hearing Is a Trust, Verification Is a Responsibility

### Verse

> **17:36** — You  shall  not  accept  any  information,  unless  you  verify  it  for  yourself. I  have  given  you  the  hearing,  the eyesight,  and  the  brain,  and  you  are responsible for using them.

> **39:18** — They  are  the  ones  who  examine  all words,  then  follow  the  best.  These are  the  ones  whom  GOD  has  guided;  these  are  the  ones  who  possess intelligence.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 17:36 refuses to leave me as a passive recipient of information. It connects hearing, sight, and thought with responsibility. Verse 39:18 connects examining words with following what is best. Read together, they suggest a way between believing everything I hear and refusing to hear anything at all: listen, investigate, and remain responsible for the conclusion.

In arguments I sometimes discover that I am not waiting to understand the next sentence. I am waiting for my turn to reply. My decision has been made before the other person finishes. If I want to change that habit, I need to hear the whole claim, verify the reference, and consider the part that conflicts with my existing view. Discomfort is not proof of falsehood, just as agreement is not proof of truth.

Digital Quran research can make that examination easier, but speed can also disguise shallow certainty. A search result is not understanding. I must still read the entire verse and its context. The obligation to verify applies to me as much as to the person whose argument I am criticizing.

**Verse references:** 17:36, 39:18.

## 12. The Lock on the Mind: To Whom Is the Question Addressed?

### Verse

> **47:24** — Why do they not study the Quran carefully? Do they have locks on their minds?

### Reflection on the Verse

At first glance I could point this verse toward other people. I would rather turn it toward my own mind. A locked mind may not recognize its own lock. On subjects that appear completely settled to me, the possibility of being mistaken can feel almost offensive.

So I want to ask where I refuse disagreement before it is even expressed. Which of my opinions has become beyond question? When does a verse become uncomfortable precisely because it calls for a change in my own behavior? No simple answer covers every case, but the call to examine the Quran carefully prevents me from treating reflection as a weapon aimed only outward.

I may criticize a position, question its evidence, or oppose an injustice. That is not the same as claiming to know the entire unseen state of the person who holds the position. Verse 47:24 reminds me that the first mind I can responsibly examine is my own.

**Verse references:** 47:24.

## 13. Veils and Deafness: Reading Severe Passages Without Pretending to Know Hearts

### Verse

> **17:46** — We  place  shields  around  their minds,  to  prevent  them  from  understanding  it,  and  deafness  in  their ears.  And  when  you  preach  your Lord, using the Quran alone,* they run away in aversion.

> **6:25** — Some of them listen to you, but we place veils on their hearts to prevent them from understanding, and deafness in their ears. Thus, no matter what kind of proof they see, they cannot believe. Thus, when they come to argue with you, the disbelievers say, “These are tales from the past.”

### Reflection on the Verse

These verses keep me from reducing every refusal of revelation to a harmless habit or a minor misunderstanding. They speak with gravity about veils, deafness, rejection, and aversion. It would be unfaithful to the text to erase that severity. Yet it would also be unwarranted to announce that a person I met today can never change.

There is a difference between the limits of my understanding and the knowledge of GOD. I do not know the whole history behind another person's response. I cannot read every fear, injury, habit, and intention at a glance. I mention these possibilities not to rewrite the verses as psychological theories but to acknowledge how little I know about an individual human being.

Verse 17:46 is especially striking in the context of preaching with the Quran alone. A refusal may occur even when the message is brought directly from scripture. That does not make contempt my duty. It makes careful delivery and honesty about my limits more important.

**Verse references:** 17:46, 6:25.

## 14. Deviating and Being Diverted: Why the Sequence Matters

### Verse

> **61:5** — Recall  that  Moses  said  to  his  people,  “O  my  people,  why  do  you  hurt me,  even  though  you  know  that  I  am GOD’s  messenger  to  you?”  When they  deviated,  GOD  diverted  their hearts.  For  GOD  does  not  guide  the wicked people.

> **7:146** — I  will  divert  from  My  revelations those  who  are  arrogant  on  earth, without justification.  Consequently, when  they  see  every  kind  of  proof they  will  not  believe.  And  when  they see  the  path  of  guidance  they  will not  adopt  it  as  their  path,  but  when they  see  the  path  of  straying  they  will adopt  it  as  their  path.  This  is  the consequence  of  their  rejecting  our proofs,  and  being  totally  heedless thereof.

### Reflection on the Verse

I notice a sequence in 61:5: a deviation by the people is followed by GOD diverting their hearts. Verse 7:146 also links unjustified arrogance with refusing the proofs and adopting the path of straying. Human response to guidance is therefore not a trivial matter.

I need to be cautious about how far I take this observation. I do not possess a mechanical description of how GOD deals with each heart. Nor do these verses give me a fixed psychological timetable for when a person becomes unreachable. Their more immediate demand on me is to stop defending an error through pride when I have reason to recognize it.

If I lose the ability to reconsider my own position, I may become less able to receive correction. But that possibility does not authorize me to pronounce someone else's final spiritual condition. Guarding my choices and claiming GOD's judgment over another person are different actions.

**Verse references:** 61:5, 7:146.

## 15. I Cannot Guide Even Someone I Love by Force of Will

### Verse

> **28:56** — You cannot guide the ones you love  GOD is the only One who guides in accordance with His will, and in accordance with His knowledge of those who deserve the guidance.

> **35:22** — Nor  are  the  living  and  the  dead; GOD  causes  whomever  He  wills  to hear.  You  cannot  make  hearers  out of those in the graves.

### Reflection on the Verse

These verses set a boundary even around good intentions. Loving someone does not give me the power to believe for them or decide their inward direction. I may explain, accompany, and speak at a suitable moment. I cannot manufacture faith by affection, disappointment, or insistence.

That boundary can hurt. In families and friendships, when I see a choice I believe is harmful, I may feel responsible for securing a different outcome. Verse 28:56 reminds me that the final work of guidance is not mine to own. Verse 35:22 relates the capacity to hear to GOD's will. Neither passage excuses me from fairness or care. They prevent me from making myself the source of another person's change.

Sometimes the difficult act of love is to remain truthful without becoming controlling. Can I continue to care while giving up pressure? Can I state what I believe without reducing someone I love to a project that must end on my preferred terms?

**Verse references:** 28:56, 35:22.

## 16. The Ethics of Speaking: Wisdom, Gentleness, and Fair Debate

### Verse

> **20:43** — “Go  to  Pharaoh,  for  he  transgressed.

> **20:44** — “Speak  to  him  nicely;  he  may  take heed, or become reverent.”

> **16:125** — You shall invite to the path of your Lord  with  wisdom  and  kind  enlightenment, and debate with them in the best possible manner. Your Lord knows best who has strayed from His path, and He knows best who are the guided ones.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verses 20:43–44 tell us both whom the messengers are addressing and how they are to speak. The wrongdoing of the addressee does not cancel the instruction to speak gently. Verse 16:125 asks for wisdom, kind enlightenment, and the best manner of debate. I hear an ethical demand here, not merely a technique for winning a conversation.

When I argue, what is driving me? A concern for truth, or the pleasure of humiliating the person who disagrees? Anger can move me from examining a claim to defeating a human being. These verses do not support the conclusion that an accurate statement licenses contempt. Tone is not proof of truth, but conduct must not contradict the justice I claim to defend.

Gentleness does not require agreement with every idea. I may disagree clearly and oppose harm without treating the other person as less than human. What I say matters; how responsibly I say it matters too.

**Verse references:** 20:43, 20:44, 16:125.

## 17. Reminding Is Not the Same as Controlling

### Verse

> **88:21** — You  shall  remind,  for  your  mission is to deliver this reminder. 

> **88:22** — You have no power over them. 

> **50:45** — We are fully aware of everything they utter, while you have no power over them. Therefore, remind with this Quran, those who reverence My warnings.

> **2:256** — There shall be no compulsion in religion: the right way is now distinct from the wrong way. Anyone who denounces the devil and believes in GOD  has  grasped  the  strongest bond; one that never breaks. GOD is Hearer, Omniscient.

### Reflection on the Verse

These verses distinguish a mission from power over people. There is a duty to remind, but not a mandate to control hearts. Verse 2:256 makes the boundary against compulsion in religion explicit. If I crush another person's freedom in the name of explaining guidance, I may be contradicting the responsibility I claim to serve.

It is easy to experience someone's lack of agreement as a personal defeat. I may speak longer, write more sharply, or try to make that person feel guilty until a desired answer appears. Yet the passages do not define my task as producing agreement on demand. Honest reminder and ownership of the outcome are two different things.

This boundary is not an excuse for indifference. I still need to respond to sincere questions, correct my own mistakes, and avoid making judgments without listening. Speaking without coercion is not silence. It is speaking without declaring oneself master over another person's conscience.

**Verse references:** 88:21, 88:22, 50:45, 2:256.

## 18. Can I Remain Just Toward Someone Who Will Not Listen to Me?

### Verse

> **5:8** — O  you  who  believe,  you  shall  be  absolutely  equitable,  and  observe GOD,  when  you  serve  as  witnesses. Do  not  be  provoked  by  your  conflicts  with  some  people  into  committing  injustice.  You  shall  be  absolutely  equitable,  for  it  is  more  righteous. You  shall  observe  GOD.  GOD  is fully  Cognizant  of  everything  you do.

> **4:135** — O  you who  believe,  you  shall  be absolutely  equitable,  and  observe GOD,  when  you  serve  as  witnesses, even  against  yourselves,  or  your parents,  or  your  relatives.  Whether the  accused  is  rich  or  poor,  GOD takes  care  of  both.  Therefore,  do not  be  biased  by  your  personal wishes.  If  you  deviate  or  disregard (this  commandment),  then  GOD  is fully  Cognizant  of  everything  you do.

### Reflection on the Verse

Feeling unheard can gradually become resentment. When someone rejects an argument I care deeply about, I may begin to interpret everything that person does through suspicion. That is where reflection meets the test of justice. Verse 5:8 warns against allowing conflict to provoke injustice. Verse 4:135 makes fairness binding even when it turns against me.

A person may reject my words; I cannot therefore diminish their rights. They may argue harshly with me; I cannot therefore spread a false claim about them. They may not accept my belief; I cannot therefore invent motives for them without evidence. These are questions I draw into my conduct from the verses' call for justice, not additional rulings I claim the verses stated word for word.

My inability to force someone to listen does not give me permission to silence them unfairly. If I want my own words to receive a fair hearing, I must take seriously the duty to hear the person with whom I disagree.

**Verse references:** 5:8, 4:135.

## 19. The Responsibility That Remains: Am I Listening to the Message I Carry?

### Verse

> **43:43** — You  shall  steadfastly  preach  what  is revealed  to  you;  you  are  in  the  right path.*

> **43:44** — This  is  a  message  for  you  and  your people; all of you will be questioned.

> **2:44** — Do  you  exhort  the  people  to  be righteous,  while  forgetting  yourselves,  though  you  read  the  scripture? Do you not understand?

> **67:10** — They also say, “If we heard or understood, we would not be among the dwellers of Hell!”

### Reflection on the Verse

Returning to the verses that follow 43:40, I see a necessary balance. After the question about whom a human speaker can make hear comes an instruction to hold firmly to revelation and a reminder of accountability. The audience's response cannot release me from responsibility for my own words and actions.

Verse 2:44 warns about exhorting others while forgetting oneself. That is a particularly serious risk when writing reflections. If I tell others to study carefully but quote carelessly, if I praise open-mindedness but reject every correction, if I speak about justice but use two different standards for friends and opponents, my work has turned back upon me.

Verse 67:10 places hearing and understanding in a grave scene of regret. I do not use it to declare the final destiny of someone who disagrees with me today. I read it as a reminder that hearing is not a minor responsibility.

Perhaps the opening question reaches its most difficult form here. While asking whether I can make the deaf hear, have I truly learned to listen myself?

**Verse references:** 43:43, 43:44, 2:44, 67:10.

## Arabic Word Roots: Hearing, Turning Away, and Manifest Straying

In 43:40, **تُسْمِعُ** (*tusmiʿu*) comes from the root **س م ع** (*s-m-ʿ*), the semantic field of hearing. The verbal form raises the question of making another person hear, rather than merely hearing oneself. The word **ٱلصُّمَّ** (*aṣ-ṣumma*) belongs to **ص م م** (*ṣ-m-m*), associated with deafness. The pairing gives the question its force: transmitting a sound is not the same power as creating understanding or acceptance in another person (43:40; 10:42).

**تَهْدِى** (*tahdī*) derives from **ه د ي** (*h-d-y*), guidance. **ٱلْعُمْىَ** (*al-ʿumya*) is related to **ع م ي** (*ʿ-m-y*), blindness. These images are placed alongside guidance, but 22:46 explicitly distinguishes physical eyes from the inward blindness it discusses. The encounter with the blind man in 80:1–10 prevents me from using this vocabulary as a moral judgment against people with disabilities.

The ending of 10:42 contains **لَا يَعْقِلُونَ** (*lā yaʿqilūn*), involving **ع ق ل** (*ʿ-q-l*), understanding and reasoning. That matters because hearing and comprehension are set apart. The term **مُدْبِرِينَ** (*mudbirīn*) in 27:80 and 30:52 is related to **د ب ر** (*d-b-r*) and occurs in the context of turning away. A root does not settle a verse by itself; the actual sentence and context are essential.

One particularly important translation distinction occurs at the end of 43:40. The Arabic is **فِى ضَلَٰلٍ مُّبِينٍ**: **ض ل ل** (*ḍ-l-l*) concerns straying, while **ب ي ن** (*b-y-n*) concerns what is clear or manifest. It does not use **بَعِيد** (*baʿīd*), the Arabic adjective for “far.” Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation nevertheless reads *far astray*, and the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation renders the idea as *çok uzak sapanlar*. I do not alter either translation. I distinguish the Arabic form, the translation choice, and any inference I may be tempted to draw from the adjective. It would be a mistake to construct a precise scale of spiritual distance from the translated wording alone.

The roots do not give me a secret verdict about other people. They give me more precise questions about hearing, understanding, turning away, and the limits of my own authority (43:40; 10:42; 27:80; 22:46).

## Other Claims

Some traditional approaches understand the images of deafness and blindness as signs of a settled spiritual refusal to accept the truth. Other approaches place more emphasis on human choice and the possibility of later change. Both raise serious questions, but neither gives me a basis for claiming that I know the final state of a living person. The continuation of the duty to carry the message in 43:43–44, together with the limit on human guidance in 28:56, keeps my own authority within bounds.

Psychological research discusses **confirmation bias**, a tendency to seek or interpret information in ways that favor what one already believes. Raymond Nickerson's <a href="https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2680.2.2.175" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">1998 review</a> examines different forms of this phenomenon. It offers a useful comparison with the possibility of feeling guided while being led elsewhere in 43:36–37. It does not scientifically establish the meaning of those verses, identify a devil as a psychological mechanism, or justify a spiritual diagnosis of someone else.

Another area is **psychological reactance**: people can resist persuasive messages when they experience them as threats to their freedom. A <a href="https://doi.org/10.1093/hcr/hqaf016" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">meta-analysis published in 2025 for the January 2026 issue</a> reports associations between freedom-threatening language, anger, and resistance. This does not prove verse 16:125 by experiment. It provides separate context for considering the possible costs of coercive communication. Not every disagreement has the same explanation.

Modern disability-language guidance also deserves care. The <a href="https://www.nih.gov/nih-style-guide/person-first-destigmatizing-language" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NIH guidance on destigmatizing language</a> advises respecting the terminology and identity preferences of deaf people and their communities. Such guidance is not a replacement for the Quran. It does, however, support the caution I want to exercise in reading 22:46 and 80:1–10 without turning physical disability into an insult.

None of these outside approaches governs the central reflection. The central question remains whether I preserve fairness, truthful speech, and humility when speaking to someone I believe is not listening.

## Conclusion

### Verse

> **43:40** — Can  you  make  the  deaf  hear;  can you  make  the  blind  see,  or  those  who are far astray?

> **10:42** — Some  of  them  listen  to  you,  but can  you  make  the  deaf  hear,  even though they cannot understand?

> **22:46** — Did they not roam the earth, then use their minds to understand, and use their ears to hear? Indeed, the real blindness is not the blindness of the eyes, but the blindness of the hearts inside the chests.

> **16:125** — You shall invite to the path of your Lord  with  wisdom  and  kind  enlightenment, and debate with them in the best possible manner. Your Lord knows best who has strayed from His path, and He knows best who are the guided ones.

> **88:21** — You  shall  remind,  for  your  mission is to deliver this reminder. 

> **88:22** — You have no power over them. 

### Reflection on the Verse

The journey that began with 43:40 has brought me somewhere I did not expect. I began by asking why someone else might not understand. I now find myself asking what the sound of my own voice does to me. There can be a dangerous distance between defending the truth and behaving as if I own it.

Hearing is not merely a matter of ears. Verse 10:42 brings understanding into view; 27:80, turning away; 22:46, inward perception; 17:36, verification; 16:125, the ethics of speaking; and 88:21–22, the limits of the messenger's power. I do not want to compress these passages into one convenient slogan. Each exposes a different part of human responsibility.

It may be easy to look down on someone who will not listen to me. It is harder to protect that person's rights while speaking clearly. It may be easy to insist that someone I love must change. It is harder to combine care with respect for a boundary I cannot cross. It is easy to search for locks on other people's minds. The hard task is to touch the lock on my own.

For me, the measure cannot simply be how much I speak. It must include whether I speak truthfully, fairly, and with attention. Above all, it must include whether I am still willing to listen when other people seem unwilling to listen to me.

### Nineteen Questions for Examining My Own Hearing

1. Have I reached a verdict before reading the verse carefully? (47:24)
2. Do I hear the argument that challenges my favorite opinion to the end? (39:18)
3. Do I verify what I hear against its source? (17:36)
4. Do I ask whether I understand what I can repeat? (2:171)
5. Do I confuse saying “I heard” with genuine attention? (8:21)
6. Do I mistake someone's silence for proof of their spiritual condition? (10:42–43)
7. Do I understand that turning away and coercing a person are two different issues? (27:80–81)
8. Have I quietly assigned myself the duty to convince everyone? (43:40)
9. Do I refuse to portray disability as a moral failure? (22:46; 80:1–4)
10. Do I give attention to the person who seeks to learn, regardless of status? (80:7–10)
11. Could the voices affirming me be hiding an error I need to examine? (43:36–37)
12. Have I rejected evidence because of pride? (7:146)
13. When I find an error in myself, do I correct it instead of defending it? (61:5)
14. Am I attempting to control a loved one's inward choice? (28:56)
15. Do I preserve wisdom and kindness while defending a truth? (20:44; 16:125)
16. Have I turned reminding into an excuse for gaining power over others? (88:21–22)
17. Do I avoid compulsion in matters of faith? (2:256)
18. Can I remain fair toward someone who rejects my words? (5:8; 4:135)
19. While encouraging others to reflect, do I examine my own behavior? (2:44; 43:43–44)

The question finally returns in a more personal form: **While asking whether I can make the deaf hear, have I examined my own listening with equal honesty?**

**Verse references:** 43:40; 10:42; 27:80–81; 22:46; 17:36; 16:125; 88:21–22; 2:44.
