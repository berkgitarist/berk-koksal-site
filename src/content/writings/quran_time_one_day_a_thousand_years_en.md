## 1. Are a Thousand Years Fitted Inside a Single Day?

### Verse

**22:47**

**Arabic**

<blockquote>
<p lang="ar" dir="rtl">وَيَسْتَعْجِلُونَكَ بِٱلْعَذَابِ وَلَن يُخْلِفَ ٱللَّهُ وَعْدَهُۥ وَإِنَّ يَوْمًا عِندَ رَبِّكَ كَأَلْفِ سَنَةٍ مِّمَّا تَعُدُّونَ</p>
</blockquote>

**English — Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation**

> They challenge you to bring retribution, and GOD never fails to fulfill His prophecy. A day of your Lord is like a thousand of your years.

**Turkish — the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation**

> Sana azabı getirmen için meydan okuyorlar ama TANRI kehanetini yerine getirmeyi asla ihmal etmez. Rabbinin bir günü sizin bin yılınız gibidir.

Source: <a href="https://kuranteyit.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">kuranteyit.com</a>

### Reflection on the Verse

When I read that a day is compared to a thousand years, my mind immediately reaches for a calculation. Does time pass differently somewhere else? Can a duration we consider long be very short on another scale? Or is our habit of treating our own waiting as the measure of all reality being questioned?

I approach 22:47 with this curiosity. But if I take only its last sentence and leave the first behind, I have already shifted the verse’s center of gravity. There is a striking comparison concerning time here; in the same verse, there is also the challenge to bring retribution and GOD’s fulfillment of His prophecy. The number does not stand outside that relationship.

The word “equal” in the title also needs care. In Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation, 22:47 uses “like”; the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation uses “gibidir.” I cannot disregard the comparison. But treating it as a general multiplication rule that produces the same result wherever it is applied requires further justification.

For me, this is where the question becomes deeper: as I investigate what a day corresponds to, what authority am I giving my own clock? When something does not happen at the moment I expect, do I really know that it will not happen, or have I reached the limit of my willingness to wait?

## 2. The Impatience Within the Challenge

### Verse

> **22:47** — They challenge you to bring retribution, and GOD never fails to fulfill His prophecy. A day of your Lord is like a thousand of your years.

> **22:48** — Many a community in the past committed evil, and I led them on for awhile, then I punished them. To Me is the ultimate destiny.

### Reflection on the Verse

Reading 22:47 and 22:48 together, I encounter an attitude that turns elapsed time into a certificate of acquittal. If a consequence has not yet appeared, it is easy to conclude that it never will. Yet waiting longer and finding a declaration invalid are not the same thing.

There is a moral side to this distinction. When I see someone else’s wrongdoing, I may want the account settled immediately. When I confront my own, I look for time to think, return, and put things right. If I regard the time allowed to someone else as a problem and the time allowed to me as an opportunity, I may not be speaking impartially about time.

The emphasis on ultimate destiny in 22:48 makes me consider that the present appearance of things is not the final judgment. I do not infer from this that I know what will happen to a particular person or when. On the contrary, I am questioning my habit of writing the last sentence of a story whose ending I have not seen.

The comparison between one day and a thousand years therefore confronts not only my fascination with a large number but also my impatience. Perhaps the harder task is not imagining a thousand years. It is refusing to use my own waiting as evidence against GOD.

## 3. The Years We Count: Measurement Is Not a Delusion

### Verse

> **10:5** — He  is  the  One  who  rendered  the  sun radiant,  and  the  moon  a  light,  and He  designed  its  phases  that  you  may learn  to  count  the  years  and  to  calculate.  GOD  did  not  create  all  this, except  for  a  specific  purpose.  He explains  the  revelations  for  people who know.

> **17:12** — We rendered the night and the day two signs. We made the night dark, and the day lighted, that you may seek  provisions  from  your  Lord therein. This also establishes for you a timing system, and the means of calculation. We thus explain everything in detail.

### Reflection on the Verse

10:5 and 17:12 stand in the way of a reading that devalues the measurement of time. Counting years, calculating, and distinguishing night from day are not excluded from human life. They appear before me as a field open to understanding and to the ordering of life.

I therefore do not want to turn the comparison in 22:47 into the statement that our time is unreal. A measure’s limitations do not make it useless or imaginary. I use a calendar to arrange a meeting, plan a piece of work, and keep track of the day on which I promised to do something. The problem is not counting. It is treating what I can count as the complete measure of everything that exists.

Here I distinguish two questions. “How much time has passed?” is a question about duration. “What does the passage of this time show about an event’s truth or GOD’s judgment?” is a different question. Looking at a calendar alone cannot answer the second.

The fact that my day is measured within the pattern of night and daylight does not, by itself, show that every context containing the same word describes the same measurement. These verses do not lead me to abandon calculation. They open a field of reflection in which I remember to ask what my calculation is actually measuring.

## 4. The Second Thousand Years: The Governance of All Matters

### Verse

> **32:5** — All  matters  are  controlled  by  Him from  the  heaven  to  the  earth.  To Him,  the  day  is  equivalent  to  one thousand of your years.

> **32:6** — Knower  of  all  secrets  and  declarations; the Almighty, Most Merciful.

### Reflection on the Verse

A thousand years appears again in 32:5. This time, however, the preceding statement concerns the control of all matters from heaven to earth, not a challenge to bring retribution. The following verse, 32:6, establishes a frame of knowledge, power, and mercy. Encountering the same number does not erase this difference in context.

In Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation, 32:5 uses “equivalent”; its Turkish translation uses “eş değerdir.” I do not report this as though it were the same wording as “like” in 22:47. The fact that both establish a day–year relationship provides a starting point for comparing their contexts. It does not eliminate every distinction.

Here I am careful not to imagine GOD as a being consulting the same kind of clock as ours, only operating more slowly. The verse does not describe His rate of aging, the gravitational field of a location, or the construction of a clock. Adding such details would not amount to an explanation; it would amount to building a model whose grounds I had not yet established.

At the same time, I cannot reduce 32:5 to an indefinite statement of magnitude. A thousand years is explicitly mentioned, and a comparison with human years is made. There is room between preserving this specificity and claiming to know the entire mechanism of the relationship. Research should continue within that space.

Before 32:6, my task is not to turn a detail I do not understand into a deficiency in GOD’s knowledge. The point at which my explanation ends is not the point at which His control ends. Rather than stopping inquiry, this thought can give it a more honest boundary.

## 5. Why Six Days Does Not Automatically Mean Six Thousand Years

### Verse

> **32:4** — GOD  is  the  One  who  created  the heavens  and  the  earth,  and  everything  between  them  in  six  days,  then assumed  all  authority.  You  have none  beside  Him  as  Lord,  nor  do you  have  an  intercessor.  Would  you not take heed?

> **32:5** — All  matters  are  controlled  by  Him from  the  heaven  to  the  earth.  To Him,  the  day  is  equivalent  to  one thousand of your years.

### Reflection on the Verse

When 32:4 and 32:5 appear consecutively, my mind may reach for a very easy operation: first six days, then a thousand years corresponding to a day; therefore six thousand years. The arithmetic is simple. But the real question is not arithmetic. It is whether the same meaning and measure can justifiably be carried from one sentence to the other.

To reach that conclusion, I would also have to show that the days concerning creation are used on precisely the same scale as the comparison in 32:5. The sentences’ proximity makes that relationship worth investigating; it does not, by itself, give a mandatory conversion instruction. Automatically replacing every occurrence of “day” with a thousand years is an even broader claim.

I therefore derive neither a necessary six-thousand-year duration of creation from these two verses nor another number to put in its place as a definitive answer. If a calculation’s premise has not been established, presenting its result as certain does not make it clearer.

There is another emphasis in 32:4 that matters to me: the account of creation does not stand apart from authority and intercession. I can become so absorbed in calculating a duration that I leave the question of whom I turn toward behind. Yet I stand before the verse not merely as an inquisitive calculator, but as a human being examining his own sources of reliance.

## 6. The Fifty-Thousand-Year Day: The Same Word, the Same Context?

### Verse

> **70:1** — A  questioner  may  question  the  inevitable retribution.

> **70:2** — For  the  disbelievers,  none  can  stop it.

> **70:3** — From  GOD;  Possessor  of  the  highest Height.

> **70:4** — The  angels,  with  their  reports,  climb to  Him  in  a  day  that  equals  fifty thousand years.

### Reflection on the Verse

The fifty thousand years in 70:4 makes my earlier easy formula come to a halt. If I have unconditionally converted every expression of a day into a thousand years, the number I encounter here calls my generalization into question. Rather than hiding the difference in the text, I need to examine which claim I made and on what basis.

70:1–4 begins with retribution and extends to the angels’ ascent. Relationships can be investigated between the challenge in 22:47, the governance in 32:5, and the ascent in 70:4. But I cannot read directly from these verses that they are three measurements of the same event, from the same observational position, under the same conditions.

Rushing to declare a contradiction and offering an easy reconciliation as though I knew every detail can share the same deficiency: I would first be treating as identical things whose identity I had not established. Where the text presents different contexts, the single clock I assume can restrict the inquiry.

Fifty thousand years does not erase a thousand years. Both expressions remain in their places. What may need to change is my desire to gather every large number into one hidden equation.

## 7. The Distance Between Seeing Something as Remote and Its Being Remote

### Verse

> **70:5** — Therefore,  you  shall  resort  to  a  gracious patience.

> **70:6** — For they see it far away.

> **70:7** — While we see it very close.

### Reflection on the Verse

Moving from 70:4 to 70:5–7, I might expect a calculation table. What I encounter instead is the relationship between patience, seeing something as distant, and seeing it as close. I take this turn after the number seriously. The passage prompts me to consider not only how long something lasts, but also how I stand before that duration.

Seeing a consequence as remote can become a reason to regard preparation as unnecessary. Thinking it near can sometimes produce thoughtless panic. What I need is not to let my sense of distance govern my decisions by itself. Before 70:5, I consider patience in terms of preserving direction without becoming consumed by the effort to force the outcome to arrive sooner.

This is not a thought to use to minimize someone else’s suffering. A person’s experience of waiting does not become insignificant because of a vast description of time that I construct from outside. Reflection on patience becomes meaningful when it disciplines my own haste; it does not carry the same sincerity when it becomes a way of silencing another person.

Before giving me a date calculation, 70:6–7 makes me feel the limitations of my perspective. Apparent distance may not be the last word. Recognizing this does not mean knowing every detail. It reminds me that I can live responsibly without knowing every detail.

## 8. The Day They Thought Had Passed, and the Three Hundred and Nine Years

### Verse

> **18:19** — When  we  resurrected  them,  they asked  each  other,  “How  long  have you  been  here?”  “We  have  been here  one  day  or  part  of  the  day,” they  answered.  “Your  Lord  knows best how  long  we  stayed here, so  let us send one of us with this money to the  city.  Let  him  fetch  the  cleanest food,  and  buy  some  for  us.  Let  him keep  a  low  profile,  and  attract  no attention.

> **18:25** — They  stayed  in  their  cave  three  hundred years, increased by nine.*

### Reflection on the Verse

18:19 and 18:25 open a different question about time. On one side are the people’s answers about how long they stayed; on the other is the duration stated in the account. Rather than treating these as two competing measurements on the same footing, I need to ask who is speaking and what they know.

A connection with 22:47 can be considered in terms of not assuming that human judgment is sufficient. But I am not saying that precisely the same mechanism is described here. The cave dwellers’ answers do not, in themselves, explain what physical clocks measured. Distinguishing remembered or estimated duration from elapsed duration does not, by itself, establish a model of motion or gravity.

Something else in 18:19 affects me: immediately after uncertainty about duration, an everyday need is addressed. It is possible to seek the right food, act carefully, and consider the task at hand without first explaining everything. Acknowledging the limits of knowledge does not bring life to a stop.

Looking back at my own past, I too can treat my impression as an exact historical account. I may have made important choices during a period that seems very short. A wait I consider long does not, by itself, tell me how I lived through it. Before these verses, I want to weigh both elapsed time and what I say about that time more carefully.

## 9. A Hundred Years Pass; the Person Says a Day

### Verse

> **2:259** — Consider the one who passed by a ghost town and wondered, “How can GOD revive this after it had died?” GOD then put him to death for a hundred years, then resurrected him. He said, “How long have you stayed here?” He said, “I have been here a day, or part of the day.” He said, “No! You have been here a hundred years. Yet, look at your food and drink; they did not spoil.  Look  at  your  donkey—we thus render you a lesson for the people. Now, note how we construct the bones, then cover them with flesh.” When he realized what had happened, he said, “Now I know that GOD is Omnipotent.”

### Reflection on the Verse

The encounter in 2:259 shows particularly clearly how a person’s own estimate of duration is corrected. Death, resurrection, food and drink, a donkey, and bones are mentioned together in the same account. I do not want to take only the difference between a day and years and leave that whole behind.

I preserve one distinction in particular: a person’s saying that a day has passed does not mean that a clock beside him is reported to have registered only one day. There is no such comparison of devices in the account. What is present is the limit of supposing that one knows an entire duration through one’s own experience.

The turn at the end of the verse is not admiration for one’s own calculation, but recognition of GOD’s power. This matters to me. If I see the event only as a puzzle about time, I may miss the change in knowledge that the encounter brings about. The person who begins by wondering ends by recognizing that his own perspective was not sufficient.

The thousand years in 22:47, the three hundred increased by nine in 18:25, and the hundred years here do not have to be combined as examples of one numerical scheme. Each has its own sentence, question, and instructive weight. A shared field of reflection can be built without erasing the differences.

## 10. When We Look Back and Count Our Lives

### Verse

> **23:112** — He  said,  “How  long  have  you  lasted on earth? How many years?”

> **23:113** — They said, “We lasted a day or part of a day. Ask those who counted.”

> **23:114** — He said, “In fact, you stayed but a brief interim, if you only knew.

### Reflection on the Verse

In 23:112–114, a question framed in years receives an answer framed in days. Reading this exchange, I consider how small the things I regard as great while living through them might later appear. But I cannot present that reflection as a calculation that converts every person’s lifetime by a fixed coefficient. No common birth date, common lifespan, or conversion table is given to me here.

The deeper question concerns the reliability of my account of the past. Remembering little of a period does not mean that I did nothing during it. I can dismiss long years in a sentence; the decisions I made in those years may still refuse to fit within that sentence.

The reference in 23:113 to those who counted is also thought-provoking. A person’s own answer and the work of counting are not held in the same place. My recollection is not the whole account. Read this way, reflection on time begins to question my unlimited confidence in my memory as well.

Before this scene, I ask myself: if I were eventually to find my life short, what would I stop postponing today? This is not a calculation of duration derived from the verse. It is a question I address to myself within the field of self-examination the passage opens.

## 11. Remembering Incorrectly Does Not Change Time

### Verse

> **30:55** — On the day when the Hour comes to pass, the guilty will swear that they lasted (in this world) only one hour. That is how wrong they were. 

> **30:56** — Those who are blessed with knowledge and faith will say, “You have lasted, according to GOD’s decree, until the Day of Resurrection. Now, this is the Day of Resurrection, but you failed to recognize it.”

### Reflection on the Verse

30:55–56 is a powerful point at which to stop: I cannot declare every perception of duration equally correct. Holding a claim intensely, even swearing to it, is not treated as sufficient for its truth. Here, a response grounded in knowledge and faith stands against the stated duration.

If I lose this distinction, the phrase “time varies from person to person” can gather very different things into a single bag. A person’s incorrectly estimating elapsed time is one claim; different durations being measured under specified conditions is another. Presenting the first as evidence for the second, or inferring from the second that all recollections are true, would be an unjustified leap.

The space these verses open is not a world in which everything is arbitrary. A human being can be mistaken, and that mistake encounters knowledge independent of the claim. I therefore cannot infer from the limitations of measurement or perception that truth disappears.

For me, this preserves the connection between investigating time and truthfulness. However convincingly I tell myself a story, I cannot assume that its persuasive force and reality are the same thing. Even my sincere belief about my past should not be closed to examination.

## 12. Not Every Large Number Establishes an Equality of Duration

### Verse

> **97:3** — The Night of Destiny is better than a thousand months. 

> **22:47** — They challenge you to bring retribution, and GOD never fails to fulfill His prophecy. A day of your Lord is like a thousand of your years.

### Reflection on the Verse

It is easy to place the thousand months in 97:3 alongside the thousand years in 22:47. But first I need to distinguish the kinds of relationship the sentences establish. In one, the comparison concerns value; in the other, a day is likened to years. Numerical resemblance does not remove that distinction.

Reading a night’s being better as meaning that the night physically lasts a thousand months would add another claim. Conversely, turning the time comparison in 22:47 into only a ranking of value would narrow its expression. Words can meet around a common subject without doing the same work.

This is an important distinction in my own life as well. Not every lengthy piece of work is valuable; an honest decision made in a short time can have a great effect. This personal observation does not replace the number in the verse. It simply reminds me why I should not equate duration with value.

If my search for time’s mystery fixes all my attention on the size of the numbers, I can lose sight of what I turn toward within time. Thinking about extending a life and thinking about living it rightly do not substitute for one another.

## 13. Knowing a Duration Does Not Make Me a Partner in Judgment

### Verse

> **18:26** — Say,  “GOD  is  the  best  knower  of how  long  they  stayed  there.”  He knows  all  secrets  in  the  heavens  and the  earth.  By  His  grace  you  can  see; by  His  grace  you  can  hear.  There is  none  beside  Him  as  Lord  and Master,  and  He  never  permits  any partners to share in His kingship.

> **32:6** — Knower  of  all  secrets  and  declarations; the Almighty, Most Merciful.

### Reflection on the Verse

In 18:26, knowledge of duration stands alongside knowledge of secrets and the emphasis on sole authority. In 32:6, too, the scope of knowledge is not limited to what I can see. Reflecting on these verses, I feel that monotheism is not a subject distant from the investigation of time.

My admiration for an explanation does not make its author the final judge of everything. Skill in calculation does not grant authority to direct another person’s religious orientation on their behalf. There is a boundary between learning something about durations and claiming a share in GOD’s knowledge and judgment.

That boundary applies to me first. I cannot make a sentence I wrote unquestionable because I worked hard on it. I cannot step in front of the verse I am reading and let my own enthusiasm dictate how it must be understood. If an interpretation of mine proves wrong, I need to remain open to correcting it rather than taking offense.

Before 18:26, I consider submission not as abandoning the effort to know, but as examining the desire for power I can build around my knowledge. I can learn more. What I learn does not make me another center of judgment beside GOD.

## 14. The Work That Is Mine While I Wait

### Verse

> **53:39** — Every human being is responsible for his own works.

> **70:5** — Therefore,  you  shall  resort  to  a  gracious patience.

### Reflection on the Verse

Reading 53:39 and 70:5 together, I cannot separate patience from responsibility. Being unable to determine the timing of the outcome is not sufficient reason to withdraw from the work that is mine to do. I cannot say that today’s choice is insignificant because I do not govern the entire calendar.

I can feel this while working on a composition, pursuing a study, or trying to correct a mistake I have made. I may not know when the result will mature; I can still attend carefully to the work before me. This is not a guarantee that a particular success will be granted. It is an acknowledgment that my effort and my claim to control the outcome are different things.

If the great comparison in 22:47 carries me away from today, perhaps I am looking for escape rather than reflection. It is possible to think about a thousand years while postponing an apology, to wonder about the duration of the universe while neglecting the truthfulness of my own words. Large questions can sometimes become hiding places from small responsibilities.

I want to take that possibility in myself seriously. In trying to carry the weight of time I do not know, I should not put down the weight of the work that belongs to me. Patience is not spending today idly. It is trying to do what is right today without controlling the whole of tomorrow.

## 15. Liking an Explanation Is Not the Same as Verifying It

### Verse

> **17:36** — You  shall  not  accept  any  information,  unless  you  verify  it  for  yourself. I  have  given  you  the  hearing,  the eyesight,  and  the  brain,  and  you  are responsible for using them.

### Reflection on the Verse

17:36 cannot function in this inquiry only as a standard I direct at others. When I make a connection between verses that appeals to me, I too am responsible for verifying it. The appearance that a conclusion serves my faith is not a reason to examine it less.

In a numerical calculation, for instance, I must first consider what is being treated as a day, how a year is defined, and which events are compared. Did I take the assumptions needed for the conclusion from the verse, or did I add them? When I find a numerical resemblance, does it really show that the relationship I propose necessarily holds?

The same care is required in rejecting an interpretation. I cannot leap from an explanation’s incompleteness to the conclusion that the subject is meaningless. Identifying an insufficient argument and saying that nothing remains to investigate are not the same attitude. Hasty acceptance stands on one side and hasty dismissal on the other; either can replace examination.

I therefore want to distinguish three levels in my own sentences: what the verse explicitly gives, the conclusion I argue for from those expressions, and the proposal that still needs testing. Before 17:36, this distinction is not a decorative caution added to the writing afterward. It is part of truthfulness.

### Reflection on the Verse

Considering 22:47, 32:5, and 70:4 together also suggests a research question for KuranTeyit. If search results display only the number in common, a reader may suppose that the three verses provide the same kind of information. A useful comparison should preserve differences while displaying the numbers.

In the workspace I imagine, each verse appears in full. Beside it is my research note: which event is being discussed, what expression establishes the comparison, and how is the duration counted by human beings mentioned? A detail not explicitly stated is left blank rather than filled with an automatic answer. A blank can mark not an error, but knowledge that is not yet complete.

Such an arrangement would help not by producing a ready-made calendar of the universe from three verses, but by making the point at which interpretation begins easier to see. When a reader changes an assumption, they can recognize how much of their conclusion depends on it. What I am considering here is a design proposal worth investigating, not the announcement of an existing feature.

For me, a good tool is not one that claims to have erased every uncertainty in my mind. It helps me distinguish where I am reading the verse, where I am making an inference, and where I do not yet know. In the investigation of time, too, responsibility remains with the researcher rather than the screen.

## 17. Arabic Word Roots: Day, Year, Measure, Counting, and Haste

### Verse

> **22:47** — They challenge you to bring retribution, and GOD never fails to fulfill His prophecy. A day of your Lord is like a thousand of your years.

> **32:5** — All  matters  are  controlled  by  Him from  the  heaven  to  the  earth.  To Him,  the  day  is  equivalent  to  one thousand of your years.

### Reflection on the Verse

Approaching the words in 22:47 and 32:5, I use root information not to produce a new translation, but to see the relationship established by the sentence more carefully. Pouring every possible root meaning into a single word is no more reliable than inserting a modern scientific term into an older word.

In 22:47, **يَوْمًا** is a form of the word for day; its root is identified as **ي و م**. In the same verse, **سَنَةٍ** is the word for year, recorded under **س ن و**. These roots help identify the words. They do not, by themselves, determine how many hours a day must contain in every context or which calendar defines a year. Those questions cannot be answered independently of the sentence’s context. The <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/wordmorphology.jsp?location=(22:47:8)" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">grammatical analysis of the word for day</a> and the <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/wordmorphology.jsp?location=(22:47:12)" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">analysis of the word for year</a> identify these forms separately.

The verb **تَعُدُّونَ** has the root **ع د د** and here concerns counting. Its second-person plural form in 22:47 directs attention to whose counting is involved. The **كـ** in **كَأَلْفِ** is not a root but a particle establishing comparison. Both the number and the way the comparison is formed therefore matter; reading a particle as a scientific coefficient is insufficient. The <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/wordmorphology.jsp?location=(22:47:14)" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">analysis of the counting verb</a> and the <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/wordbyword.jsp?chapter=22&amp;verse=47" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">word structure of 22:47</a> make this distinction traceable.

The word **مِقْدَارُهُۥ** in 32:5 is connected with **ق د ر**, within the field of quantity and measure. The presence of measurement matters, but the word itself does not name a particular equation of modern physics. A statement of measure and a theory explaining how that measure arises are different things. The <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/wordmorphology.jsp?location=(32:5:13)" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">grammatical analysis of this word</a> supports this limited linguistic observation.

Finally, **وَيَسْتَعْجِلُونَكَ**, at the beginning of 22:47, contains the root **ع ج ل**, associated here with demanding haste or acceleration. If I overlook this word, I separate the verse’s day–year comparison from the human challenge. The <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/wordmorphology.jsp?location=(22:47:1)" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">analysis of the opening verb</a> returns me to the beginning of the sentence: while seeking to understand time, am I trying to force it to conform to my demand?

## 18. Other Claims

### Verse

> **17:36** — You  shall  not  accept  any  information,  unless  you  verify  it  for  yourself. I  have  given  you  the  hearing,  the eyesight,  and  the  brain,  and  you  are responsible for using them.

> **70:4** — The  angels,  with  their  reports,  climb to  Him  in  a  day  that  equals  fifty thousand years.

### Reflection on the Verse

Before 17:36, explanations proposed concerning 22:47, 32:5, and 70:4 need to be considered without granting them the same status as the verses. Historical interpretation, physical measurement, mathematical thought experiment, and personal reflection can touch one another, but they are not the same kind of knowledge.

**Historical explanations.** <a href="https://quran.com/70%3A4/tafsirs/ar-tafsir-ibn-kathir" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Ibn Kathir’s commentary on 70:4</a> reports different approaches, including the distance of ascent, the duration of the world, and the Day of Resurrection. Their presence shows that discussion in the past was not confined to one explanation; it does not automatically establish the truth of each. A reading that associates the thousand and the fifty thousand years with different events can be considered, without supplying unreported details as certain knowledge. Likewise, treating large numbers solely as expressions of magnitude is an interpretive choice, not permission to erase the numbers from the text.

**Time dilation in physics.** Experimental findings must be distinguished from interpretation of verses. The possibility that clocks under conditions of relative motion or different gravitational conditions measure different durations is experimentally investigated in modern physics. <a href="https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2010/09/nist-pair-aluminum-atomic-clocks-reveal-einsteins-relativity-personal-scale" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Experiments reported by NIST in 2010</a> investigated small differences in speed and height; the <a href="https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2022/02/jila-atomic-clocks-measure-einsteins-general-relativity-millimeter-scale" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NIST/JILA work reported in 2022</a> examined a clock-frequency difference associated with a millimeter-scale difference in height. This is not the same as a wait feeling long. Nevertheless, these experiments did not test which physical mechanism the thousand or fifty thousand years in the verses describes.

**A day–thousand years example with explicit assumptions.** In special relativity, neglecting gravitational effects, a clock moving at constant speed relative to an inertial frame measures its own interval `Δτ` between two events. That interval is related to the frame’s interval `Δt` by `Δt = γΔτ`, where `γ = 1 / √(1 − v²/c²)`. <a href="https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/5-3-time-dilation" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">OpenStax, University Physics, “Time Dilation”</a> explains this relationship and the conditions under which the measurements are compared.

If, solely for this example, I take a year to be 365.25 days, the ratio required for one day on the moving clock to correspond to a thousand years in the comparison frame is **365,250**, not **1,000**. Substitution gives `v/c ≈ 0.999999999996252`. This is a mathematical calculation under chosen assumptions, not a demonstration of a practicable journey or an explanation of the verse. Changing the definition of a year changes the numerical example. I cannot carry this result into the verses without separately establishing that they describe two such physical clocks.

The reciprocal measurement relationship in relative motion, and the travel histories of clocks that reunite, also matter. Not every difference can be explained by saying that time is slow somewhere. The <a href="https://www.einstein-online.info/en/spotlight/light-clocks-time-dilation/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Max Planck Einstein Online explanation</a> distinguishes these issues. Placing GOD in the formula as one of its moving observers would attribute physical properties to Him that the verses do not provide.

**Calendars, the speed of light, and date calculations.** The footnote accompanying 18:25 offers a calendar explanation through solar and lunar years. This is an interpretation separate from the verse’s text. Numerical conversion between calendars is not identical to physical clocks measuring different durations. Moreover, a ratio of durations alone does not yield a speed; a distance is also needed. If a particular lunar orbit, a chosen day length, or another distance is added to 32:5 for a calculation, every added assumption requires its own justification. Likewise, the numbers in these three verses do not, by themselves, produce a calendar starting point or a necessary date for the end of the world.

Among my earlier writings, my reflection on <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/the_impatient_human_being_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">human impatience</a>, my discussion of <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/had-the-retribution-come-immediately-en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">retribution not coming immediately and the time we are granted</a>, and my article on <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/finding-is-one-thing-understanding-is-another-verses-in-the-age-of-search-engines/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">the difference between finding and understanding</a> raise neighboring questions. They, too, are my investigations; they do not replace the verses read here. The existence of an earlier thought of mine does not guarantee that my new comparison is correct.

## 19. Conclusion: Not Losing Today While Thinking About a Thousand Years

### Verse

> **103:1** — By the afternoon. 

> **103:2** — The human being is utterly lost. 

> **103:3** — Except  those  who  believe  and  lead a  righteous  life,  and  exhort  one another  to  uphold  the  truth,  and  exhort one another to be steadfast.

### Reflection on the Verse

How can a day correspond to a thousand years? The answer I reach through the verses is not to enclose a large number within a single formula. The comparison in 22:47 should not be severed from the challenge to bring retribution; the expression in 32:5 should not be severed from the control of all matters; and the fifty thousand years in 70:4 should not be severed from the angels’ ascent. Shared words about time do not eliminate these differences.

18:19 and 18:25, 2:259, 23:112–114, and 30:55–56 also open a field of thought in which I should not easily identify what a person says about duration with what actually occurred. Meanwhile, 10:5 and 17:12 preserve the value of counting and calculation. I am therefore compelled neither to reduce time to an illusion nor to fit all reality into my own calendar.

These verses give me comparisons concerning time. I do not derive from them permission to claim that I have explained every detail of their physical mechanism. Inquiry does not end here. Its questions become more precise: which duration, which event, which comparison, and which boundary of knowledge?

Before 103:1–3, I want this investigation to return to my own life. The lifetime whose length I discuss is also the lifetime in which I do right or wrong. Upholding truth and remaining steadfast require more than contemplating the immensity of time.

For me, the weight of monotheism here is that my clock must not judge in GOD’s place. The weight of submission is that I must not evade my own work because I cannot know every duration. The weight of truthfulness is that I preserve the boundary between an exciting possibility and what I know.

I can continue investigating how a thousand years corresponds to a day. But I cannot lose the day entrusted to me by hiding behind that question.
