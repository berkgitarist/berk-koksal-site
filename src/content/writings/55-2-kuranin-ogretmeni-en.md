# [55:2] Who Is the Teacher of the Quran? Who Teaches the Quran?

*A Nineteen-Part Reflection on Quran 55:2*

**Author:** Berk KÖKSAL

**Date:** October 11, 2026

**Location:** Mersin

### Verse

> **55:2**
>
> عَلَّمَ ٱلْقُرْءَانَ
>
> *Teacher of the Quran.*
>
> Kuran’ın Öğretmeni.
>
> Source: <a href="https://kuranteyit.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">kuranteyit.com</a>

## Introduction

**Greetings. Peace be upon you.**

When I ask, “Who teaches the Quran?”, the first voice I imagine may belong to another human being: someone reciting, explaining, translating, or researching. But 55:2 begins the question somewhere else. I do not want to pass over its brevity. I want to read it alongside the surrounding verses and the passages about knowledge, explanation, testimony, and responsibility.

The purpose of this reflection is to consider 55:2 together with related verses and to ask what bearing witness to the oneness of GOD means in the life of a person who wants to learn truthfully, act justly, speak accurately, and submit. I am not trying to set human acts of teaching against GOD's role as Teacher. I am trying to understand the boundary between them. For I can acquire many references and still lose my place as a student.

## 1. The Teacher of the Quran: The First Answer

### Verse

> **55:2** — Teacher of the Quran.

### Reflection on the Verse

When I read 55:2 on its own, a remarkably short sentence holds me longer than I expect. Read with 55:1, the answer is direct: GOD is the Teacher of the Quran. It does not lay out a teaching technique. It identifies a teacher. Before I answer the question with the name of a scholar, a school, or even myself, I have to notice where the verse directs my attention.

This does not mean human beings cannot teach one another. The verse does not say that. It does, however, require me to distinguish the source of instruction from the people who help me receive it. Someone may read, explain, translate, or patiently clarify a passage. I can be grateful for that effort without confusing the servant with the source of what is taught.

For me, the first question is therefore not which method will make me knowledgeable fastest. It is whom I recognize as the ultimate teacher. If years of study make me more certain of my own superiority than attentive to the Quran, something in my position has shifted. This is a personal reckoning prompted by 55:2, not a new ruling placed inside it.

**Verse references:** 55:2, 55:1.

## 2. Grace, Creation, and the Gift of Discernment

### Verse

> **55:1** — The Most Gracious.

> **55:3** — Creator of the human beings.

> **55:4** — He taught them how to distinguish.

### Reflection on the Verse

Reading around 55:2 makes the answer harder to misunderstand. The passage begins with the Most Gracious, then speaks of teaching the Quran, creating human beings, and teaching them how to distinguish. These statements belong together. I cannot reduce instruction here to the transfer of information from one mind to another. Human existence and the capacity to discern are placed alongside the teaching itself.

The word “distinguish” is what stays with me. What use is a collection of memorized expressions if I cannot distinguish my preference from what the text says, or a careful explanation from an attractive assertion? Verse 55:4 does not promise that every person will be infallible in every judgment. That would be more than it says. But it gives me reason to view discernment as a responsibility, not a decoration.

To recognize GOD's grace is not to despise human effort. It is to prevent effort from turning into self-worship. I can work long hours to understand one sentence and still be thankful that understanding is possible at all. The sequence of 55:1-4 calls me back to that gratitude whenever achievement begins to sound like ownership.

**Verse references:** 55:1, 55:3, 55:4.

## 3. A Life That Begins Without Knowledge

### Verse

> **16:78** — GOD brought you out of your mothers’ bellies knowing nothing, and He gave you the hearing, the eyesight, and the brains, that you may be appreciative.

> **96:4** — Teaches by means of the pen. 

> **96:5** — He teaches man what he never knew. 

### Reflection on the Verse

Knowledge is not the point from which human life begins. Verse 16:78 takes me back to the condition I tend to forget once I have learned a great deal. I came into the world knowing nothing. Hearing, eyesight, and brains are mentioned in relation to appreciation, not entitlement.

Verses 96:4-5 bring the pen into the picture. I think of the pen as a way to make a thought visible and available for another examination. In my own work, writing something down often exposes the gap between being impressed by an idea and genuinely understanding it. A conclusion can seem perfectly clear in my head until I try to explain why it follows from what I have read.

None of this means that every written statement is true. A pen can record an error as easily as a discovery, and the mind can mistake confidence for knowledge. The tools are gifts, but their possession is not proof of their correct use. Sometimes the first step toward learning is not acquiring another fact. It is remembering how little I knew at the beginning, and allowing that memory to soften the pride I attach to what I know now.

**Verse references:** 16:78, 96:4, 96:5.

## 4. The Names Taught to Adam and the Humility of Knowledge

### Verse

> **2:31** — He  taught  Adam  all  the  names* then presented  them to the angels, saying, “Give me the names of these, if you are right.”

> **2:32** — They  said,  “Be  You  glorified,  we have  no  knowledge,  except  that which  You  have  taught  us.  You  are the Omniscient, Most Wise.”

### Reflection on the Verse

The teaching of names to Adam in 2:31 prevents me from imagining knowledge as territory conquered by human beings alone. Someone learns; someone teaches. Then comes the answer in 2:32. What moves me is not only an admission of limited knowledge but the absence of shame in admitting it.

I think of my own research. There is real joy in recognizing a connection or understanding a sentence that resisted me for weeks. Yet rejoicing in discovery is one thing; claiming exclusive ownership of understanding is another. These verses give me no license to rule over another person's mind because I have learned something.

In my earlier reflection, [The Teaching of Names](https://berkkoksal.com/writings/the_teaching_of_names_en/), I asked whether naming a thing is the same as knowing it. Here I ask a different question: What kind of person does knowledge make me? Can I say I do not know? Can I revise a cherished explanation without feeling diminished? I hear in 2:31-32 an invitation to be serious about learning and equally serious about its limits. Receiving knowledge is not the same as becoming its independent source.

**Verse references:** 2:31, 2:32.

## 5. The Collection and Explanation of the Quran

### Verse

> **75:16** — Do  not  move  your  tongue  to  hasten it.

> **75:17** — It  is  we  who  will  collect  it  into Quran.

> **75:18** — Once  we  recite  it,  you  shall  follow such a Quran.

> **75:19** — Then it is we who will explain it.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verses 75:16-19 challenge my haste. The warning about rushing is placed alongside statements about the Quran being collected, recited, followed, and explained. If I take 55:2 seriously, I cannot ignore what 75:19 says about the source of explanation.

Yet it would be careless to conclude that these verses forbid every human attempt to discuss a verse. They do not say that. The distinction is more demanding: GOD's explanation cannot be placed on the same level as mine. My explanation may be incomplete. I may have missed a connection or overlooked a word. Another verse may force me to reconsider an interpretation I was proud of. If I cannot allow that possibility, I may be defending my voice rather than attending to the Quran.

Haste is not always a matter of speaking too quickly. Sometimes it is the desire to publish a conclusion before the question has had time to trouble me. Sometimes I mistake the satisfaction of an answer for the discipline of understanding. In front of 75:16-19, I want to listen, follow, and think before I speak. When I do speak, I want to make the limits of my words visible.

**Verse references:** 75:16, 75:17, 75:18, 75:19.

## 6. Learning Made Accessible, and the Discipline of Listening

### Verse

> **54:17** — We made the Quran easy to learn. Does any of you wish to learn?

> **7:204** — When  the  Quran  is  recited,  you  shall listen  to  it  and  take  heed,  that  you may attain mercy.

### Reflection on the Verse

The question at the end of 54:17 gives me both hope and responsibility. Making the Quran easy to learn does not remove the need to want to learn it. A text can be on my screen, beside my bed, or in my hearing without becoming something I have truly attended to.

Verse 7:204 joins listening with taking heed. Listening may be much more active than remaining silent. Sometimes the hardest thing is not forming my rebuttal before a sentence has finished. I notice how often I fail at this even in ordinary conversation. What makes me think I can approach the Quran with less care?

When I work on a piece of music, listening to the same passage several times can reveal relationships that the first hearing concealed. Music does not explain the Quran, and I would not ask it to. It simply reminds me, from another part of my life, how attention changes what I notice. I want to bring that patience to reading: the placement of a word, the surrounding verses, the resistance of my own assumptions. The open invitation of 54:17 matters. So does my answer to it.

**Verse references:** 54:17, 7:204.

## 7. The Distance Between Reading and Careful Study

### Verse

> **47:24** — Why do they not study the Quran carefully? Do they have locks on their minds?

> **38:29** — This is a scripture that we sent down to you, that is sacred—perhaps they reflect on its verses. Those who possess intelligence will take heed.

### Reflection on the Verse

The question in 47:24 unsettles me because it is easy to say I have read something. It is harder to say I have examined it carefully. Familiarity is not understanding. Verse 38:29 places reflection upon the verses at the heart of how the scripture is approached.

When a verse appears to disturb a conclusion I already hold, do I quietly pass it by? Or do I return, compare, and allow the possibility that my conclusion needs repair? Perhaps some of the locks I encounter are not caused by lack of access to information but by my reluctance to be wrong. That is my reflection; the verse does not identify every possible cause of a locked mind.

In [Finding Is One Thing, Understanding Is Another](https://berkkoksal.com/writings/finding-is-one-thing-understanding-is-another-verses-in-the-age-of-search-engines/), I wrote about the distance between locating a verse and giving it serious attention. Reading 55:2 gives that concern a new weight. Before a book taught by GOD, success cannot consist only in finding the right reference quickly. Sometimes the most responsible act is to stay with a difficult question and read again.

**Verse references:** 47:24, 38:29.

## 8. Verification Is the Cost of Seeking Truth

### Verse

> **17:36** — You  shall  not  accept  any  information,  unless  you  verify  it  for  yourself. I  have  given  you  the  hearing,  the eyesight,  and  the  brain,  and  you  are responsible for using them.

> **39:18** — They  are  the  ones  who  examine  all words,  then  follow  the  best.  These are  the  ones  whom  GOD  has  guided;  these  are  the  ones  who  possess intelligence.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 17:36 places responsibility on me before I accept information. Verse 39:18 connects examining words with following what is best. Read together, they prevent me from treating investigation and submission as enemies. Verification is not necessarily an endless refusal to trust. It is the willingness to see what my trust rests upon.

That responsibility also concerns how I name the texts I use. In my faith, **Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation** has a defined place. When I discuss the Turkish wording, I call it **the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation**. Those are not interchangeable descriptions. Precision about a text's status is part of the honesty with which I want to handle it.

To verify does not mean living in constant suspicion of other people. It begins with accountability for my own words. If I transcribe a verse incorrectly, I should correct it. If a word is unclear to me, I should not pretend that certainty has already arrived. I return to a question that is more uncomfortable than checking someone else's claim: Do I examine my own confident statements by the same standard?

**Verse references:** 17:36, 39:18.

## 9. The Messenger Teaches Without Replacing the Source

### Verse

> **2:151** — (Blessings)  such  as  the  sending  of  a messenger  from  among  you  to  recite our  revelations  to  you,  purify  you, teach  you  the  scripture  and  wisdom, and  to  teach  you  what  you  never knew.

> **62:2** — He is the One who sent to the gentiles  a  messenger  from  among  them, to  recite  to  them  His  revelations, purify  them,  and  teach  them  the scripture  and  wisdom.  Before  this, they had gone far astray.

> **42:52** — Thus, we inspired to you a revelation proclaiming our commandments. You had no idea about the scripture, or faith. Yet, we made this a beacon to guide whomever we choose from among our servants. Surely, you guide in a straight path.

### Reflection on the Verse

An important balance appears here. Verse 55:2 directs attention to the source of teaching, while 2:151 and 62:2 describe a messenger reciting revelations and teaching scripture and wisdom. Therefore, “GOD teaches” cannot honestly be used to erase the human role these verses explicitly describe. The source and the bearer must not be confused, but neither can be dismissed from the passage.

What catches my attention in 42:52 is that revelation and guidance are not portrayed as knowledge the messenger generated independently. Inspiration is given to him. The messenger's activity is real; its origin is not made his private possession.

A person can teach another person what they have read. They can help someone notice a connection, ask a better question, or remain patient with a difficult passage. That work can be valuable. Yet the moment a human explanation is presented as though it were itself an untouchable revelation, the boundary between service and authority becomes uncertain. I cannot extract a complete institutional model from these verses. I can recognize a duty to teach carefully without climbing above the message.

**Verse references:** 2:151, 62:2, 42:52.

## 10. Inviting with Wisdom Is Not an Act of Coercion

### Verse

> **16:125** — You shall invite to the path of your Lord  with  wisdom  and  kind  enlightenment, and debate with them in the best possible manner. Your Lord knows best who has strayed from His path, and He knows best who are the guided ones.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 16:125 turns my attention from the ethics of learning to the ethics of teaching. If someone does not arrive at my conclusion, that does not give me permission to mistreat them. Wisdom, kind enlightenment, and the best manner of debate appear together. Those are demanding conditions for anyone who believes they have something important to say.

A reader may question my explanation. They may find an argument unconvincing, or notice a weakness I have missed. At that moment I can try to understand the question, or I can try to end the conversation by force of confidence. The second option might feel powerful, but it resembles the instruction of 16:125 far less.

The final part of the verse also places a limit on my judgments. The Lord knows best who has strayed and who is guided. Winning an argument is not the same as seeing another person's inner condition. If I want to teach, I must examine the manner in which I teach. Raising my voice does not make an uncertain claim true. A gentler voice cannot repair a false claim either. Both content and conduct matter.

**Verse references:** 16:125.

## 11. The Freedom to Ask, and the Limits of the One Who Answers

### Verse

> **21:7** — We  did  not  send  before  you  except men  whom  we  inspired.  Ask  those who  know  the  scripture,  if  you  do not know.

> **20:114** — Most  Exalted  is  GOD,  the  only true  King.  Do  not  rush  into  uttering the  Quran  before  it  is  revealed  to you,  and  say,  “My  Lord,  increase  my knowledge.”

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 21:7 tells those who do not know to ask those who know the scripture. I cannot read that and declare all human teaching unnecessary. Seeking help from someone with experience is a recognizable part of learning. But consulting someone is different from transferring the whole responsibility of faith to that person's answer.

Then 20:114 gives me a prayer that has no graduation date. Do I still want my knowledge to increase, or have I begun to see every new question as an attack on what I already know? After defending a view for years, even the smallest correction may feel threatening. The verse reminds me of a posture I do not want to lose.

A good teacher, as I understand this pair of verses, need not silence questions to preserve authority. A good student need not reject every human explanation to protect independence. One can receive help and still do the work of understanding. Neither verse tells me that a particular human being is incapable of error. Their combined invitation is more difficult: ask when I do not know, and remain capable of learning after I receive an answer.

**Verse references:** 21:7, 20:114.

## 12. Knowledge, Oneness, and the Weight of Testimony

### Verse

> **3:18** — GOD  bears  witness  that  there  is  no god except He, and so do the angels and  those  who  possess  knowledge. Truthfully  and  equitably,  He  is  the absolute  god;  there  is  no  god  but He, the Almighty, Most Wise.

> **6:19** — Say,  “Whose  testimony  is  the greatest?” Say, “GOD’s. He is the witness between me and you that this Quran* has been inspired to me, to preach it to you and whomever it reaches. Indeed, you bear witness that there are other gods* beside GOD.” Say, “I do not testify as you do; there is only one god, and I disown your idolatry.”

### Reflection on the Verse

In 3:18, those who possess knowledge are mentioned among those who bear witness to GOD's oneness. That gives testimony more weight than a slogan I can repeat without thinking. Truth, equity, power, and wisdom appear in the same verse. It is difficult for me to treat knowledge as morally neutral in that company.

Verse 6:19 places the greatest testimony with GOD. That becomes a standard against which I must examine my own declarations. “I believe this” and “GOD says this” are not identical statements. The first describes my conviction. The second must be tested against the actual words of the verse. Sincerity does not turn my interpretation into revelation.

For me, oneness is not merely a number in a confession. It concerns where I place final authority. If my favorite explanation, my reputation, or the person I admire becomes immune to the Quran's scrutiny, I may be multiplying authorities while speaking of only one GOD. I do not raise this question in order to accuse people easily. I begin with myself: How many masters am I allowing into my thinking while declaring that GOD is one?

**Verse references:** 3:18, 6:19.

## 13. When Knowledge Is Tested by Justice

### Verse

> **55:7** — He  constructed  the  sky  and  established the law.

> **55:8** — You shall not transgress the law. 

> **55:9** — You  shall  establish  justice;  do  not violate the law.

> **5:8** — O  you  who  believe,  you  shall  be  absolutely  equitable,  and  observe GOD,  when  you  serve  as  witnesses. Do  not  be  provoked  by  your  conflicts  with  some  people  into  committing  injustice.  You  shall  be  absolutely  equitable,  for  it  is  more  righteous. You  shall  observe  GOD.  GOD  is fully  Cognizant  of  everything  you do.

### Reflection on the Verse

I find it significant that the same sura that speaks of the Teacher of the Quran also turns toward law, measure, and justice. Verses 55:7-9 put the question of right order before me. Verse 5:8 reaches into the harder territory of conflict: even disagreement must not become an excuse for injustice.

I recognize a very personal danger here. Do I excuse an error when it comes from someone I admire and magnify it when it comes from someone I dislike? Do I demand precision from others while giving myself permission to be vague? If I do, what has my accumulation of verses actually changed in me?

Justice is not only something that happens in a courtroom. It can begin in how I reproduce an opponent's argument, acknowledge a correction, or refuse to use a person's mistake as entertainment. These examples are my applications, not additional commandments claimed to be inside the verses. The command to uphold justice remains the measure. If I speak of GOD as the teacher, I should also ask whether learning has made me more equitable toward the people with whom I disagree.

**Verse references:** 55:7, 55:8, 55:9, 5:8.

## 14. Truthful Speech: Learning Becomes a Responsibility

### Verse

> **33:70** — O  you  who  believe,  reverence  GOD and  utter  only  the  correct  utterances.

> **49:6** — O you who believe, if a wicked person brings any news to you, you shall first investigate, lest you commit injustice towards some people, out of ignorance, then become sorry and remorseful for what you have done.

### Reflection on the Verse

Read beside 55:2, verse 33:70 makes my speech part of the test of learning. If I am going to pass on what I think I understand, being eloquent is not enough. I have to care whether I am correct. Verse 49:6 warns of the injustice that may follow when news is acted upon without investigation. Truthful speech and justice cannot be pulled apart so easily.

In a matter of faith, a mistaken sentence can travel further than I intended. Someone may trust it, repeat it, or make a decision because of it. That changes how I think about an article title, a public comment, or a verse shared without context. It is not difficult to write something forceful. It takes more discipline to write something fair.

If I am unsure, saying so is not weakness. If I discover an error, correcting it is not humiliation. The connection to 49:6 is my application to the circulation of claims; I am not pretending the verse names modern media. What it does confront is the danger of ignorance producing injustice. I want the words that leave my mouth to be no more confident than the knowledge behind them.

**Verse references:** 33:70, 49:6.

## 15. When Does a Human Teacher Cross the Boundary?

### Verse

> **3:79** — Never would a human being whom GOD blessed with the scripture and prophethood say to the people, “Idolize me beside GOD.” Instead, (he would say), “Devote yourselves absolutely to your Lord alone,” according to the scripture you preach and the teachings you learn.

> **3:80** — Nor would he command you to idolize the angels and the prophets as lords. Would he exhort you to disbelieve after becoming submitters? 

### Reflection on the Verse

Verses 3:79-80 force me to examine the boundary of a human teacher. Even a person blessed with scripture and prophethood is not portrayed as inviting people to idolize him. Knowledge, office, closeness, and public respect are not grounds for taking a place beside GOD.

I find myself asking what success looks like for someone who teaches. Do students become dependent on repeating the teacher's voice? Or do they become more capable of reading, asking, and testing what they hear against the scripture? Those questions are my applications of the principle, not extra words attributed to the verses.

If I want to help people, I should not secretly need them to remain dependent on me. If someone comes to a sounder understanding than I have, can I be glad for them? Pride in knowledge can make another person's growth feel like a threat. The direction of 3:79-80 is different: devotion belongs to the Lord. The best help I can imagine gives the learner more attention to the source, not more fear of disappointing the person who taught them.

**Verse references:** 3:79, 3:80.

## 16. The Danger of Making Religious Leaders into Lords

### Verse

> **9:31** — They  have  set  up  their  religious leaders  and  scholars  as  lords,*  instead of GOD. Others deified the Messiah, son of Mary. They were all commanded  to  worship  only  one god. There is no god except He. Be He glorified, high above having any partners.

> **39:3** — Absolutely,  the  religion  shall  be devoted to GOD alone. Those who set up idols beside Him say, “We idolize them only to bring us closer to GOD; for they are in a better position!” GOD will judge them regarding their disputes. GOD does not guide such liars, disbelievers.

### Reflection on the Verse

It would be easy to read 9:31 and immediately begin listing other people's failures. I find it more demanding to turn the warning toward myself. Listening to someone is not identical to making that person a lord. Respecting expertise is not the same as granting a human voice the final authority that belongs to GOD.

Verse 39:3 intensifies the concern. The appeal to drawing nearer to GOD shows how an apparently devoted intention can accompany a deeply confused direction. Still, neither verse gives me permission to announce that every scholar, teacher, or community is guilty of the same thing. That would go beyond their specific warning.

The danger I recognize is quieter. What happens when an explanation from a person I admire appears to conflict with the wording of a verse? Which one do I protect? Have I made a claim immune to examination because of the reputation attached to it? I want to remain capable of gratitude toward people who teach me, without allowing gratitude to become worship. These verses make that distinction spiritually urgent.

**Verse references:** 9:31, 39:3.

## 17. Truth, Justice, and the Source of Judgment

### Verse

> **6:114** — Shall I seek other than GOD as a source of law, when He has revealed to you this book fully detailed?* Those who received the scripture recognize that it has been revealed from  your  Lord,  truthfully.  You shall not harbor any doubt.

> **6:115** — The  word  of  your  Lord  is  complete,* in truth and justice. Nothing shall abrogate His words. He is the Hearer, the Omniscient.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verses 6:114-115 speak of a fully detailed book, truth, justice, and the word of GOD. Before statements that weighty, I have to examine my habits as an interpreter. The distinction between divine words and my explanation is not merely polite. It is a matter of accuracy. Which sentence belongs to the text? Which connection have I proposed? Which claim is still uncertain?

In research, an attractive pattern can feel convincing before it has been examined carefully. Liking a conclusion does not strengthen its evidence. The joining of truth and justice in 6:115 makes me think about the moral side of selective reading. Keeping the details that support my argument while hiding those that trouble it would be unfair, even if I were outwardly speaking in defense of the Quran.

Nor does accepting the completeness of the scripture remove my responsibility to understand it. A complete text can still be misread by an incomplete reader. Admitting that possibility does not diminish the scripture. It places my own words where they belong. If GOD is the teacher, intellectual honesty requires me to keep His word distinguishable from my work upon it.

**Verse references:** 6:114, 6:115.

## 18. Does Guidance Appear in Reading or in Living?

### Verse

> **17:9** — This Quran guides to the best path, and  brings  good  news  to  the  believers who lead a righteous life, that they have deserved a great  recompense.

> **29:69** — As for those who strive in our cause, we will surely guide them in our paths. Most assuredly, GOD is with the pious.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 17:9 speaks of the Quran guiding to the best path and of believers who lead a righteous life. That connection refuses to let me treat learning as a private collection of answers. Being moved by a verse is one thing. Looking for the consequences in how I live is another.

In 29:69, striving and guidance appear together. I do not take this to mean that enough effort guarantees the interpretation I already want. The verse does not promise that kind of personal victory. Yet I cannot call indifference submission. Studying, correcting my errors, trying to be just, and speaking truthfully are forms of effort that reach beyond the page.

Sometimes I think I have made great progress in an investigation, and an ordinary conversation reveals how far I still have to go. If I react unfairly to another person, what has knowing a correct reference accomplished in me? If defending a claim makes me cruel, where has knowledge led me? These verses invite me to consider the value of study not only in terms of what I can explain, but in terms of the person I am becoming while I study.

**Verse references:** 17:9, 29:69.

## 19. Remaining a Student: Asking for More Knowledge

### Verse

> **20:114** — Most  Exalted  is  GOD,  the  only true  King.  Do  not  rush  into  uttering the  Quran  before  it  is  revealed  to you,  and  say,  “My  Lord,  increase  my knowledge.”

> **7:204** — When  the  Quran  is  recited,  you  shall listen  to  it  and  take  heed,  that  you may attain mercy.

### Reflection on the Verse

I want to return to 20:114 at the end of these reflections. Asking who teaches the Quran leads me toward an answer, but it does not finish the question of how I should learn. To ask for more knowledge is to acknowledge that my education is unfinished. There may be something I do not understand today that will remain difficult tomorrow. That is not a reason to abandon effort. It is a reason to practice patience.

Verse 7:204 calls me again to listen and take heed. At the beginning, I considered the identity of the teacher. Here I consider the posture of the student. Can I put down a cherished assumption? When a verse troubles me, do I avoid it? Am I willing to let what I hear change my conduct?

I do not need a grand title for myself. I need a sound direction. If increasing knowledge does not make me more attentive before GOD, more equitable toward people, and more careful with words, what exactly have I increased? The question opened by 55:2 is not exhausted by a single article. Perhaps remaining a student begins precisely here: recognizing the Teacher, and then learning to listen again.

**Verse references:** 20:114, 7:204.

### On the Arabic Roots of the Words

The verb **عَلَّمَ** (*ʿallama*) in 55:2 belongs to the **ع-ل-م** (*ʿ-l-m*) root family associated with knowing and teaching. The same family helps me attend to the relationship between teaching and the limits of knowledge in 2:31-32. **ٱلْقُرْءَانَ** (*al-Qurʾān*) is related to the **ق-ر-أ** (*q-r-ʾ*) root family of reading or reciting. Teaching and the recited text stand beside each other in this compact verse.

The idea of distinguishing in 55:4 also brings the **ب-ي-ن** (*b-y-n*) family into view; I keep it in mind when considering explanation in 75:19. In the call to careful study in 47:24, the **د-ب-ر** (*d-b-r*) family is also worth noticing. Root relationships are aids to reflection, not a license to import every possible dictionary meaning into a verse. I do not want a clever word study to outrank the text it is meant to illuminate. **Verse references:** 55:2, 55:4, 2:31-32, 75:19, 47:24.

## Other Claims

Different approaches can be brought to 55:2. Some readers may understand the divine teaching described here in a way that makes every human attempt to teach or explain seem unnecessary. Others may emphasize the teaching work of messengers and knowledgeable people so strongly that the divine source recedes into the background. The first approach needs to account for 2:151 and 62:2; the second for 55:2 and 75:19.

One may also encounter attempts to build a detailed chronology of the Quran's existence in relation to human creation from the order of 55:1-4. I do not think this short sequence, by itself, settles such a chronology. Likewise, traditional approaches that grant a scholar or institution the status of a final, unchallengeable authority can be questioned in light of 9:31 and 3:79-80. That question should not be turned into a blanket accusation against everyone who teaches.

I do not make any of these approaches the foundation of the reflection. A claim still needs to be weighed against the wording of the verses, and a reader should be honest about where interpretation begins.

## Conclusion

“Who is the Teacher of the Quran?” is no longer, for me, merely a question with a two-word answer. In 55:1-4 I see the identity of the Teacher alongside creation and the human capacity to distinguish. Verses 75:16-19 place a limit on the authority I claim for my own explanations. Verses 2:151 and 62:2 acknowledge that a human being can recite and teach without becoming the origin of the revelation.

That is where the test begins. Am I willing to examine carefully, as 47:24 challenges me to do? Do I apply the responsibility of 17:36 to myself, not only to others? Does my testimony about oneness make me more equitable and more truthful, as 3:18 causes me to consider? When I read the warning of 9:31, am I willing to examine even the voices I love and trust?

To recognize GOD as the Teacher does not make human effort unnecessary. It gives that effort a more demanding direction: listen, think, verify, speak carefully, act justly, and admit the boundaries of what I know. I can thank a person who has helped me understand a passage without giving that person the place of its source.

Above all, I want to keep my own words open to correction and the Quran free from being concealed behind my voice. Let my knowledge increase without persuading me that knowledge belongs to me. Let me remain a student before GOD, the Teacher of the Quran.
