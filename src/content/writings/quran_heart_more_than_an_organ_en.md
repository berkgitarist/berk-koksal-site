## 1. The Heart in My Chest, the Question in My Life

### Verse

**22:46**

**Arabic**

<blockquote data-verse="22:46" data-language="ar">
<p lang="ar" dir="rtl">أَفَلَمْ يَسِيرُوا۟ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ فَتَكُونَ لَهُمْ قُلُوبٌ يَعْقِلُونَ بِهَآ أَوْ ءَاذَانٌ يَسْمَعُونَ بِهَا فَإِنَّهَا لَا تَعْمَى ٱلْأَبْصَٰرُ وَلَٰكِن تَعْمَى ٱلْقُلُوبُ ٱلَّتِى فِى ٱلصُّدُورِ</p>
</blockquote>

**English — Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation**

<blockquote data-verse="22:46" data-language="en">
<p lang="en">Did they not roam the earth, then use their minds to understand, and use their ears to hear? Indeed, the real blindness is not the blindness of the eyes, but the blindness of the hearts inside the chests.</p>
</blockquote>

**Turkish — the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation**

<blockquote data-verse="22:46" data-language="tr">
<p lang="tr">Yeryüzünü dolaşmadılar mı, sonrasında anlamak için akıllarını ve işitmek için kulaklarını kullanmadılar mı? Doğrusu, gerçek körlük gözlerin körlüğü değil, ancak göğüslerin içindeki kalplerin körlüğüdür.</p>
</blockquote>

Source: <a href="https://kuranteyit.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">kuranteyit.com</a>

<blockquote data-verse="22:45" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>22:45</strong> — Many a community have we annihilated because of their wickedness. They ended up with ruins, stilled wells, and great empty mansions.</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

Can a person notice every detail and still fail to understand what is happening? Can someone recognize how a falsehood is constructed, yet defend it because admitting the truth would threaten an interest? These are the questions that first hold my attention when I consider the heart. Before asking what mechanism operates inside my chest, I need to ask how far an unwelcome truth is allowed to enter my life.

22:46 prevents me from treating the ability to see and the willingness to heed reality as the same achievement. Read alongside the ruined communities of 22:45, its invitation to travel is about more than changing location. A consequence may stand directly before me while I protect myself from the question it raises about my own conduct. Seeing, in this sense, is not yet facing what I have seen.

My first response to the title is therefore this: **What the QURAN says about the heart is not confined to describing an organ.** Understanding, attentiveness, and openness to truth belong to this language as well. That does not mean I have extracted a medical explanation of how cardiac muscle produces thought. The verse does not describe such a biological mechanism.

This limit does not diminish my curiosity. It turns the inquiry back toward me. Discussing what the heart is can be easier than examining why my own heart closes against certain truths. Standing before this verse, I feel the weight of the second question first. As the person whose heart continues to beat, what am I doing with what I encounter?

**Verse references:** 22:46; 22:45.

## 2. Not Forcing Every Occurrence into the Same Narrow Meaning

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="33:4" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>33:4</strong> — GOD  did  not  give  any  man  two hearts  in  his  chest.  Nor  did  He  turn your  wives  whom  you  estrange  (according  to  your  custom)  into  your mothers.*  Nor  did  He  turn  your adopted  children  into  genetic  offspring.  All  these  are  mere  utterances  that  you  have  invented.  GOD speaks  the  truth,  and  He  guides  in the (right) path.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="7:179" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>7:179</strong> — We have committed to Hell multitudes of jinns and humans. They have minds with which they do not understand, eyes with which they do not see, and ears with which they do not hear. They are like animals; no, they  are  far  worse—they  are  totally unaware.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="47:24" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>47:24</strong> — Why do they not study the Quran carefully? Do they have locks on their minds?</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

In 33:4, the statement about two hearts in a man's chest appears alongside claims people make about family relationships. I cannot ignore that usage and declare that every mention of the heart refers to something wholly detached from the body. Nor can I reduce the failure to understand in 7:179, or the failure to study in 47:24, to an organ's operation alone. I have to attend to what each sentence is doing.

Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation uses “minds” in both 7:179 and 47:24. In 22:46, “minds” and “hearts” appear within the same verse. The Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation likewise distinguishes the corresponding expressions. Erasing these differences and inserting my preferred word everywhere would change the text I am examining.

What I take from this is not a picture of reason and the heart as opposing camps. If I set sincerity against understanding, or submission against careful examination, I move away from the question asked directly in 47:24. Closure here is associated with the absence of careful study, not with an excess of thought.

In *The Names Taught to Adam: What Did the Angels Not Know?*, I explored the difference between being given knowledge and becoming infallible. A similar distinction matters here: possessing a capacity does not mean I have used it well. Speaking about the heart cannot exempt me from the responsibility to understand.

**Verse references:** 33:4; 7:179; 47:24; 22:46.

## 3. Can “My Heart Says So” Take the Place of Evidence?

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="17:36" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>17:36</strong> — You  shall  not  accept  any  information,  unless  you  verify  it  for  yourself. I  have  given  you  the  hearing,  the eyesight,  and  the  brain,  and  you  are responsible for using them.</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

An idea may feel deeply close to me. Giving it up may even feel like losing part of myself. Yet there is a distance between an assertion's familiarity and its truth. 17:36 does not allow me to cross that distance merely through the intensity of a feeling.

If listening to my heart means honestly noticing what is happening within me, I see an opening for inquiry. What hurt me? Which outcome do I desire? Which words bring me relief? These may be important questions about myself. But their answers alone cannot establish that another person is guilty, that an interpretation perfectly expresses GOD’s command, or that a report is true. The reality of my feeling does not guarantee the reality of what I take it to indicate.

I need to preserve this distinction in my own reflections, too. Discovering a connection that moves me does not mean the verse explicitly and conclusively states that connection. I may offer a thought for consideration; I cannot speak with certainty while leaving the reader to do the verification I have not done.

The responsibility in 17:36 does not appear as a burden that I can hand over to a person or a tool. Finding a verse in KuranTeyit is not the same as considering what it says to me. Feeling reassured cannot replace that work either. I do not want to silence my inner voice. I want to avoid giving it a position beyond the reach of truth.

**Verse references:** 17:36.

## 4. Disease: Addressed to Whom, and in What Context?

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="2:8" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>2:8</strong> — Then there are those who say, “We believe in GOD and the Last Day,” while they are not believers.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="2:9" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>2:9</strong> — In trying to deceive GOD and those who  believe,  they  only  deceive themselves without perceiving.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="2:10" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>2:10</strong> — In  their  minds  there  is  a  disease. Consequently,  GOD  augments  their disease. They have incurred a painful retribution for their lying.</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

Taken in isolation, the language of disease may seem to permit many conclusions. Read together, however, 2:8–10 describes a particular relationship between a profession of belief, deception, and lying. I cannot abandon that connection and identify any bodily illness or psychological difficulty with the condition condemned here.

A person's cardiac illness does not allow me to determine their moral condition from these verses. Nor do I have grounds for applying this language of disease to someone simply because they are sad, anxious, or mentally exhausted. Transferring a language of moral accountability onto medical diagnoses would confuse different questions rather than answer them.

A different possibility troubles me more directly. While considering myself honest, could I be concealing the conflict between what I say and the interest I am protecting? While presenting a good image to others, might I also be learning to believe that image myself? If I read the self-deception in 2:9 only as somebody else's story, the warning remains comfortably distant. When I let it address me, my excuses become less secure.

Reflection on the heart should therefore not encourage an eagerness to classify other people's inner lives. I first need to examine the distance between my own words and my actual position. Even believing that I know myself well is no guarantee that I am hiding nothing from myself.

**Verse references:** 2:8; 2:9; 2:10.

## 5. Sealing: A Grave Warning, Not an Escape from Responsibility

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="2:6" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>2:6</strong> — As for those who disbelieve, it is the same for them; whether you warn them, or not warn them, they cannot believe.*</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="2:7" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>2:7</strong> — GOD seals their minds and their hearing, and their eyes are veiled. They have incurred severe retribution.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="61:5" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>61:5</strong> — Recall  that  Moses  said  to  his  people,  “O  my  people,  why  do  you  hurt me,  even  though  you  know  that  I  am GOD’s  messenger  to  you?”  When they  deviated,  GOD  diverted  their hearts.  For  GOD  does  not  guide  the wicked people.</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

I do not want to diminish the gravity of 2:6–7. GOD’s sealing is explicitly present. At the same time, I cannot overlook the relationship in 61:5 between the people's deviation and the diversion of their hearts. If I separate the human response from GOD’s judgment, I risk using one passage to silence the other.

I cannot claim to derive from these passages a complete system that resolves every question about freedom and destiny. Nevertheless, I hear a serious warning for myself: my response to truth is not inconsequential. Rejecting something I recognize simply because it does not suit me cannot be treated as an insignificant intellectual preference.

That warning does not give me authority to announce that a particular person's heart has been sealed. Disagreement with me does not mean I know GOD’s final judgment concerning them. Likewise, using my own difficulties to conclude that further inquiry or turning toward GOD is now pointless would go beyond what these verses establish.

When I think about the sealing of the heart, the question I want to ask is not who can no longer be saved. A nearer question demands my attention: how much effort am I investing in resisting a truth before me? Could the intelligence I imagine I am using to understand actually be constructing defenses against understanding?

**Verse references:** 2:6; 2:7; 61:5.

## 6. I Do Not Remain Outside the Things I Do

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="83:14" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>83:14</strong> — Indeed, their hearts have become shielded by their sins.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="2:74" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>2:74</strong> — Despite  this,  your  hearts  hardened like  rocks, or even harder. For there are  rocks  from  which  rivers  gush out.  Others  crack  and  release  gentle streams,  and  other  rocks  cringe  out of reverence for GOD.  GOD is never unaware of anything you do.</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

The relationship between sins and the shielding of the heart in 83:14 makes it difficult to imagine that my actions have consequences only in the external world. I am also the person who lives within what I do. While continuing an injustice, I may try to reshape my perception of it in my own favor.

I am not offering this as a mechanical psychological law applying identically to everyone. I am directing the verse's moral warning toward my own conduct. Suppose I protect a feeling of superiority gained by dismissing another person's work. Recognizing the value of that work may then become costly. Accepting the truth could require more than correcting a sentence; I might also have to relinquish a position I have enjoyed.

The comparison with rocks in 2:74 becomes especially powerful here. It stops me from confusing hardness with strength. Remaining unmoved is not always integrity; it can mean that a justified appeal cannot get through. The rocks themselves are not described as uniformly closed, yet how readily I can call my own stubbornness a virtue.

Protecting my heart cannot mean placing it where no criticism can reach it. What needs protection is not only my comfort against being hurt, but also my ability to recognize an error. As I continue to defend a view, I want to ask what I am defending most fiercely: the truth, or the image of myself I have built around that view?

**Verse references:** 83:14; 2:74.

## 7. Having Believed Does Not End the Question of Hardening

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="57:16" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>57:16</strong> — Is  it  not  time  for  those  who  believed to  open  up  their  hearts  for  GOD’s message,  and  the  truth  that  is  revealed  herein?  They  should  not  be  like the  followers  of  previous  scriptures whose  hearts  became  hardened with  time  and,  consequently,  many of them turned wicked.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="57:17" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>57:17</strong> — Know  that  GOD  revives  the  land after  it  had  died.  We  thus  explain the  revelations  for  you,  that  you  may understand.</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

What stops me in 57:16 is that the appeal is addressed to those who believed. Identifying myself as a believer does not settle every question about my heart. The verse brings the need to open it into that very identity.

Years of proximity to a text are not necessarily years of equal openness to being changed by it. Recognizing verses, knowing their subjects, and discussing them do not make examination of my own conduct unnecessary. If I always read truth as an instrument for correcting somebody else, I may be excluding its address to me.

The revival of the land in 57:17, immediately beside this appeal, gives me room to reflect on hope. I cannot turn it into a timetable guaranteeing the same inward transformation for every person. But recognizing hardness does not require me to declare it an unchangeable feature of my character either. The invitation to heed remains a question I can take seriously.

Perhaps its first expression in ordinary life is unremarkable: hearing an objection before defending myself, correcting a sentence I got wrong, or removing a striking claim from an essay because I cannot support it adequately. I do not present these as a program listed by the verse. They are possible ways the invitation to open the heart to truth might take shape in my own work.

**Verse references:** 57:16; 57:17.

## 8. Abraham’s Heart: Is Asking a Question the Opposite of Trust?

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="2:260" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>2:260</strong> — Abraham said, “My Lord, show me how You revive the dead.” He said, “Do you not believe?” He said, “Yes, but I wish to reassure my heart.” He said, “Take four birds, study their marks, place a piece of each bird on top of a hill, then call them to you. They will come to you in a hurry. You should know that GOD is Almighty, Most Wise.”</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

Reading Abraham's question makes me hesitate to construct a language in which belief and inquiry must oppose one another. His belief and his wish to be shown appear within the same conversation. Defining every search for reassurance as the absence of faith would narrow this encounter.

Of course, I cannot conclude that every request made by every person will receive the same response. Nor must I treat every question as automatically sincere. The point I notice is more limited: a person's desire to understand and deepen assurance is not, by itself, presented here as grounds for condemnation.

For my own research, this means resisting two easy responses when I have not yet found an answer. I do not want to turn a weak explanation into a certainty simply because it would comfort me. Nor do I want to treat every unresolved question as proof that all my trust is worthless. I can stand before the verse and acknowledge what I do not know.

The heart's reassurance need not be identified with silencing reason. Abraham's request makes me reconsider that supposed opposition. Perhaps what I most need is not to make questioning into a performance of courage, either. It is to ask because I genuinely wish to learn the answer—and to remain honest toward that answer when it is not the one I hoped to receive.

**Verse references:** 2:260.

## 9. Rejoicing, Trembling, and Softening: More Than One Emotional Appearance

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="13:28" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>13:28</strong> — They  are  the  ones  whose  hearts rejoice  in  remembering  GOD.  Absolutely,  by  remembering  GOD,  the hearts rejoice.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="8:2" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>8:2</strong> — The true believers are those whose hearts tremble when GOD is mentioned, and when His revelations are recited to them, their faith is strengthened, and they trust in their Lord.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="39:23" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>39:23</strong> — GOD  has  revealed  herein  the  best Hadith;  a  book  that  is  consistent, and  points  out  both  ways  (to  Heaven and  Hell).  The  skins  of  those  who reverence  their  Lord  cringe  therefrom, then their skins and their hearts  soften  up  for  GOD’s  message.  Such  is  GOD’s  guidance;  He bestows  it  upon  whomever  He  wills. As  for  those  sent  astray  by  GOD, nothing can guide them.</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

13:28 speaks of the heart's rejoicing. In 8:2, trembling appears alongside strengthened faith and trust. In 39:23, the response of the skin is followed by the softening of skins and hearts toward the message. Reading these expressions together, I cannot compress turning toward GOD into a single emotional appearance.

If I make comfort my only measure, I may misunderstand a warning that confronts me with my own error. If I make intensity my only measure, I may consider an overwhelming experience sufficient even when it changes nothing in my conduct. Rather than arranging these expressions as mutually exclusive emotional states, I need to ask what the person is turning toward.

In *If a Machine Sings a Song Glorifying GOD, Whose Act of Remembrance Is It?*, I distinguished the sound that is heard from the listener's remembrance. The question touches my responsibility as an artist again here. A work's ability to create a powerful atmosphere does not mean I know the direction of the listener's heart. I may observe an effect; I cannot assign a conclusive spiritual score to someone's relationship with GOD through that effect.

These verses do not provide a pulse threshold, a required quantity of tears, or an emotional state every person must visibly maintain at every moment. Nor is my purpose to dismiss emotion. It is to remember that faith, trust, and openness to the message stand beside it. After being moved, what do I do more honestly? For me, that is where the question returns to life.

**Verse references:** 13:28; 8:2; 39:23.

## 10. Moses’ Mother: A Heart in Need of Strengthening

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="28:10" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>28:10</strong> — The  mind  of  Moses’  mother  was growing  so  anxious  that  she  almost gave  away  his  identity.  But  we strengthened  her  heart,  to  make  her  a believer.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="48:4" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>48:4</strong> — He is the One who places contentment into the hearts of believers to augment more faith, in addition to their faith. To GOD belongs all forces of the heavens and the earth. GOD is Omniscient, Most Wise.</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

Anxiety is not concealed in the account of Moses' mother. In 28:10, we read how intensely she was struggling; then comes the strengthening of her heart. Before this scene, I do not want to use an easy language that turns every experience of fear into a straightforward sign of deficient belief.

The verse does not romanticize maternal distress. It describes a situation serious enough that she almost disclosed his identity. It makes me reflect on the danger of judging a person only by the composure they display. Someone who does not appear strong may be carrying a burden another person cannot adequately understand.

48:4 also speaks of contentment as something GOD places in believers' hearts, together with an increase in faith. The picture is not of a person expected to accomplish everything alone and need no support. The heart belongs to an inner life that requires strengthening, not merely commands.

In *The Wisdom Behind Events That Seem Evil: Knowledge, Patience, and Justice in Moses’ Journey*, I explored why trusting does not mean claiming that nothing hurts. I want to preserve that attention here. Rather than immediately confronting a frightened person with a judgment about their faith, I should try to understand them. I am not deriving a diagnosis or treatment for psychological conditions from these verses. I am considering why human vulnerability should not be converted into a spiritual accusation.

**Verse references:** 28:10; 48:4.

## 11. Are the Tongue’s Claim and the Heart’s Condition Always Identical?

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="49:14" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>49:14</strong> — The Arabs said, “We are Mu’mens (believers).” Say, “You have not believed; what you should say is, ‘We are  Muslims  (submitters),’  until  belief is established in your hearts.” If you obey GOD and His messenger, He will not put any of your works to waste. GOD is Forgiver, Most Merciful.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="16:106" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>16:106</strong> — Those  who  disbelieve  in  GOD,  after having  acquired  faith,  and  become fully  content  with  disbelief,  have  incurred  wrath  from  GOD.  The  only ones  to  be  excused  are  those  who are  forced  to  profess  disbelief,  while their hearts are full of faith.*</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

These verses illuminate the relationship between audible speech and inward condition from two different directions. In 49:14, the profession a person makes is not automatically equated with belief established in the heart. In 16:106, faith may remain in the heart despite a compelled profession of disbelief. How can I be so confident that hearing someone's words gives me complete access to their inner life?

This does not make conduct or speech irrelevant. Both passages give importance to the circumstances of what is said and to the person's orientation. What I take from them is that a convenient label does not explain everything. Knowing the conditions under which a person spoke may matter as well as knowing the words.

The same care applies when I look at myself. Becoming fluent in the right expressions does not establish that they have taken root in my life. I cannot use my religious vocabulary as a certificate I have issued to myself. 49:14 makes me consider something more demanding than a name or designation: an inward establishment.

Yet I do not want to turn this reflection into a test of other people's hearts. These verses do not supply a scale on which a conversation, a post, or a single reaction can measure someone's entire faith. Trying to give substance to my own words is very different from imagining that I know everything within another person.

**Verse references:** 49:14; 16:106.

## 12. Responsibility Within: The Line Between Intention and Excuse

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="2:225" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>2:225</strong> — GOD  does  not  hold  you  responsible for  the  mere  utterance  of  oaths;  He holds  you  responsible  for  your  innermost  intentions.  GOD  is  Forgiver, Clement.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="33:5" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>33:5</strong> — You  shall  give  your  adopted  children  names  that  preserve  their  relationship  to  their  genetic  parents. This  is  more  equitable  in  the  sight  of GOD.  If  you  do  not  know  their parents,  then,  as  your  brethren  in religion,  you  shall  treat  them  as members  of  your  family.  You  do  not commit  a  sin  if  you  make  a  mistake in  this  respect;  you  are  responsible for  your  purposeful  intentions. GOD is Forgiver, Most Merciful.</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

2:225 concerns oaths; 33:5 concerns the naming of adopted children and their family relationships. I cannot skip these specific settings and turn intention into a general excuse that removes responsibility for every wrong. I do, however, see the importance of distinguishing purpose from mistake in both passages.

Speaking about the heart's interior must not become a way of making external harm disappear. Thinking that my intentions are good does not prove that everything I say is equitable or that every method I choose is right. The emphasis on equity in 33:5 matters here: taking intention into account does not exclude establishing a relationship correctly.

Nor do I want to identify every passing thought with a settled choice. A thought's appearance, its adoption, its defense, and its implementation are not identical. I consider these distinctions not a ready-made solution to every moral question, but a necessary care when speaking about purpose.

For me, the serious examination is not a search for beautiful sentences that will prove my intentions to somebody else. It is the ability to ask honestly what purpose I am protecting. If I invoke good intentions to avoid correcting an error, intention may already have become an excuse. Speaking about my heart should not place my work and my words beyond examination.

**Verse references:** 2:225; 33:5.

## 13. Monotheism: Whose Mention Satisfies My Heart?

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="39:45" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>39:45</strong> — When GOD ALONE is  mentioned, the  hearts  of  those  who  do  not believe in the Hereafter shrink with aversion. But when others are mentioned  beside  Him,  they  become satisfied.*</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="3:18" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>3:18</strong> — GOD  bears  witness  that  there  is  no god except He, and so do the angels and  those  who  possess  knowledge. Truthfully  and  equitably,  He  is  the absolute  god;  there  is  no  god  but He, the Almighty, Most Wise.</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

When I read 39:45, I want to direct it toward myself first. Is the mention of GOD alone sufficient for me? Or do I expect someone I admire, the circle to which I belong, or my favored interpretation to be present before my confidence can settle? I am not equating love for a person with idolatry. I am examining the authority I attach to that love.

I can learn from a researcher, appreciate an artist's work, and feel gratitude for a teacher's effort. But I need to examine where allegiance begins to turn their statements into something unquestionable. This is a question for understanding my own dependencies more honestly, not for claiming access to other people's hearts.

In 3:18, the witness of those who possess knowledge and of the angels appears in the same verse as GOD’s oneness and justice. This conjunction makes me reflect on why monotheism should not remain merely a correct sentence on my tongue. What I rely on for knowledge, and whether I preserve justice when bearing witness, also disclose the direction of my life.

Seeking wholeness of heart does not mean trying to remove people from my life. It means trying not to give anyone GOD’s place. My own interpretation is included. Having defended a thought for a long time does not end the need to examine it. For me, monotheism also means refusing to proclaim myself the owner of the final word.

**Verse references:** 39:45; 3:18.

## 14. A Gentle Heart Tested by Justice

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="3:159" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>3:159</strong> — It was mercy from GOD that you became  compassionate  towards them.  Had  you  been  harsh  and mean-hearted, they would have abandoned you. Therefore, you shall pardon them and ask forgiveness for them, and consult them. Once you make a decision, carry out your plan, and trust in GOD. GOD loves those who trust in Him.*</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="5:8" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>5:8</strong> — O  you  who  believe,  you  shall  be  absolutely  equitable,  and  observe GOD,  when  you  serve  as  witnesses. Do  not  be  provoked  by  your  conflicts  with  some  people  into  committing  injustice.  You  shall  be  absolutely  equitable,  for  it  is  more  righteous. You  shall  observe  GOD.  GOD  is fully  Cognizant  of  everything  you do.</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

Compassion, pardon, consultation, decision, and trust stand together in 3:159. Gentleness here is not indecision or an inability to act. The verse invites reflection on a way of acting that does not dismiss listening to others or identify decisiveness with domination.

I therefore do not want to measure the softness of my heart only by how readily I become emotional. What happens when a person working beside me objects to my judgment? When I correct someone, do I consider humiliation necessary? Do I consult because I wish to learn, or do I turn consultation into a ceremony in which my decision is expected to receive approval?

5:8 makes the examination harder still. Conflict with a person does not make injustice toward them right. Here is where sincerity may be tested for me: can I acknowledge a true statement made by someone I dislike? Can I recognize a wrong on my own side without minimizing it? While describing myself as someone who loves GOD, am I leaving another person's rights outside that description?

Bearing witness is not just an opinion I carry inwardly. My words may affect someone's life. A gentle tone can still carry an injustice, while a forceful objection may be needed to preserve equity. The heart I consider beside these verses is not a privately pure space detached from human relationships. It is where I cannot escape responsibility for compassion and justice.

**Verse references:** 3:159; 5:8.

## 15. Coming with the Whole Heart: Beyond My Own Certificate of Purity

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="26:88" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>26:88</strong> — That  is  the  day  when  neither  money, nor children, can help.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="26:89" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>26:89</strong> — Only  those  who  come  to  GOD  with their whole heart (will be saved).</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="50:33" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>50:33</strong> — They  reverenced  the  Most  Gracious,  in their privacy,  and came wholeheartedly.</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

The expression in 26:89 in Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation is “with their whole heart.” The Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation uses “tüm kalbiyle.” For me, this poses a more searching question than the reassurance that I possess a clean heart: is my turning toward GOD actually whole?

Following the day when money and children cannot help in 26:88, this verse prevents me from equating the supports I accumulate outside myself with my inward orientation. Reputation, creative output, or the number of people who love me cannot explain the condition of my heart on my behalf. The reference to reverence in privacy in 50:33 also turns my attention toward the life that is not on display.

I do not infer that wholeness means never struggling or never having made a mistake. Earlier passages brought together faith and increase, guidance and continued prayer, and hardening and an appeal to open. Rather than claiming perfection, I consider the need to face whatever divides my orientation.

“My heart is clean” can become a sentence that ends a conversation. These verses lead me in the opposite direction: toward examining myself. If the purpose I describe publicly and the interest I defend when nobody is watching have grown far apart, a flattering self-description cannot close the distance. Wanting to turn toward GOD with my whole heart requires me to take that discrepancy seriously.

**Verse references:** 26:88; 26:89; 50:33.

## 16. I Do Not Possess the Final Word About My Own Heart

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="3:8" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>3:8</strong> — “Our Lord, let not our hearts waver, now that You have guided us. Shower us with Your mercy; You are the Grantor.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="50:37" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>50:37</strong> — This should be a lesson for everyone who possesses a mind, or is able to hear and witness.</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

3:8 gives us a prayer after guidance. I do not hear in it the voice of someone who, having found the right direction, no longer needs help. I hear an appeal from someone who also needs mercy in order to remain oriented.

This prayer does not mean abandoning inquiry and waiting for an inward change to happen by itself. The relationship in 50:37 between receiving a lesson, having a mind, hearing, and witnessing returns me to the use of my attention. If I ask that my heart be preserved while closing my opinions against examination, I conceal from myself the very need expressed in the prayer.

A meaningful direction for research in KuranTeyit emerges here as well. Someone investigating a word should be able to examine not only verses containing the same Turkish equivalent, but also passages in which an Arabic usage receives different renderings. This is a possible research direction, not a system for measuring a person's inner condition. No arrangement of verse references gives me—or any other user—the right to announce the state of someone's heart.

I do not want my desire to understand the heart's secrets to become a claim of superiority over other people. The question I wish to direct toward myself is simpler. Does what I read make me more open to correction? As my knowledge grows, do I understand my need for mercy more deeply, or do I merely become more certain of myself?

**Verse references:** 3:8; 50:37.

## 17. Arabic Word Roots: Heart, Understanding, Chest, and Wholeness

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="22:46" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>22:46</strong> — Did they not roam the earth, then use their minds to understand, and use their ears to hear? Indeed, the real blindness is not the blindness of the eyes, but the blindness of the hearts inside the chests.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="28:10" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>28:10</strong> — The  mind  of  Moses’  mother  was growing  so  anxious  that  she  almost gave  away  his  identity.  But  we strengthened  her  heart,  to  make  her  a believer.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="26:89" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>26:89</strong> — Only  those  who  come  to  GOD  with their whole heart (will be saved).</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

When examining a root, I need to avoid loading all its possible associations into a single sentence. Identifying a word's linguistic family and determining what a verse says are not the same operation. The roots here help trace usage more carefully; they do not replace the translation with newly invented sentences.

**ق ل ب — qalb / qulub.** Qalb is singular and qulub plural; the same root family also contains verbs of turning. The two occurrences in 22:46 are rendered in relation to understanding as “minds,” and to chests as “hearts.” The root connection can invite reflection on changeability, but it does not justify translating every occurrence simply as change or deriving a biological mechanism from it. <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/qurandictionary.jsp?q=qlb" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Quranic Arabic Corpus: ق ل ب</a>.

**ع ق ل — understanding and reasoning.** The relevant verb in 22:46 belongs to this root. What matters here is an action rather than the naming of an organ. Understanding appears as something the human being is expected to do. This does not establish an opposition between reason and the heart, nor require treating the invitation to think as an enemy of feeling. <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/qurandictionary.jsp?q=Eql" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Quranic Arabic Corpus: ع ق ل</a>.

**ف أ د — fuad.** Fuad and qalb occur in the same verse in 28:10. Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation renders the first “mind” and the second “heart.” In 17:36, fuad is rendered “brain.” These examples do not establish a fixed partition in which fuad always means emotion and qalb always means thought. <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/qurandictionary.jsp?q=fAd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Quranic Arabic Corpus: ف أ د</a>.

**ص د ر — sadr / sudur.** The expression for chests in 22:46 belongs to this root family. The heart and the chest in which it is mentioned are not the same word. We can acknowledge this bodily language without assuming that the expression alone explains the tissue in which all thinking occurs. <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/qurandictionary.jsp?q=Sdr" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Quranic Arabic Corpus: ص د ر</a>.

**س ل م — salim.** The form salim in 26:89 is associated with soundness in lexical explanations. Yet Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation uses “with their whole heart.” Examining the root is not permission to replace that rendering silently. The wholeness expressed here opens a more demanding question of orientation than simply declaring my own sincerity. <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/qurandictionary.jsp?q=slm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Quranic Arabic Corpus: س ل م</a>.

These words are related, but their relationships do not produce an anatomical diagram with distinct compartments and settled boundaries. What I can follow explicitly in the verses is the treatment of human understanding, orientation, and responsibility through different expressions.

**Verse references:** 22:46; 28:10; 26:89; 17:36.

## 18. Other Claims

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="17:36" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>17:36</strong> — You  shall  not  accept  any  information,  unless  you  verify  it  for  yourself. I  have  given  you  the  hearing,  the eyesight,  and  the  brain,  and  you  are responsible for using them.</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

**Historical and Sufi interpretations.** Some interpretations distinguish the physical organ from the heart as a spiritual subtlety. Süleyman Uludağ’s “Kalp” entry in the TDV Encyclopedia of Islam describes this approach and ways in which heart, intellect, and self have been related. Such readings can be considered attempts to explain the heart's religious significance. Elaborate spiritual layers or personal intuition treated as certain knowledge should not, however, be presented as a scheme directly established by the verses examined here. <a href="https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/kalb--kalp" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Süleyman Uludağ, “Kalp,” TDV Encyclopedia of Islam</a>.

**The biological organ.** In medicine, the heart pumps blood and operates through an electrical system and relationships with other bodily systems. This is a physiological account, not a spiritual assessment. Accepting it does not require reducing every Quranic expression about the heart to circulation; a spiritual reading does not replace physiology either. <a href="https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/heart" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NHLBI, How the Heart Works</a>.

**Interaction between heart and brain.** In Garfinkel and colleagues' 2014 experiments, perception of fearful faces differed according to when images were presented within the cardiac cycle: fearful faces were detected more readily and rated more intensely during systole. This is a bounded experimental finding about bodily signals and perception. It does not establish that the heart is an independent moral agent, generates faith, or supplies a solved physiological mechanism for a verse. <a href="https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC4012313" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Garfinkel et al., Fear from the Heart, Journal of Neuroscience, 2014; 34:6573–6582</a>.

**The claim that what I feel must be true.** Valuing spiritual experience and making it binding evidence for everyone are different things. The sincerity of an intuition does not establish that it is a judgment from GOD. The responsibility to verify in 17:36 does not disappear when I use spiritual language. While trying to understand the heart, I also need to keep the conclusions I reach in its name open to examination.

These approaches do not answer an identical question by an identical method. Historical interpretation, experimental measurement, and personal experience cannot substitute for one another. Neither having verses deliver a medical diagnosis nor extracting a person's spiritual worth from a medical measurement would be adequately grounded.

**Verse references:** 17:36.

## 19. Conclusion: The Heart’s Secret Does Not Release Me from Responsibility

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="22:46" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>22:46</strong> — Did they not roam the earth, then use their minds to understand, and use their ears to hear? Indeed, the real blindness is not the blindness of the eyes, but the blindness of the hearts inside the chests.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="26:89" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>26:89</strong> — Only  those  who  come  to  GOD  with their whole heart (will be saved).</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

Is the heart merely an organ? If we are asking about its use in these Quranic passages, I find a language broader than an anatomical description. Understanding, closure, purpose, trust, rejoicing, softening, and turning toward GOD all enter the inquiry. That breadth does not authorize me to make certain claims about biological questions I have not established.

The question of 22:46 becomes this reflection for me: am I ready to admit the truth before my eyes into my life? 17:36 preserves my responsibility to verify even when I feel strongly convinced. 57:16 makes me consider why identifying as a believer does not end the call to open my heart. And 5:8 reminds me that inward orientation cannot be protected from examination where another person's rights are concerned.

I do not want to end the inquiry by proclaiming my heart clean. I want to learn to ask more honest questions about it. Can I relinquish a view that examination shows to be wrong? Do I neglect evidence in exchange for comforting words? Can I defend the rights of someone I dislike? Can I feel gratitude for what others teach me without turning them—or myself—into an unquestionable authority?

I cannot claim a flawless inner life while pursuing any of this. My need for guidance, forgiveness, and mercy continues. Coming with the whole heart in 26:89 is not an announcement of achievement for me. It is a demanding and beautiful call that gathers my direction together.

**I want to investigate my heart's secret not by imagining that I know other people's interiors, but by opening my own life to truth. In turning toward GOD, I do not want to silence reason. I want my knowledge, my intentions, and my conduct to become honest enough that they can no longer evade one another.**

**Verse references:** 22:46; 26:89; 17:36; 57:16; 5:8.
