# [2:37] What If Adam Had Not Received Words from His Lord? Redemption, Mercy, and Guidance

**Author:** Berk KÖKSAL
**Date:** November 24, 2026
**Place:** Mersin

This reflection considers 2:37 alongside the surrounding passages about Adam's error, the words he received, his redemption, and the guidance that followed. Its concern is what bearing witness to the oneness of GOD asks of us in knowledge, truthful speech, justice, mercy, and submission.

Peace. Peace be upon you.

Sometimes what disturbs me most about a verse is not what it says, but what it leaves unreported. Adam received words from his Lord. What if he had not? At first, the question sounds like an attempt to reconstruct an absent episode from a very distant past. Soon, however, it turns back toward me. After I have done wrong, whose word do I seek? Can I face the truth about myself? And what happens between hearing guidance and living by it?

It is tempting to settle the mystery with a confident alternative ending. Without the words, surely this would have happened; without them, surely that could never have happened. But the verses do not write that other history. They report a boundary, a violation, words received, redemption, and guidance. My task is to attend to those connections without disguising imagination as knowledge.

In my earlier essay, [The Names Taught to Adam: What Did the Angels Not Know?](https://berkkoksal.com/writings/names_taught_to_adam_en/), I reflected on the names Adam learned and the limits of what we can know. Here I return to the same narrative at a more vulnerable moment. Why are the words Adam receives placed beside redemption? Are words merely information, or do the passages also call me to examine how truth is spoken, responsibility is acknowledged, and guidance is followed? I want to pursue those questions without forcing the verse to say what it has not said.

## 1. If the Words Had Not Been Received: What the Verse Does and Does Not Say

### Verse

> **2:37**
>
> فَتَلَقَّىٰٓ ءَادَمُ مِن رَّبِّهِۦ كَلِمَـٰتٍۢ فَتَابَ عَلَيْهِ ۚ إِنَّهُۥ هُوَ ٱلتَّوَّابُ ٱلرَّحِيمُ
>
> Then, Adam received from his Lord words, whereby He redeemed him. He is the Redeemer, Most Merciful.
>
> Sonra Âdem Rabbinden kelimeler aldı, O böylelikle onu kurtardı. O Kurtarıcıdır, En Merhametlidir.

Source: <a href="https://kuranteyit.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">kuranteyit.com</a>

### Reflection on the Verse

What would have happened if Adam had not received words from his Lord? The verse does not narrate that alternative history. It reports something more definite and more demanding of our attention: Adam received words, and GOD redeemed him. To notice the connection is necessary. To transform it into the claim that redemption would have been impossible under every other circumstance would be to go beyond what the verse actually says.

Even the counterfactual conceals several different questions. What if the words had never been given? What if they had been offered but not received? What if Adam had received them and refused to respond? The passage does not recount any of those events. I can ask about them, but I must not treat my hypothetical answer as revelation. Restraint is not an obstacle to thought; it protects the difference between what I know and what I imagine.

The remarkable fact is that Adam's story of redemption is told through words received from his Lord. My first impulse is to ask for their precise content. But I may be missing the more basic relationship: a human being in need, a Lord who gives, words that are received, and a redemption the text attributes to GOD. If I turn this into a hunt for a hidden formula before attending to that relationship, I could lose sight of what the verse has actually made visible.

**Verse references:** 2:37.

## 2. Before the Words, There Was a Boundary and a Violation

### Verse

> **2:35** — We said, "O Adam, live with your wife in Paradise, and eat therefrom generously, as you please, but do not approach this tree, lest you sin."

> **2:36** — But the devil duped them, and caused their eviction therefrom. We said, "Go down as enemies of one another. On Earth shall be your habitation and provision for awhile."

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 2:35 first describes an extraordinary breadth of permission. Adam and his wife may enjoy Paradise, with a prohibition concerning a particular tree. Verse 2:36 then describes deception, eviction, and an earthly habitation. The words of 2:37 enter a story in which a boundary has already been crossed. If I reverse the order, I risk speaking about mercy without honestly facing what preceded it.

There is a temptation to turn the forbidden tree into an investigation of hidden botanical details. What species was it? What did its fruit look like? The cited passages do not answer those questions. Something more uncomfortable is in plain sight: why does the presence of a single prohibition become so significant amid an abundance of permission? I can ask that about Adam as a distant character, but the question becomes more serious when I ask it about my own life.

Receiving words did not erase the event that had already happened. The text does not present redemption as pretending that the violation never took place. Nor does it say that a mistake automatically cancels every possibility of change. Between those extremes lies the morally difficult territory of admitting what happened and learning how to turn again.

**Verse references:** 2:35, 2:36, 2:37.

## 3. Hearing the Warning and Keeping It

### Verse

> **7:19** — "As for you, Adam, dwell with your wife in Paradise, and eat therefrom as you please, but do not approach this one tree, lest you fall in sin."

> **20:117** — We then said, "O Adam, this is an enemy of you and your wife. Do not let him evict you from Paradise, lest you become miserable.

### Reflection on the Verse

The boundary is not left to guesswork. Verse 7:19 repeats the prohibition, and 20:117 names the devil as an enemy and warns of eviction from Paradise. Adam's situation therefore involves more than vague intuition. A warning has been given. Yet a warning heard and a warning lived are not the same thing.

I sometimes assume that recognizing a danger is enough to keep me safe from it. I know a habit is destructive, so surely I will stop. I know a claim is dishonest, so surely I will not repeat it. Experience often makes those assurances uncomfortable. The verses do not offer a universal psychological theory, but they do allow me to see that knowledge and conduct can part company.

The subsequent receipt of words does not make the original warning trivial. Both moments matter. GOD's communication appears first as a command that deserves respect and later, in 2:37, as words connected to redemption. I cannot turn that sequence into permission to disregard the first word because I hope for another word afterward.

**Verse references:** 7:19, 20:117, 2:37.

## 4. When a Lie Presents Itself as Advice

### Verse

> **7:20** — The devil whispered to them, in order to reveal their bodies, which were invisible to them. He said, "Your Lord did not forbid you from this tree, except to prevent you from becoming angels, and from attaining eternal existence."

> **7:21** — He swore to them, "I am giving you good advice."

### Reflection on the Verse

The deception in 7:20 does not come as an openly hostile command. The devil supplies an explanation for the prohibition, presenting it as a barrier to becoming angels and attaining eternal existence. In 7:21 he reinforces his claim with an oath and the appearance of good advice. Falsehood is made persuasive by a story about what is supposedly being withheld.

When I deeply want something, how carefully do I examine an explanation that seems to justify it? A confident voice can sound like knowledge. A pledge of sincerity can sound like evidence. But the oath in this passage does not make the assertion true. I need to distinguish the strength with which a statement is delivered from its fidelity to what has already been said.

This matters when someone claims moral or religious authority over me. A declaration that a person has my best interests at heart cannot, by itself, overturn a boundary given by GOD. I do not conclude that all advice is suspect. Rather, the episode asks me to judge advice by its content, not merely by the assurance or status of the one who offers it.

**Verse references:** 7:20, 7:21.

## 5. What Do We Try to Cover?

### Verse

> **7:22** — He thus duped them with lies. As soon as they tasted the tree, their bodies became visible to them, and they tried to cover themselves with the leaves of Paradise. Their Lord called upon them: "Did I not enjoin you from that tree, and warn you that the devil is your most ardent enemy?"

### Reflection on the Verse

After they taste the tree, 7:22 describes their bodies becoming visible and their attempt to cover themselves with leaves. I am struck by the distinction between dealing with an event and trying to manage its visibility. When something painful about me becomes known, my first instinct may be to control what others see rather than to examine the action itself.

I do not want to read an unsupported theory of the human body into this scene. The verse recounts a particular change and a particular response. It also records a question from their Lord recalling the earlier warning. What becomes visible is not the only issue; what had already been said is brought back to their attention.

There is an ethical difference between concealing evidence of a wrong and acknowledging the wrong itself. The story makes that distinction worth asking about, although it does not tell us every intention behind the movement to cover themselves. I should not pretend to know their inner thoughts. I can, however, ask what I do when truth becomes uncomfortable for me to face.

**Verse references:** 7:22.

## 6. Words and an Unhidden Confession

### Verse

> **7:23** — They said, "Our Lord, we have wronged our souls, and unless You forgive us and have mercy on us, we will be losers."

### Reflection on the Verse

The words recorded in 7:23 do not shift responsibility onto somebody else. Adam and his wife acknowledge having wronged their own souls; they ask for forgiveness and mercy, recognizing the danger of loss. Read beside 2:37, this moment reveals the moral weight of speaking truthfully about one's own action.

There is nevertheless an important boundary. Verse 7:23 records a supplication. Verse 2:37 reports that Adam received words from his Lord. The latter verse does not explicitly identify those words by quoting 7:23. The passages can be read together without pretending that their relationship is more precisely specified than it is. I do not want an appealing connection to become a certainty that the text has not granted me.

What moves me is the absence of an elaborate defense. They are not presented as explaining why their behavior should be excused. They acknowledge a wrong and seek mercy from GOD. That does not mean that every human problem is solved by self-accusation; this is a particular admission of a particular act. It asks whether I am capable of naming my own responsibility before demanding that others recognize it.

**Verse references:** 7:23, 2:37.

## 7. Redemption Did Not Cancel the Descent

### Verse

> **7:24** — He said, "Go down as enemies of one another. On earth shall be your habitation and provision for awhile."

> **7:25** — He said, "On it you will live, on it you will die, and from it you will be brought out."

### Reflection on the Verse

The story refuses a simple equation between redemption and the immediate removal of every consequence. Verse 2:37 announces redemption; 7:24–25 still speak of life on earth, death, and being brought forth from it. The account allows these realities to stand together. A person can be spoken of as redeemed while the earthly journey has not been undone.

This does not give me a complete explanation of every reason for the descent. I know that 2:36 already speaks of going down and that redemption is then reported. I must avoid turning that sequence into a grand hidden chronology. But it challenges an assumption I sometimes carry: that mercy must look like the restoration of everything I have lost. The persistence of a consequence does not by itself prove the absence of mercy. Nor can I promise that every consequence in every life signifies mercy.

If I make “What if he had not received the words?” merely another way of asking whether he could have stayed in Paradise, I risk asking a narrower question than the verses invite. A moral future remains to be lived. Redemption is not an excuse to stop attending to how one lives afterward.

**Verse references:** 7:24, 7:25, 2:37, 2:36.

## 8. Forgetting and Failing to Stand Firm

### Verse

> **20:115** — We tested Adam in the past, but he forgot, and we found him indecisive.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 20:115 describes Adam as having forgotten and as having been found indecisive. I find this deeply unsettling because it complicates my desire to divide every moral failure into an easily recognized act of deliberate hostility. Forgetfulness and hesitation can matter. Yet it would be equally careless to declare every lapse of memory sinful or every moment of uncertainty rebellious.

I know how easily an obligation can fade from attention. I promise to speak fairly but hesitate when fairness will cost me something. I know that I should correct an error, yet postpone the correction because I would rather not face the discomfort. These are my examples, not a claim that Adam experienced each of them. The passage invites comparison while keeping the original event distinct.

Read in this light, the words received in 2:37 draw attention to a human being who does not possess perfect constancy. The verse does not tell us what would have happened without the words. It does make me ask another question: when I have forgotten something I was meant to honor, will I still be willing to learn, acknowledge, and return?

**Verse references:** 20:115, 2:37.

## 9. My Desires Before the Promise of Eternity

### Verse

> **20:120** — But the devil whispered to him, saying, "O Adam, let me show you the tree of eternity and unending kingship."

### Reflection on the Verse

The temptation described in 20:120 has a particular shape: the tree of eternity and an unending kingship. I should not reduce the passage to curiosity alone, for the verse tells us something about the attraction being offered. The promise concerns duration and power. It does not reveal everything Adam thought or felt, but it does reveal what the devil placed before him.

I recognize the human wish to keep what matters. I want the people I love to remain with me, my work to endure, my health not to fail, the good I have built not to disappear. Those desires are not, by themselves, a transcript of Adam's motives. Still, they help me understand why a promise framed as permanent gain deserves careful scrutiny. Longing for something cannot turn an unreliable offer into truth.

If I focus only on the words of redemption while ignoring the words of temptation, I overlook part of the moral movement in the narrative. Words can be presented as guidance while working against it. That is why the source, content, and consequence of a message must remain distinct, especially when the message promises exactly what I wish to hear.

**Verse references:** 20:120.

## 10. Naming the Fall Without Excusing It

### Verse

> **20:121** — They ate from it, whereupon their bodies became visible to them, and they tried to cover themselves with the leaves of Paradise. Adam thus disobeyed his Lord, and fell.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 20:121 does not conceal the act behind a softer description. It says Adam disobeyed his Lord and fell. That clarity matters. If I want to speak seriously about redemption, I cannot first remove the wrong from the story. The verse also recalls the visibility of their bodies and their attempt to cover themselves, but the judgment about disobedience is explicit.

I notice how carefully I sometimes choose words to protect my own image. I call a deliberate wrong an unfortunate incident. I say that circumstances forced me, even when I had some room to act differently. Circumstances can be genuinely difficult; the point is not to ignore them. It is to ask whether I am describing my own conduct accurately or editing the description until I no longer have to face it.

Yet the narrative does not end at 20:121. The next verse reports something else. If I omit the fall, I turn mercy into sentimentality. If I omit the next verse, I turn a person's failure into their entire identity. The sequence forces me to respect both the seriousness of the act and the reality of what follows.

**Verse references:** 20:121.

## 11. Chosen, Redeemed, and Guided

### Verse

> **20:122** — Subsequently, his Lord chose him, redeemed him, and guided him.

> **2:37** — Then, Adam received from his Lord words, whereby He redeemed him. He is the Redeemer, Most Merciful.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 20:122 is brief but dense: Adam's Lord chose him, redeemed him, and guided him. Verse 2:37 connects the receiving of words with redemption. Together these statements resist the reduction of Adam's story to a single event of human failure. The fall is reported, and so is what follows it.

Neither verse supplies a complete technical account of how choosing, redeeming, and guiding occur. I cannot responsibly turn those verbs into an invisible sequence of spiritual mechanisms that every human must repeat. The subject of the acts is GOD. Adam's ability to receive knowledge does not make him the author of his own redemption.

The original question acquires a sharper boundary here. The fact that Adam received words and was redeemed does not tell me what his Lord could or could not have done in every imagined alternative. I know the path the text reports, not every possible path. But that knowledge already challenges despair: the same person who fell is described as chosen, redeemed, and guided.

**Verse references:** 20:122, 2:37.

## 12. Why Did Adam, Taught the Names, Also Receive Words?

### Verse

> **2:31** — He taught Adam all the names* then presented them to the angels, saying, "Give Me the names of these, if you are right."

> **2:32** — They said, "Be You glorified, we have no knowledge, except that which You have taught us. You are the Omniscient, Most Wise."

> **3:18** — GOD bears witness that there is no god except He, and so do the angels and those who possess knowledge. Truthfully and equitably, He is the absolute god; there is no god but He, the Almighty, Most Wise.

### Reflection on the Verse

In 2:31 Adam is taught the names. In 2:32 the angels acknowledge that their knowledge reaches only as far as what they have been taught. Knowledge appears here not as self-created sovereignty but as something received. Later in the narrative, Adam receives words from his Lord in a different and morally charged setting.

In my earlier reflection, [The Names Taught to Adam: What Did the Angels Not Know?](https://berkkoksal.com/writings/names_taught_to_adam_en/), I explored the distinction between knowing the names and acknowledging the limits of knowledge. The question before me now is more personal: does knowing many things guarantee that I can face my own wrongdoing truthfully? A person may be capable of naming objects and still need to learn how to speak honestly about themselves.

Verse 3:18 places testimony to the oneness of GOD beside knowledge and equity. That connection warns me against treating knowledge as an ornament to my own superiority. Bearing witness requires truthful conduct as well as the right words.

The angels' statement also restrains my own confidence. I cannot reconstruct Adam's private thoughts or list every word he received where the passage has not listed them. The admission of a limit is not the abandonment of study. It is what lets study remain faithful to the difference between what has been revealed and what I have merely inferred.

**Verse references:** 2:31, 2:32, 3:18.

## 13. The Voice of Superiority and the Voice of Responsibility

### Verse

> **2:34** — When we said to the angels, "Fall prostrate before Adam," they fell prostrate, except Satan; he refused, was too arrogant, and a disbeliever.

> **7:12** — He said, "What prevented you from prostrating when I ordered you?" He said, "I am better than he; You created me from fire, and created him from mud."

> **7:23** — They said, "Our Lord, we have wronged our souls, and unless You forgive us and have mercy on us, we will be losers."

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 2:34 names Satan's refusal and arrogance. In 7:12 he presents an argument about the materials from which he and Adam were created, claiming superiority. Place that beside the words spoken in 7:23: Adam and his wife acknowledge having wronged their own souls. Two markedly different kinds of speech become visible—self-elevation and an admission of responsibility.

I must not turn that comparison into a complete theory about unreported outcomes. I cannot say with certainty that Satan would have been redeemed by uttering particular words, or that Adam would necessarily have shared Satan's fate had the words never been received. The cited verses do not narrate those possibilities. What I can compare is the direction of the speech we actually hear.

It is easy to recognize arrogance in somebody else. The harder question is whether I recognize it in my own defense of an opinion, in the contempt I sometimes show toward someone I consider less informed, or in my refusal to correct what I have said. I want the comparison to examine my own voice, not become another reason to declare myself superior.

**Verse references:** 2:34, 7:12, 7:23.

## 14. Seeking Forgiveness Without Persisting in Wrongdoing

### Verse

> **3:135** — If they fall in sin or wrong their souls, they remember GOD and ask forgiveness for their sins—and who forgives the sins except GOD—and they do not persist in sins, knowingly.

> **7:23** — They said, "Our Lord, we have wronged our souls, and unless You forgive us and have mercy on us, we will be losers."

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 3:135 speaks about people who, after sinning or wronging their souls, remember GOD, seek forgiveness, and do not knowingly persist in their sins. Read beside the admission in 7:23, this keeps words and conduct within the same moral horizon. Saying something true matters. So does the relationship between what I say and what I continue to do.

If I learn a beautiful request for forgiveness while intentionally defending the very harm I have caused, what have I understood? Verse 3:135 directs attention to persistence as well as speech. It does not give me a perfect test for judging the hidden sincerity of other people. I cannot inspect their hearts. But I can examine whether my own actions contradict the direction I claim to be taking.

That is why I hesitate to treat the words in 2:37 as though they were a magical password. The verse does not describe a mechanical transaction. The confession in 7:23 and the warning against knowingly persisting in sin in 3:135 provide a stronger moral question: do I want the appearance of returning, or am I willing to face the changes that truthful return may demand?

**Verse references:** 3:135, 7:23, 2:37.

## 15. Do Not Despair of Mercy or Flee Responsibility

### Verse

> **39:53** — Proclaim: "O My servants who exceeded the limits, never despair of GOD's mercy. For GOD forgives all sins. He is the Forgiver, Most Merciful."

> **39:54** — You shall obey your Lord, and submit to Him totally, before the retribution overtakes you, then you cannot be helped.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 39:53 addresses servants who have exceeded the limits and tells them not to despair of GOD's mercy. Set beside Adam's redemption, the invitation reminds me that the subject is not only an isolated event in the remote past. The scripture also speaks directly to those who have gone wrong. Still, I must not recast that invitation as a guarantee that my choices can have no consequences.

The immediately following 39:54 calls for obedience and total submission before retribution arrives. The proximity of the verses is important. One resists despair; the other resists postponement and complacency. I cannot use mercy as an excuse to keep acting unjustly, and I cannot use the seriousness of wrongdoing as a reason to declare mercy unavailable.

I do not know the unwritten outcome of a world in which Adam never received the words. But these verses do not leave me entirely without direction in my own life. They ask what I am doing now with the opportunity to respond: whether I will correct my speech, face a wrong, abandon a harmful course, and stop telling myself that a better day for change will always be available.

**Verse references:** 39:53, 39:54.

## 16. The Path of Guidance Did Not End with Adam

### Verse

> **2:38** — We said, "Go down therefrom, all of you. When guidance comes to you from Me, those who follow My guidance will have no fear, nor will they grieve.

> **20:123** — He said, "Go down therefrom, all of you. You are enemies of one another. When guidance comes to you from Me, anyone who follows My guidance will not go astray, nor suffer any misery.

> **7:29** — Say, "My Lord advocates justice, and to stand devoted to Him alone at every place of worship. You shall devote your worship absolutely to Him alone. Just as He initiated you, you will ultimately go back to Him."

### Reflection on the Verse

Verses 2:38 and 20:123 speak about guidance coming from GOD after the descent. The assurances attached to following that guidance matter, and their placement matters too. Adam's story does not close with an isolated act of redemption. The narrative opens toward a future in which people are called to respond to guidance themselves.

I need to keep two moments distinct. Adam's receiving words from his Lord is reported as a particular event. The later call to follow guidance addresses a continuing responsibility. Verse 7:29 brings justice and devotion to GOD alone into the discussion. That makes the question of guidance more than a search for comfort: I must examine whose authority I accept, and whether my conduct remains fair. The fact that Adam was redeemed is not presented here as an automatic decision made on behalf of every descendant, regardless of what they do. The verses make following guidance meaningful rather than redundant.

In my earlier essay, [Leaders and Followers](https://berkkoksal.com/writings/leaders-and-followers-satans-leadership-en/), I considered responsibility within human chains of authority. Here the question reaches its source: whose guidance do I actually follow? I cannot replace the hard work of listening and living with the comfort of saying that somebody before me was guided. The reported redemption calls me to consider my own response, not to borrow somebody else's.

**Verse references:** 2:38, 20:123, 7:29.

## 17. Arabic Word Roots: Receiving, Returning, and Mercy

### Verse

> **2:37** — Then, Adam received from his Lord words, whereby He redeemed him. He is the Redeemer, Most Merciful.

> **7:23** — They said, "Our Lord, we have wronged our souls, and unless You forgive us and have mercy on us, we will be losers."

> **20:122** — Subsequently, his Lord chose him, redeemed him, and guided him.

### Reflection on the Verse

The verb **فَتَلَقَّىٰٓ (fa-talaqqā)** in 2:37 is associated with the root **ل ق ي (l-q-y)**, a family of meanings involving encounter and reception. **كَلِمَـٰتٍۢ (kalimātin)** belongs to **ك ل م (k-l-m)** and occurs here as a plural noun. **رَّبِّهِۦ (rabbihi)** relates to **ر ب ب (r-b-b)**; within the sentence it identifies the source from whom the words came. Neither the roots nor the plural form give me an exact list of the words, their number, or the events of a hypothetical alternative history.

In the same verse **فَتَابَ (fa-tāba)** and **ٱلتَّوَّابُ (al-tawwāb)** share the root **ت و ب (t-w-b)**, connected with turning and returning. The grammar and context matter for understanding who is acting toward whom. **ٱلرَّحِيمُ (al-raḥīm)** is associated with **ر ح م (r-ḥ-m)**, a family of meanings concerning mercy. Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation uses “redeemed” in 2:37; the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation renders this with “kurtardı.” The lexical observation can illuminate the comparison; it cannot replace the actual sentence.

In 7:23, **ظَلَمْنَا (ẓalamnā)** comes from **ظ ل م (ẓ-l-m)** and **تَغْفِرْ (taghfir)** from **غ ف ر (gh-f-r)**. The verb **هَدَىٰ (hadā)** in 20:122 is related to **ه د ي (h-d-y)**, the root family of guidance. These words place confession, forgiveness, redemption, and guidance in conversation with one another. Studying their roots should make me more exact about what the verses say, not more tempted to claim a hidden certainty they have not supplied.

**Verse references:** 2:37, 7:23, 20:122.

## 18. Other Claims

### Verse

> **2:37** — Then, Adam received from his Lord words, whereby He redeemed him. He is the Redeemer, Most Merciful.

> **7:23** — They said, "Our Lord, we have wronged our souls, and unless You forgive us and have mercy on us, we will be losers."

> **20:122** — Subsequently, his Lord chose him, redeemed him, and guided him.

### Reflection on the Verse

Interpretations differ over the identity of the words Adam received. Some traditional explanations identify them with the supplication recorded in 7:23, beginning with the acknowledgment that Adam and his wife wronged their souls. That reading connects two clearly related passages. Still, 2:37 does not provide an explicit quotation of the words, so I should not present the identification as though it appeared verbatim within that verse.

Rashad Khalifa’s footnote to 2:37 draws an analogy between Adam's reception of words and the specific words provided to human beings in Sura 1 for communication with GOD, including an emphasis on mathematical structure. This is a note attached to the translation, not a list of the words inside the main verse itself. One may study or contest the explanatory proposal, but the footnote and the verse remain different kinds of statements.

Other approaches may maintain that, without the words, Adam could never have been redeemed, or that a single fixed wording is the only possible route of redemption for every human being. Those claims go beyond the reported event. A traditional or historical explanation need not be dismissed merely for being old; neither does its age confer certainty. My task here is to recognize the reported redemption without claiming knowledge of every counterfactual the scripture has not narrated.

**Verse references:** 2:37, 7:23, 20:122.

## 19. Conclusion

### Verse

> **2:37** — Then, Adam received from his Lord words, whereby He redeemed him. He is the Redeemer, Most Merciful.

> **7:23** — They said, "Our Lord, we have wronged our souls, and unless You forgive us and have mercy on us, we will be losers."

> **20:122** — Subsequently, his Lord chose him, redeemed him, and guided him.

> **2:38** — We said, "Go down therefrom, all of you. When guidance comes to you from Me, those who follow My guidance will have no fear, nor will they grieve.

### Reflection on the Verse

I began by asking what would have happened if Adam had not received words from his Lord. The question has not yielded an invented alternative ending. Instead, it has returned me to the actual testimony of 2:37: Adam received words, and GOD redeemed him. Verse 7:23 records the acknowledgment of wrongdoing and the request for forgiveness and mercy. Verse 20:122 reports choosing, redemption, and guidance. Verse 2:38 leaves open a continuing human responsibility to follow guidance when it comes.

Four concerns remain with me: the seriousness of a warning, the truthfulness with which I name my conduct, my dependence on GOD rather than a human intermediary for mercy, and the responsibility of following the guidance I receive. I do not claim that these form an automatic equation guaranteeing a particular outcome for every person. They are connected moral questions raised by the verses themselves. Knowledge, accurate speech, and responsible action are not interchangeable, even when they belong together.

Two easy answers now seem inadequate. One says, “Without the words, redemption could never have been possible,” as though the passage had given a ruling about an unrealized world. The other says, “He was redeemed, so the warning no longer matters.” I cannot accept either without losing something the text preserves. The weight of 2:37 lies in a narrative that does not hide wrongdoing yet still reports GOD's mercy. I cannot know the fate of an event that did not happen. I can ask, however, how I respond to the words and guidance that have reached me today.

**Verse references:** 2:37, 7:23, 20:122, 2:38.
