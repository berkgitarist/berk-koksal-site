# What Is the Deepest Intention? What Are Our Deepest Intentions?

**Author:** Berk KÖKSAL  
**Date:** September 08, 2026  
**Place:** Mersin

The main purpose of this article is to contemplate the verses together with other relevant verses and to explore, sincerely and deeply, what bearing witness to the oneness of GOD means for human life in terms of knowledge, justice, truthfulness, and submission.

I may think I know why I did something.

“I meant well.”

“I did not want to hurt anyone.”

“I only wanted to help.”

“I was searching for what is right.”

All of these may genuinely be true. Yet the Quran brings me to a more uncomfortable question:

**Is the intention I state the same as the deepest intention within me?**

Even while explaining our own actions, we may not fully know ourselves. Beneath the visible purpose there may be a desire for reputation, acceptance, superiority, safety, control, recognition, or gratitude.

I may tell myself, “I did something good.” But was I seeking the good, or seeking to be seen as good?

Was I defending truth, or defending my need to be right?

Was I seeking GOD’s pleasure, or human approval?

This is where the question of the deepest intention begins.

## 1. GOD Knows the Innermost Intentions

### Verse

**4:63**

**Arabic:** أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ ٱلَّذِينَ يَعْلَمُ ٱللَّهُ مَا فِى قُلُوبِهِمْ فَأَعْرِضْ عَنْهُمْ وَعِظْهُمْ وَقُل لَّهُمْ فِىٓ أَنفُسِهِمْ قَوْلًۢا بَلِيغًا

**English:** GOD  is  fully  aware  of  their  innermost  intentions.  You  shall  ignore them,  enlighten  them,  and  give  them good  advice  that  may  save  their souls.

**Turkish:** TANRI onların en içteki niyetlerinin tamamen farkındadır. Sen, onlara aldırış etme, onları aydınlat ve onlara nefislerini kurtarabilecek güzel öğüt ver.

Source: <a href="https://kuranteyit.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">kuranteyit.com</a>

### Reflection on the Verse

The expression that stops me most in this verse is not merely “intentions,” but **“innermost intentions.”**

Human action therefore has more than an outward face. Beneath the reason I give, there may be another inner direction. More importantly, GOD knows not only the action but also that depth.

I may persuade other people. I may even persuade myself. I can construct a polished, moral, and logical explanation for what I did, while still being unable to hide from GOD what actually moved me.

This does not invite me to guess other people’s hidden motives. The verse does not give me that authority. It tells me that GOD knows what is within them.

Perhaps the deepest intention is not the story a person tells the world, but **what the heart truly seeks when excuses, titles, appearances, and self-justifications are stripped away.**

**Verse reference: 4:63.**

## 2. Is Saying “Our Intentions Were Good and Righteous” Enough?

### Verse

> **4:62** — How  will  it  be  when  a  disaster  hits them,  as  a  consequence  of  their  own works?  They  will  come  to  you  then and  swear  by  GOD:  “Our  intentions were good and righteous!”

> **4:63** — GOD  is  fully  aware  of  their  innermost  intentions.  You  shall  ignore them,  enlighten  them,  and  give  them good  advice  that  may  save  their souls.

### Reflection on the Verse

The sequence of these two verses reaches directly into the heart of the subject.

People make a statement about themselves: **“Our intentions were good and righteous!”** Immediately afterward, the text says that GOD is fully aware of their **innermost intentions.**

A distance appears between the intention that is declared and the intention known by GOD. This does not mean that every claim of good intention is false. It means that saying “I meant well” does not automatically make a person right.

Sometimes “I did not mean anything bad” may be true. At other times it may become a way of avoiding the consequences of what I did. The deeper question is: What was I protecting—my reputation, comfort, control, position in a group, the feeling of being right, or what was actually true?

A person can use the language of good intentions. GOD knows the direction beneath the words.

**Verse references: 4:62, 4:63.**

## 3. Being Responsible for Our Innermost Intentions

### Verse

> **2:225** — GOD  does  not  hold  you  responsible for  the  mere  utterance  of  oaths;  He holds  you  responsible  for  your  innermost  intentions.  GOD  is  Forgiver, Clement.

### Reflection on the Verse

This verse does not leave the matter only within GOD’s knowledge. It brings it into responsibility: **He holds us responsible for our innermost intentions.**

We often reduce morality to what is visible: What did I do? What did I say? Did I follow the rule? But 2:225 opens an inward door: Why did I do it? Why did I say it? What stood behind my words?

It also matters that the verse ends by describing GOD as Forgiver and Clement. Facing our inner motives does not have to produce despair. A person can notice the mixture within and seek to purify it.

Seeing the deepest intention may therefore be less about condemning oneself and more about becoming more truthful before GOD.

**Verse reference: 2:225.**

## 4. What Is the “Actual Intention”?

### Verse

> **5:89** — GOD does not hold you responsible for the mere utterance of oaths; He holds you responsible for your actual intentions. If you violate an oath, you shall atone by feeding ten poor people from the same food you offer to your own family, or clothing them, or by freeing a slave. If you cannot afford this, then you shall fast three days. This is the atonement for violating the oaths that you swore to keep. You shall fulfill your oaths. GOD thus explains His revelations to you, that you may be appreciative.

### Reflection on the Verse

2:225 speaks of “innermost intentions,” while 5:89 speaks of “actual intentions.” The two expressions illuminate one another.

What is “actual” lies beneath appearance. A person may give the first explanation that comes to mind, yet discover another motive when looking more deeply.

I may believe that I am defending truth in an argument. But why do I become uncomfortable when the other person turns out to be right? If truth is truly what I want, should I not be glad that truth became clear, regardless of whose mouth it came from?

One way of learning our motives may be to watch how they react when the outcome does not favor us. Do I rejoice when what is right becomes clear, or only when I am the one being vindicated?

**Verse reference: 5:89.**

## 5. What Lies Beneath What We Conceal and Declare?

### Verse

> **64:4** — He  knows  everything  in  the  heavens and  the  earth,  and  He  knows  everything  you  conceal  and  everything you  declare.  GOD is  fully  aware  of the innermost thoughts.

### Reflection on the Verse

Human life has what I show and what I hide. 64:4 places both within GOD’s knowledge. Yet the verse goes further and speaks of the **innermost thoughts.**

What I consciously conceal may not even be the deepest layer. Sometimes the reason I give for my behavior is real but incomplete. I may help someone because I truly want to help, while also wanting that person to love me, thank me, or see me as good.

Human intention does not have to be one-dimensional. That may be why the question of the deepest intention is difficult: **If every secondary benefit disappeared, would I still do it?**

If no one saw, thanked, praised, or identified me with the act—if no one knew except GOD—what would remain?

**Verse reference: 64:4.**

## 6. Hiding Does Not Change the Intention

### Verse

> **3:29** — Say,  “Whether  you  conceal  your  innermost  thought,  or  declare  it,  GOD is  fully  aware  thereof.”  He  is  fully aware  of  everything  in  the  heavens and the earth. GOD is Omnipotent.

### Reflection on the Verse

People learn to keep some thoughts private, and that can be appropriate. Saying everything that crosses the mind is not a virtue. The relationship with GOD is different: there is no successful concealment there.

The unsettling freedom of 3:29 is this: there is no reason to perform before GOD. If I am jealous, I am jealous. If I want recognition, I want it. If I am afraid, I am afraid. Anger, pride, or the desire for superiority are not purified merely because I place religious language over them.

GOD already knows.

Why, then, should I hide them from myself? Perhaps one beginning of repentance and inner purification is becoming willing to see honestly what GOD already knows.

**Verse reference: 3:29.**

## 7. A Person Can Cover Himself, but Not from GOD

### Verse

> **11:5** — Indeed,  they  hide  their  innermost thoughts,  as  if  to  keep  Him  from knowing  them.  In  fact,  as  they  cover themselves  with  their  clothes,  He knows  all  their  secrets  and  declarations.  He  knows  the  innermost thoughts.

### Reflection on the Verse

The image in this verse is powerful: people cover themselves and try to hide, yet they cannot place a veil between themselves and GOD’s knowledge.

I can also read this through the defenses I build about myself. I can invent explanations, rationalize my error, shift responsibility, and make my behavior look better in my own mind. None of that changes what GOD sees.

That is why the search for the deepest intention may be more meaningful in solitude than in public explanation.

I can ask: **If I owed no explanation to anyone, how would I explain this action to myself?** And deeper still: **If I stood before GOD without any explanation at all, what would remain?**

**Verse reference: 11:5.**

## 8. The Moral Weight of the Inner World

### Verse

> **2:284** — To GOD belongs everything in the heavens and the earth. Whether you declare your innermost thoughts, or keep them hidden, GOD holds you responsible for them. He forgives whomever He wills, and punishes whomever He wills. GOD is Omnipotent.

### Reflection on the Verse

2:284 makes clear that the inner world is not morally irrelevant. Yet caution is necessary. We should not go beyond the verse and treat every passing mental association as though it necessarily belongs to the same category.

The wording focuses on the **innermost thoughts**—what a person carries inwardly and turns toward, rather than every fleeting idea.

This calls me more to seriousness than to panic. Morality is not only outward performance. There is also a hidden life: what I want when no one sees, how I react inwardly to another person’s success, what I would do if I had power, whether I can do good without demanding return.

Outward action matters, but the Quran does not reduce the human being to the outer shell.

**Verse reference: 2:284.**

## 9. The Distance Between What Is in the Heart and What Appears Outside

### Verse

> **33:51** — You  may  gently  shun  any  one  of them,  and  you  may  bring  closer  to you  any  one  of  them.  If  you  reconcile with  any  one  you  had  estranged,  you commit  no  error.  In  this  way,  they will  be  pleased,  will  have  no  grief, and  will  be  content  with  what  you equitably  offer  to  all  of  them.  GOD knows  what  is  in  your  hearts.  GOD is Omniscient, Clement.

> **33:54** — Whether  you  declare  anything,  or hide  it,  GOD  is  fully  aware  of  all things.

### Reflection on the Verse

Knowing that GOD knows what is in the heart makes the inward side of human relationships morally serious as well.

I may behave fairly while carrying another calculation inside. I may be gentle because I am genuinely compassionate, or because gentleness helps me avoid conflict, gain affection, or preserve a good image.

Two actions may look identical from the outside while their inward directions differ. Other people may never fully know the difference—and perhaps they do not need to. GOD knows.

That is why purity of intention cannot itself become a performance. The moment I say, “Look how sincere I am,” another audience may already have entered the sincerity. Real inner work is often quiet.

**Verse references: 33:51, 33:54.**

## 10. The Mouth Can Say “We Believe” While the Heart Is Elsewhere

### Verse

> **5:41** — O  you  messenger,  do  not  be  saddened  by  those  who  hasten  to  disbelieve  among  those  who  say,  “We believe,”  with  their  mouths,  while their  hearts  do  not  believe.  Among the  Jews,  some  listened  to  lies.  They listened  to  people  who  never  met you,  and  who  distorted  the  words out  of  context,  then  said,  “If  you  are given  this,  accept  it,  but  if  you  are given  anything  different,  beware.” Whomever  GOD  wills  to  divert,  you can  do  nothing  to  help  him  against GOD. GOD does not wish to cleanse their  hearts.  They  have  incurred humiliation  in  this  world,  and  in  the Hereafter,  they  will  suffer  a  terrible retribution.

### Reflection on the Verse

The mouth and the heart do not always say the same thing. This is one of the clearest warnings about intention.

Religious sentences are easy to form: “I believe,” “I am doing this for GOD,” “I am defending truth.” Words alone do not establish whether those claims are true.

More revealing questions may be these: Do I accept truth when it harms my interest? Do I submit when GOD’s command challenges my habit? Do I still do what I believe is right when people do not admire me for it?

The direction of the heart may become clearest not in comfort, but in moments of tension.

**Verse reference: 5:41.**

## 11. Showing Off Changes the Direction of Intention

### Verse

> **4:142** — The  hypocrites  think  that  they  are deceiving  GOD,  but  He  is  the  One who  leads  them  on.  When  they  get up  for  the  Contact  Prayer  (Salat), they  get  up  lazily.  That  is  because they  only  show  off  in  front  of  the people,  and  rarely  do  they  think  of GOD.

### Reflection on the Verse

From the outside there is worship. The verse, however, opens the direction behind the action: **showing off before people.**

What is disturbing here is that a correct outward act can be attached to the wrong center. The same movement can be performed, the same words spoken, the same ritual observed, while the answer to “for whom?” changes the inner meaning.

Showing off need not appear only in dramatic religious display. It can take the form of wanting to appear more knowledgeable, more pious, more moral, or even more detached than others.

So the search for the deepest intention may sometimes require a question deeper than “What did I do?”: **Whom did I want to see me doing it?**

**Verse reference: 4:142.**

## 12. Even GOD’s Name Can Be Used for Reputation

### Verse

> **2:224** — Do  not  subject  GOD’s  name  to  your casual  swearing,  that  you  may  appear  righteous,  pious,  or  to  attain credibility  among  the  people.  GOD is Hearer, Knower.

### Reflection on the Verse

This verse shows how subtly intention can be corrupted. A person can even use GOD’s name as a means of gaining credibility among people.

The outer expression may be religious while the inward aim is reputation. That makes me examine my own religious speech: when I speak about GOD, am I truly turning toward GOD, or am I trying to appear serious, pure, learned, or trustworthy?

Religious language can give the ego powerful legitimacy. One test of the deepest intention may therefore be: **Is GOD’s name my end, or my instrument?**

Am I seeking GOD, or trying to obtain something else by speaking about GOD?

**Verse reference: 2:224.**

## 13. Seeking GOD’s Pleasure

### Verse

> **30:38** — Therefore, you shall give the relatives their rightful share (of charity), as well as the poor, and the traveling alien. This is better for those who sincerely seek GOD’s pleasure; they are the winners.

> **30:39** — The  usury  that  is  practiced  to  increase some people’s wealth, does not gain anything at GOD. But if you give to charity, seeking GOD’s pleasure,  these  are  the  ones  who receive their reward manifold.

### Reflection on the Verse

Here a positive standard appears: **seeking GOD’s pleasure.** This may be one of the simplest Quranic answers to the question of deepest intention.

Why am I doing this? Am I truly seeking GOD’s pleasure?

The striking point is that the verses look inside an action that already appears good from the outside: giving charity. Good action itself is therefore not treated as separate from inward direction.

I may help someone, yet if I expect loyalty, influence, gratitude, or reputation in return, a tension remains between the outward good and the inward aim.

Seeking GOD’s pleasure may also mean refusing to turn goodness into a debt that another person owes me.

**Verse references: 30:38, 30:39.**

## 14. Acting Without Expecting Reward or Thanks

### Verse

> **76:8** — They  donate  their  favorite  food  to the  poor,  the  orphan,  and  the  captive.

> **76:9** — “We  feed  you  for  the  sake  of  GOD; we  expect  no  reward  from  you,  nor thanks.

### Reflection on the Verse

These verses provide a powerful mirror for intention: **“we expect no reward from you, nor thanks.”**

One of the most honest tests of why I do good may be the disappearance of return. Would I still do it if no one thanked me? If no one knew? If the person never repaid me? If the person even forgot me?

If I become deeply angry when there is no recognition, perhaps what appeared free carried an invisible price: praise, loyalty, gratitude, status.

A person may ask for no money while still expecting payment in other currencies. 76:9 breaks that hidden transaction. Good done for the sake of GOD is not meant to place the recipient in debt to us.

That may be a form of freedom in intention.

**Verse references: 76:8, 76:9.**

## 15. Devoting the Religion Absolutely to GOD Alone

### Verse

> **39:11** — Say,  “I  have  been  commanded  to worship  GOD,  devoting  the  religion absolutely to Him alone.

> **39:12** — “And  I  was  commanded  to  be  the utmost submitter.”

> **39:13** — Say,  “I  fear,  if  I  disobeyed  my  Lord, the retribution of a great day.”

> **39:14** — Say,  “GOD  is  the  only  One  I  worship,  devoting  my  religion  absolutely to Him alone.

### Reflection on the Verse

“Devoting the religion absolutely to Him alone.” The phrase radically simplifies the center of intention.

Human approval becomes secondary. Social status becomes secondary. Group belonging becomes secondary. Ego becomes secondary. GOD is the center.

It is easy to say and much harder to live. The reaction of visible people can feel stronger than an unseen Hereafter. Human praise arrives immediately; GOD’s pleasure cannot be measured by our eyes.

Perhaps this is part of the difficulty of sincerity: moving away from the visible audience and making the unseen GOD the real center.

From the perspective of oneness, this gathers life around one center instead of many competing audiences.

**Verse references: 39:11, 39:12, 39:13, 39:14.**

## 16. To Whom Am I Devoting My Whole Life?

### Verse

> **6:162** — Say,  “My  Contact  Prayers  (Salat), my  worship  practices,  my  life  and my  death,  are  all  devoted  absolutely to  GOD  alone,  the  Lord  of  the  universe.

> **6:163** — “He  has  no  partner.  This  is  what  I am commanded  to believe,  and  I  am the first to submit.”

> **6:164** — Say,  “Shall  I  seek  other  than  GOD as a lord, when He is the Lord of all things?  No  soul  benefits  except from  its  own  works,  and  none  bears the  burden  of  another.  Ultimately, you  return  to  your  Lord,  then  He informs  you  regarding  all  your  disputes.”

### Reflection on the Verse

Intention is not merely a sentence spoken before isolated actions. 6:162 gives a much larger frame: worship, life, and death are all devoted to the same center.

This suggests that the deepest intention may be broader than a momentary decision. What is the basic direction of my life? What am I ultimately seeking? To whom do I want to return? By what standard do I measure success?

Beneath many smaller motives there may be a deeper one: to be valued in human eyes, to feel secure in the world, to possess control, to build the life the ego desires—or to devote life truly to GOD.

The deepest intention may therefore concern not only why I performed one action, but around what center my life turns.

**Verse references: 6:162, 6:163, 6:164.**

## 17. Can Distress Reveal the Real Intention?

### Verse

> **10:22** — He is the One who moves you across the land and sea. You get onto the ships, and they sail smoothly in nice breeze. Then, while rejoicing therein, violent wind blows, and the waves surround them from every side. This is when they implore GOD, sincerely devoting their prayers to Him alone: “If You only save us this time, we will be eternally appreciative.”

> **10:23** — But as soon as He saves them, they transgress on earth, and oppose the truth. O people, your transgression is only to the detriment of your own souls. You remain preoccupied with this worldly life, then to us is your ultimate return, then we inform you of everything you had done.

### Reflection on the Verse

Crisis can strip away layers. Control disappears, reputation loses importance, material power becomes insufficient, and the person turns directly to GOD.

But 10:23 leaves an important question: what happens after the danger passes?

We cannot conclude that every prayer in crisis is false. The verse does not make such a generalization. Yet continuity can reveal something about intention.

Did I want GOD, or only rescue? Did I turn toward GOD Himself, or treat Him as a means of solving my immediate need?

Perhaps the depth of intention becomes visible after the blessing arrives. When the need ends, what I remain attached to can reveal how lasting the inward direction truly was.

**Verse references: 10:22, 10:23.**

## 18. The Inner Direction of Perfect Religion: Absolute Devotion

### Verse

> **98:5** — All that was asked of them was to worship  GOD,  devoting  the  religion absolutely  to  Him  alone,  observe the  contact  prayers  (Salat),  and  give the  obligatory  charity  (Zakat).  Such is the perfect religion. 

### Reflection on the Verse

The verse lists acts of worship, but before doing so it defines their inward direction: **devoting the religion absolutely to GOD alone.**

That order deserves attention. A person may perform religious practices while still building other centers: human opinion, social status, acceptance by a group, the feeling of superiority, or the image of being a “good person.”

98:5 returns the center to GOD.

Purifying the deepest intention is therefore not only about removing bad motives. It has a positive direction: **making GOD truly central.**

Perhaps pure intention is not the absence of all purpose. It is the unification of purpose.

**Verse reference: 98:5.**

## 19. Arabic Word Roots: How Does 4:63 Express the “Innermost Intention”?

### Verse

> **4:63** — GOD  is  fully  aware  of  their  innermost  intentions.  You  shall  ignore them,  enlighten  them,  and  give  them good  advice  that  may  save  their souls.

### Reflection on the Verse

Looking closely at the Arabic of 4:63 reveals an important detail.

The part rendered in Rashad Khalifa’s **Authorized English Translation** as **“innermost intentions”** is **مَا فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ — mā fī qulūbihim**, literally pointing toward “what is in their hearts.” There is not a separate standalone Arabic word for “intention” in this clause; the Authorized English Translation renders what is in their hearts, in context, as “innermost intentions.” The Turkish text used alongside it is the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation; it is not itself an authorized translation.

**قُلُوب — q-l-b (ق ل ب):** the plural of *qalb*, heart. The root field carries senses of turning and change. In Quranic usage, the heart repeatedly appears as a center of inner orientation, understanding, and attitude.

**يَعْلَمُ — ʿ-l-m (ع ل م):** to know. The central claim is that GOD knows what is within the heart.

**أَنفُسِهِمْ — n-f-s (ن ف س):** their selves, souls, inner persons. The continuation of the verse points toward speech that reaches them inwardly.

**وَعِظْهُمْ — w-ʿ-ẓ (و ع ظ):** to admonish, counsel, awaken moral attention.

**قَوْلًا — q-w-l (ق و ل):** speech, saying.

**بَلِيغًا — b-l-gh (ب ل غ):** connected with reaching; here it carries the sense of effective speech that reaches its target.

Taken together, the movement is striking: GOD knows what is in the heart, yet the person is not simply abandoned. There is enlightenment, admonition, and speech meant to reach the inner self.

So the statement “GOD knows my deepest intention” need not become only a source of fear. The heart cannot be hidden, but it can be changed.

**Verse reference: 4:63.**

## Other Claims

### Verse

> **4:62** — How  will  it  be  when  a  disaster  hits them,  as  a  consequence  of  their  own works?  They  will  come  to  you  then and  swear  by  GOD:  “Our  intentions were good and righteous!”

> **4:63** — GOD  is  fully  aware  of  their  innermost  intentions.  You  shall  ignore them,  enlighten  them,  and  give  them good  advice  that  may  save  their souls.

### Reflection on the Verse

Two opposite claims can be made about intention.

The first is, **“If my intention is good, the outcome does not really matter.”** 4:62-63 does not allow such an easy defense. People can say, “Our intentions were good and righteous,” yet the next verse states that GOD fully knows their innermost intentions. A person’s own claim of good intention is therefore not the final measure.

The second extreme is, **“Only intention matters; the action itself is unimportant.”** The verses used in this article do not support that either. 30:38-39 speaks of giving, 76:8-9 of feeding, 98:5 of Salat and Zakat, and 6:162 of worship practices. Inner direction and visible action are not rivals.

The more balanced picture is this: action matters, and the direction of the action matters. An outwardly good deed can be attached to showing off; likewise, a claim of sincerity cannot automatically turn a wrong action into a right one.

Intention is therefore not an excuse that replaces action. It is the inward direction of action that must be examined honestly before GOD.

**Verse references: 4:62-63, 30:38-39, 76:8-9, 98:5, 6:162.**

## Conclusion

### Verse

> **5:89** — GOD does not hold you responsible for the mere utterance of oaths; He holds you responsible for your actual intentions. If you violate an oath, you shall atone by feeding ten poor people from the same food you offer to your own family, or clothing them, or by freeing a slave. If you cannot afford this, then you shall fast three days. This is the atonement for violating the oaths that you swore to keep. You shall fulfill your oaths. GOD thus explains His revelations to you, that you may be appreciative.

### Reflection on the Verse

Returning to the opening question: **What is the deepest intention?**

I do not want to force the Quranic language into a single technical sentence. But the verses point in a powerful direction.

The deepest intention is not merely the purpose a person announces. It is the direction truly carried in the heart. It may lie even beneath what we consciously hide from others, because a person can sometimes mislead himself before misleading anyone else.

“I meant well,” while really wanting to be seen. “I defended truth,” while really wanting to win. “I helped,” while expecting gratitude. “I did it for GOD,” while also protecting status in human eyes.

Recognizing such mixture does not necessarily mean that every good act becomes worthless. Human beings are complex, and motives can be mixed. The task is not to sanctify that mixture, but to purify it.

The question that remains for me at the end of this article is:

**What would I do if every audience disappeared?**

If no one praised me, thanked me, knew my name, noticed how much I knew, or heard about my sacrifice? If the act brought me no status, belonging, or reputation? If only GOD remained as witness?

Would I still do it?

There is an even deeper question: **What do I want from GOD?** Only His gifts? Only rescue? Only solutions to my problems? Or His pleasure itself?

30:38-39 speaks of seeking GOD’s pleasure. 76:9 shows giving for the sake of GOD without demanding reward or thanks. 39:11-14 speaks of devoting religion absolutely to Him alone. 6:162 gathers life and death into devotion to GOD. 98:5 places that absolute devotion at the heart of perfect religion.

Read together, these verses suggest that the purest intention is not merely the absence of a bad motive. It is something deeper: **the direction of the self turning toward GOD.**

On one side the ego seeks reputation, control, recognition, security, superiority, or return. On the other side the person seeks GOD’s pleasure. These directions can become mixed throughout life.

For that reason, intention may not be a one-time decision but a direction that must be examined repeatedly.

Before or after an important action, perhaps a few questions are enough: Why did I really do this? Am I expecting return? Do I want to be seen? Do I want truth, or do I want to be right? Am I seeking GOD’s pleasure, or human approval? Would I still do it if no one knew?

And most importantly:

**Who is at the deepest center of my heart?**

I may hide the answer from people. For a while I may even hide it from myself. But not from GOD.

That reality is not only frightening. GOD knows not merely the version of me I can present to others, but also the confusion I struggle to explain to myself. He knows the wrong motive, the desire to correct it, and the search for sincerity.

After stating in 4:63 that GOD knows the innermost intentions, the verse still speaks of enlightenment, admonition, and speech that may save the soul. Seeing the true inward direction is therefore not necessarily the end of the road. It may be the beginning.

**The deepest intention is the real direction of the heart that GOD already knows and that the human being must learn to examine honestly.**

And, as far as these verses allow us to say, the most beautiful direction is for life to become increasingly devoted to GOD alone.
