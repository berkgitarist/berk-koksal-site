## 1. Seeking the Names, Encountering the Limits of Knowledge

### Verse

**2:31**

**Arabic**

<blockquote data-verse="2:31" data-language="ar">
<p lang="ar" dir="rtl">وَعَلَّمَ ءَادَمَ ٱلْأَسْمَآءَ كُلَّهَا ثُمَّ عَرَضَهُمْ عَلَى ٱلْمَلَٰٓئِكَةِ فَقَالَ أَنۢبِـُٔونِى بِأَسْمَآءِ هَٰٓؤُلَآءِ إِن كُنتُمْ صَٰدِقِينَ</p>
</blockquote>

**English — Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation**

<blockquote data-verse="2:31" data-language="en">
<p lang="en">He  taught  Adam  all  the  names* then presented  them to the angels, saying, “Give me the names of these, if you are right.”</p>
</blockquote>

**Turkish — the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation**

<blockquote data-verse="2:31" data-language="tr">
<p lang="tr">O, Âdem’e bütün isimleri* öğretti, sonra  “Eğer haklıysanız, bunların isimlerini bana bildirin” diyerek onları meleklere de sundu.</p>
</blockquote>

Source: <a href="https://kuranteyit.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">kuranteyit.com</a>

### Reflection on the Verse

What did Adam know that the angels did not? I understand the attraction of the question. It can seem as though finding that list of names would give us a key to the entire mystery of being human. Yet before 2:31, I need to slow my curiosity down. None of the names is individually listed here. The giving of knowledge is declared; its complete inventory is not disclosed to us.

The answer established by 2:31–33 is clear within these limits: Adam is taught the names; the angels are asked to give the names of what is presented to them; they acknowledge that they have no knowledge except what they have been taught; and Adam makes the names known to them. The answer the angels cannot give in this encounter is one Adam can give through the knowledge taught to him. Which language was used, how the teaching took place, and precisely which names were involved are details these verses do not explain.

That distinction does not end research. It allows research to recognize what it actually knows. **Knowing that knowledge was given does not mean that we ourselves possess all of its contents.**

In my earlier essay, <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/the_teaching_of_names_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">“The Teaching of Names: Does Naming a Thing Mean Knowing It?”</a>, I considered the distance between a name and knowledge. Here I approach that distance from another side: What do I do when I discover that I do not know? Do I turn toward learning more, or close the gap with an impressive explanation?

Two elements of this encounter matter together to me: Adam being taught and the angels acknowledging the limits of their knowledge. One brings the value of learning before me; the other, the honesty of owning what I do not know.

**Verse references:** 2:31; 2:32; 2:33.

## 2. The Question That Comes Before the Names

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="2:30" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>2:30</strong> — Recall  that  your  Lord  said  to  the angels,  “I  am  placing  a  representative (a temporary god) on Earth.” They said, “Will You place therein one who will spread evil therein and shed blood, while we sing Your praises,  glorify  You,  and  uphold Your  absolute  authority?”  He  said, “I know what you do not know.”</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

The teaching of names is not an isolated vocabulary contest. It is preceded by a conversation about placement on Earth, evil, and bloodshed. Alongside the concern the angels express stands GOD’s answer that He knows what they do not know. The weight of the next scene emerges within this difference in knowledge.

It may be tempting to draw an immediate conclusion: Because human beings know names, their wrongdoing no longer matters. But 2:30–33 does not present such a moral calculation. Knowledge of names is not offered as payment to offset bloodshed. Possessing knowledge does not turn evil into good; nor does the limit revealed to the angels justify every human action.

For the same reason, I cannot reconstruct from this passage an earlier history explaining how the angels knew about the possibility of evil. I know what they say in the verse; I do not know the whole background that enabled them to say it. When I treat an unexplained past as certain, I risk turning an account of the limits of knowledge into material for my own refusal of limits.

What unsettles me most is that genuinely recognizing a danger does not mean that I understand the entire design. My partial knowledge may be correct without being comprehensive. These are not contradictory possibilities.

The difference in knowledge expressed in 2:30 returns at the end of 2:33. The encounter therefore concerns not only what Adam knows, but also whose knowledge remains encompassing throughout the conversation.

**Verse references:** 2:30; 2:31; 2:33.

## 3. Teaching, Presenting, Making Known

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="2:33" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>2:33</strong> — He  said,  “O  Adam,  tell  them  their names.”  When  he  told  them  their names,  He  said,  “Did  I  not  tell  you that  I  know  the  secrets  of  the  heavens  and  the  earth?  I  know  what  you declare, and what you conceal.”</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

There is a movement between 2:31 and 2:33 worth attending to. Knowledge is taught, called upon in an encounter, and then communicated to others. What has been learned does not remain a private claim; it appears as knowledge with which the requested answer can be given.

I try not to collapse its stages. Hearing the sound of a name is one thing; identifying by name what is being asked about is another. The account does not say that arbitrary sounds are put before Adam for him to repeat to others. Nor does it provide a detailed account of his mental operations. I need neither diminish the reality of the knowledge taught nor invent the mechanism of learning that remains unexplained.

The difference between giving a name and making one up also matters here. Adam does not announce an independent authority over beings. He makes known what he has been taught. Learning and communicating do not turn him into the absolute source of knowledge.

I encounter a related question when I prepare a work or put a thought into writing: Do I genuinely know what I can say? Because I can carry someone else’s sentence, do I assume that I also carry its justification? This is a question I direct toward the accountability of my own speech, not toward casting doubt on Adam’s knowledge.

In 2:33, Adam’s making the names known is expressly narrated. The correctness of my own answer, however, cannot be established merely by my sounding confident. Knowledge confirmed within the account and a claim I make today do not carry the same assurance.

**Verse references:** 2:31; 2:33.

## 4. What the Angels Did Not Know Was Not GOD’s Existence

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="2:32" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>2:32</strong> — They  said,  “Be  You  glorified,  we have  no  knowledge,  except  that which  You  have  taught  us.  You  are the Omniscient, Most Wise.”</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

To read the angels’ inability to give the names as meaning that they know nothing would distort the conversation before us. In 2:30, they address GOD and speak of glorifying Him and upholding His absolute authority. In 2:32, they recognize to whom knowledge and wisdom belong. A limit in their knowledge is not the absence of all knowledge.

Their answer in 2:32 also contains an exception: what they have been taught. Rather than making ignorance absolute, it relates knowledge to its source. I therefore cannot use this scene to conclude that angels are incapable of learning, incapable of understanding, or merely producers of sound.

Their witness to GOD’s oneness also appears in 3:18. Reading these contexts together reminds me not to confuse knowing the answer to a particular question with knowing GOD. The knowledge absent in the naming encounter does not mean that they possess no knowledge of faith.

The same care can affect how I look at people. Someone’s unfamiliarity with a concept I know is not evidence that the person knows nothing. When I make my own field of expertise the measure of someone’s entire worth, the pursuit of knowledge can become a display of superiority. This is not a classification of people given by the verse; it is a question I ask myself while reading about the angels’ limit in a particular encounter.

I therefore keep the question precise: Here the angels cannot give the names requested of them. To pronounce a broader incapacity, I would have to add what the account does not say.

**Verse references:** 2:30; 2:31; 2:32; 3:18.

## 5. The Distance Between Having No Answer and Inventing One

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="2:32" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>2:32</strong> — They  said,  “Be  You  glorified,  we have  no  knowledge,  except  that which  You  have  taught  us.  You  are the Omniscient, Most Wise.”</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

When I read 2:32, what I notice before the missing names is the absence of a fabricated answer. The angels do not present knowledge they lack as though they possess it. They do not turn the question into a blank that must be filled to preserve their standing.

There is a simplicity here that challenges me. When someone asks a question, especially in an area where I want to consider myself knowledgeable, I may feel the urge to answer immediately. My investigation may be insufficient, but my mind produces a possible explanation. I can then overlook the distance between an explanation appearing coherent and being true.

The angels’ answer makes me consider why a gap in knowledge should not be covered with a gap in honesty. **What I do not know may be an area in which I still need to learn. What I present as known despite not knowing it may mislead someone else as well.** The moral weight of these situations is not the same.

In <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/the_most_argumentative_creature_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">“The Most Argumentative Creature: The Difference Between Seeking Truth and Needing to Be Right”</a>, I considered how the need to appear right can narrow inquiry. Here, even winning an argument is unnecessary. Sometimes simply being unable to tolerate having no answer can push me into saying what I do not actually know.

The openness of 2:32 does not close the door to learning. It identifies where sound learning can begin. When I acknowledge where I stand, I can consider new knowledge without first having to defend an old claim. Admitting what is missing is not abandoning truth. It is choosing to adjust myself to truth rather than adjusting truth to myself.

**Verse references:** 2:31; 2:32.

## 6. Adam Makes the Names Known; Who Knows the Secrets?

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="2:33" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>2:33</strong> — He  said,  “O  Adam,  tell  them  their names.”  When  he  told  them  their names,  He  said,  “Did  I  not  tell  you that  I  know  the  secrets  of  the  heavens  and  the  earth?  I  know  what  you declare, and what you conceal.”</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="20:110" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>20:110</strong> — He  knows  their  past  and  their  future,  while  none  encompasses  His knowledge.</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

In 2:33, it is important not to lose track of who is speaking and acting. Adam makes the names known. GOD declares His knowledge of the secrets of the heavens and the earth, of what is disclosed and concealed. These two levels of knowledge appear within the same verse, but they are not attributed to the same person.

I therefore cannot turn the completeness expressed in 2:31 into unlimited knowledge. Being taught all the names is not a statement that Adam knows every truth, every future event, and every heart without exception. The inability to encompass GOD’s knowledge in 20:110 is another important boundary here. Something being taught to me and my encompassing GOD’s knowledge are different claims.

Two different unknowns also need to be distinguished. The first is the names the angels cannot give in that encounter. The second is the wider domain of secrets GOD declares that He knows. The naming scene does not say that this entire second domain is opened and given to everyone. Demonstrating a particular difference in knowledge does not abolish every unknown.

I find this relevant to my own research. Once I learn the root of a concept, I may treat it as settled across every part of life. Having identified a cause, I may assume I also know every consequence. Yet opening one door does not mean that every room has been explored.

Accepting that I do not encompass GOD’s knowledge does not render what I know worthless. It prevents me from giving it more authority than it deserves. I can work with it and use it to correct things. Where it does not reach, however, I should not manufacture a certainty that pronounces judgment in its place.

**Verse references:** 2:31; 2:33; 20:110.

## 7. Is This Scene an Intelligence Contest?

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="2:31" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>2:31</strong> — He  taught  Adam  all  the  names* then presented  them to the angels, saying, “Give me the names of these, if you are right.”</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

Adam knowing the names while the angels do not can initially look like a contest of superiority. But forgetting the starting condition would make the comparison misleading: Adam has been taught this knowledge. The result is not detached from that teaching.

I cannot infer from this that all human beings are more intelligent than all angels in every respect. Nor does this encounter establish that angels are worth less or that human beings are naturally superior in every field of knowledge. The giving of particular knowledge and a comparison of every attribute of two kinds of being are not the same inquiry.

Care is also needed before framing the issue as the fairness of a test. The account does not describe a competition designed to compare the independent abilities of participants given the same education. Explaining the disclosure of a limit in knowledge through the conditions of a human examination requires additional assumptions. Moreover, 2:32 does not say that the angels are punished for being unable to give the requested names.

My concern is not to diminish Adam’s learned knowledge. It is to avoid making it the foundation of a generalization it does not warrant. A person may be given knowledge of a particular matter. That does not mean the person must be followed in every other matter.

I can make a similar mistake in my own life. Solving a musical problem does not automatically make my judgment in another field correct. Building functioning software does not exempt a religious inference from examination. The question I draw from 2:31–33 is precisely this: Am I substituting the particular thing I know for everything I do not?

**Verse references:** 2:31; 2:32; 2:33.

## 8. A Limit in Knowledge Is Not the Same as Arrogance

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="2:34" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>2:34</strong> — When  we  said  to  the  angels,  “Fall prostrate  before  Adam,”  they  fell prostrate,  except  Satan;  he  refused, was too arrogant, and a disbeliever.</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

I do not want to confuse two attitudes placed close together. In 2:32, the angels acknowledge the limits of their knowledge. In 2:34, Satan’s refusal, arrogance, and disbelief are described. The first is an honest statement about knowledge not possessed. The second is a refusal of a command. These are not the same condition.

This distinction changes how I look at not knowing. It is easy to belittle someone for lacking information; it is harder to consider how a person acts despite what is known. I may have much to say, use the correct concepts, and still remain closed to correction. The breadth of my vocabulary does not by itself show that I am free of arrogance.

The angels’ prostration before Adam is not described as their declaring him the independent source of knowledge, either. The verse contains a command and a response to it. I cannot extend this into a requirement of absolute allegiance to any knowledgeable human being.

The question I ask myself is more ordinary, but difficult: Can I accept something true when it comes from someone unlike me? Or do I feel that learning would weaken my standing? Sometimes my reason for rejecting knowledge may not be insufficient evidence, but my reluctance to yield before the person expressing it.

Between the openness of 2:32 and the arrogance of 2:34 lies a distance against which I can examine the direction of my heart. Acknowledging what I do not know does not diminish me. Acting as though I cannot be wrong does not enlarge my knowledge.

**Verse references:** 2:32; 2:34.

## 9. Why Did Knowing All the Names Not Prevent Error?

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="2:35" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>2:35</strong> — We  said,  “O  Adam,  live  with  your wife  in  Paradise,  and  eat  therefrom generously,  as  you  please,  but  do  not approach this tree, lest you sin.”</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="2:36" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>2:36</strong> — But  the  devil  duped  them,  and caused  their  eviction  therefrom.  We said,  “Go  down  as  enemies  of  one another.  On  Earth  shall  be  your habitation  and  provision  for  awhile.”</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="20:115" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>20:115</strong> — We  tested  Adam  in  the  past,  but  he forgot, and we found him indecisive.</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

The account does not end with the names. A boundary is given to Adam and his wife; deception and eviction from Paradise follow. In 20:115, Adam’s forgetting and being found indecisive are mentioned. Read alongside 2:31, these passages show me that learned knowledge does not automatically secure conduct.

This does not mean the names were unnecessary. Knowledge need not make a person infallible in every circumstance in order to have value. Yet it cannot be worn as a certificate of moral immunity. Knowing something, remembering it, holding to it when confronted by a desire, and making the right choice can come apart.

For me, this is even more personal than debating the extent of the names. Why might I want to conceal a mistake despite being able to explain honesty? Why can letting go of control remain difficult despite my being able to speak about submission? Possessing the word does not show that the conduct it calls for is complete in me.

Being taught the names did not mean that Adam would no longer need guidance. The very presence of the command in 2:35 shows that responsibility for direction continues after knowledge. Learning is not independence from limits.

I should see myself here not only beside Adam as a learner, but also in the position of the human being who forgets and errs. Otherwise, I take a claim of superiority from the names while excluding the continuation that examines my pride. Refusing to count learning and living rightly as the same achievement does not diminish knowledge. It removes the false guarantee I have attached to it.

**Verse references:** 2:31; 2:35; 2:36; 20:115.

## 10. The One Who Knows the Names Still Needs Words of Return

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="2:37" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>2:37</strong> — Then,  Adam  received  from  his  Lord words,  whereby  He  redeemed  him. He is the Redeemer, Most Merciful.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="7:23" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>7:23</strong> — They  said,  “Our  Lord,  we  have wronged  our  souls,  and  unless  You forgive  us  and  have  mercy  on  us,  we will be losers.”</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

In 2:31, Adam is taught names. In 2:37, he receives words from his Lord and is redeemed. Between these two moments, knowledge acquires a significance that is not exhausted by recognizing the world outside us: A human being also needs to return to the right place before his own wrongdoing.

I am not declaring the names and the words identical. The contents of the words received in 2:37 are not individually given in that verse. In 7:23, however, Adam and his wife acknowledge their own wrongdoing and ask for forgiveness and mercy. Considering the two passages together helps me approach words through a truthful orientation rather than treating them first as a hidden technique.

Naming something can sometimes allow me to hold it at a distance. I can speak of error, arrogance, or injustice and identify them all in other people’s conduct. Acknowledging that I have wronged my own soul brings the word home. I am no longer only the person offering an explanation; I also stand before it.

That is why receiving again in 2:37 makes me feel that learning has not ended. Knowledge given earlier does not eliminate a later need for correction and mercy. When I recognize an error, I should not turn my earlier work, creative output, or knowledge into a protective shield.

What I want this inquiry into the teaching of names to produce in me is not more impressive speech. It is the ability, when necessary, to form a truer sentence: one that admits the possibility of my error, does not transfer my wrongdoing to someone else, and turns toward GOD’s mercy.

**Verse references:** 2:31; 2:37; 7:23.

## 11. Does Being Taught Make My Own Effort Unnecessary?

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="16:78" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>16:78</strong> — GOD brought you out of your mothers’ bellies knowing nothing, and He gave you the hearing, the eyesight, and the brains, that you may be appreciative.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="96:4" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>96:4</strong> — Teaches by means of the pen. </p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="96:5" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>96:5</strong> — He teaches man what he never knew. </p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

I cannot read that Adam was taught the names and then assume that all knowledge is already present in every human being. In 16:78, our own beginning is described through our emergence into the world knowing nothing. Hearing, eyesight, and the brain are mentioned alongside appreciation. My task is not to turn the opportunity to learn into a claim that I already know everything.

The association of teaching with the pen in 96:4–5 matters too. These verses give no reason to suppose that GOD teaching makes tools, effort, and the process of learning meaningless. On the contrary, teaching is explicitly connected with a means. In 17:36, responsibility for using the faculties given to us is recalled.

Of course, this does not establish that Adam was taught the names through a pen. Turning different contexts into a single teaching mechanism would be another claim. The connection I can draw is that knowledge being given does not exclude learning and responsibility.

I find both a spiritual and a practical significance here. Correcting a mistake may require me to read again, compare, and try. When I finally understand, I do not want to treat that understanding solely as evidence of my own greatness. But I cannot put appreciation in the place of work, either.

Taking knowledge’s dependence on GOD seriously is not a reason to leave my eyes, ears, and mind unused. It makes the question of what I do with what has been given more demanding. Learning is not merely possessing more; it is trying to do justice to the opportunity I possess.

**Verse references:** 2:31; 16:78; 96:4; 96:5; 17:36.

## 12. Distinguishing: Carrying Knowledge More Honestly

<p lang="en"><strong>55:3</strong> — Creator of the human beings.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="55:4" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>55:4</strong> — He taught them how to distinguish.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="20:114" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>20:114</strong> — Most  Exalted  is  GOD,  the  only true  King.  Do  not  rush  into  uttering the  Quran  before  it  is  revealed  to you,  and  say,  “My  Lord,  increase  my knowledge.”</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

In 55:1–4, mercy, teaching, creation, and distinguishing follow one another. Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation renders 55:4 in terms of distinguishing. Looking back at the names, I consider whether preserving the differences between things that appear similar is itself part of learning.

I need to distinguish taught knowledge from conjecture, the narration of an event from my interpretation of it, and recognizing a word from claiming to know its whole scope. Without such distinctions, every connection in my mind may begin to look equally certain. Many things will then be connected, but what is actually known will become unclear.

The teaching in 2:31 and the teaching in 55:4 are not presented as two accounts of the same event. I therefore do not use distinguishing as a definition that fills in the missing inventory of names. The reflective connection I draw is that knowledge calls a person toward more attentive understanding.

The placement of not rushing beside asking for increased knowledge in 20:114 also makes me pause. Waiting is not abandoning learning. Sometimes learning well requires not announcing an answer that has not yet taken shape.

This is a patience I need in writing, too. Being able to form a sentence may not mean that I possess enough knowledge to warrant it. Between the angels’ acknowledgment in 2:32 and the request to learn in 20:114, I see a direction in which I can remain both humble and diligent.

**Verse references:** 2:31; 2:32; 55:1; 55:2; 55:3; 55:4; 20:114.

## 13. Reaching an Answer Is Not the Same as Verifying It

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="17:36" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>17:36</strong> — You  shall  not  accept  any  information,  unless  you  verify  it  for  yourself. I  have  given  you  the  hearing,  the eyesight,  and  the  brain,  and  you  are responsible for using them.</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

I want to meet every explanation of the names with the same question: How do I know this? Does the verse say it, does a commentary propose it, or is my mind completing it? The responsibility in 17:36 becomes especially important in subjects I am eager to learn about. Wanting an answer can also make me eager to accept one.

An interpretation being detailed does not mean that it is present in the verse. I might, for example, read a long account of the language in which the names were taught. It may flow well and its connections may seem mutually reinforcing. Yet 2:31–33 still does not name the language. I should not substitute the existence of an interpretation for its verification.

In <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/tr/writings/bulmak-baska-anlamak-baska-arama-motoru-caginda-ayet-gelistirilmis/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">“Finding Is One Thing, Understanding Is Another: The Verse in the Age of Search Engines” (Turkish original)</a>, I considered the distance between access and understanding. That distance becomes particularly concrete here: I can find hundreds of answers to a question without finding the list of names the verses do not supply. More answers do not necessarily provide the missing evidence.

This is the direction I want for KuranTeyit as well. An interface can place the verse, relevant words, and opportunities for comparison before me. It cannot automatically transform a connection I propose as a possibility into knowledge declared by GOD. A tool helps when it shows where further inquiry is needed, not when it covers every gap with an appearance of certainty.

Reading the angels’ answer requires me to see the limits of my own answers. I cannot leave the responsibility of 17:36 to someone else’s knowledge, a book’s reputation, or the fluency of software. Accepting an answer is also something I do.

**Verse references:** 2:31; 2:32; 2:33; 17:36.

## 14. When Knowledge Is Separated from Witness and Justice

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="3:18" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>3:18</strong> — GOD  bears  witness  that  there  is  no god except He, and so do the angels and  those  who  possess  knowledge. Truthfully  and  equitably,  He  is  the absolute  god;  there  is  no  god  but He, the Almighty, Most Wise.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="4:135" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>4:135</strong> — O  you who  believe,  you  shall  be absolutely  equitable,  and  observe GOD,  when  you  serve  as  witnesses, even  against  yourselves,  or  your parents,  or  your  relatives.  Whether the  accused  is  rich  or  poor,  GOD takes  care  of  both.  Therefore,  do not  be  biased  by  your  personal wishes.  If  you  deviate  or  disregard (this  commandment),  then  GOD  is fully  Cognizant  of  everything  you do.</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

In 3:18, angels and those who possess knowledge are mentioned together in bearing witness to GOD’s oneness. The limit in knowledge revealed in the naming scene does not appear here as an obstacle that abolishes witness. Taught knowledge and GOD’s absoluteness are not rivals. Knowing may call me to consider more carefully what, and to whom, I bear witness rather than to declare myself self-sufficient.

Yet I can easily deceive myself if witness remains only a grand declaration of belief. In 4:135, justice must stand even where I, my family, or those close to me are concerned. Knowledge is not something to value when it serves me and disregard when it does not.

If I conceal contrary evidence because I have adopted a view, I may have multiplied names while diminishing honesty. If love for someone leads me knowingly to misrepresent that person’s mistake, I turn knowledge into an instrument of partiality rather than faithfulness. Reading these two verses together makes remaining just before what I know as important to me as learning more.

In 2:33, GOD’s knowledge of what is disclosed and concealed is recalled. I am not exhausted by how truthful I appear outwardly. The reason I hide, the fact I leave unspoken because I dislike it, and the standard I relax for myself also stand before this reminder.

If knowledge is to bring me nearer to GOD, it should not make me more skillful at defending injustice in my life. My witness is tested not only in the names I know, but also in whom I favor when confronted by a truth I know.

**Verse references:** 2:32; 2:33; 3:18; 4:135.

## 15. Not Every Name Carries the Same Authority

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="53:23" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>53:23</strong> — These  are  but  names  that  you  made up,  you  and  your  forefathers.  GOD never  authorized  such  a  blasphemy. They  follow  conjecture,  and  personal  desire,  when  the  true  guidance  has  come  to  them  herein  from their Lord.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="7:180" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>7:180</strong> — To GOD belongs the most beautiful names; call upon Him therewith, and disregard those who distort His names. They will be requited for their sins.</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

In 2:31 there are names that are taught; in 53:23, names that are made up; in 7:180, the most beautiful names belonging to GOD. The occurrence of the same word in different contexts does not mean that each context concerns the same referents. What the names are attached to, and what is claimed through them, matters.

Verse 7:180 gives an explicit direction concerning GOD’s names. Its existence does not by itself prove that all the names taught to Adam in 2:31 were exclusively GOD’s names. Nor can I use 2:31 to attribute a hidden power to every name. Moving from a repeated word to an identical scope requires further grounds.

The problem in 53:23 is not people naming things in ordinary life. The verse condemns names connected with unauthorized blasphemy, conjecture, and personal desire. Naming a project and placing divine authority behind a name are different acts.

This distinction has a personal importance for me. I can give a research tool, a musical approach, or an essay a powerful name. That name cannot by itself guarantee that the thought behind it is correct. It may help a thought to be examined; it cannot replace the examination.

Turning to GOD through His names is one thing. Presenting names I have created as an endorsement from GOD is another. An inquiry into names should lead me not into their spell, but toward accountability for the authority I attach to them.

**Verse references:** 2:31; 53:23; 7:180.

## 16. Learning from a Teacher Without Making the Teacher Unlimited

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="18:65" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>18:65</strong> — They  found  one  of  our  servants, whom  we  blessed  with  mercy,  and bestowed  upon  him  from  our  own knowledge.</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="18:66" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>18:66</strong> — Moses  said  to  him,  “Can  I  follow you,  that  you  may  teach  me  some  of the  knowledge  and  the  guidance  bestowed upon you?”</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

The person Moses encounters is introduced as a servant upon whom knowledge has been bestowed. Moses asks to learn from that knowledge. Here is a relationship in which learning from another is not treated as degrading. Knowledge being given to someone does not diminish the person who wishes to learn it.

The connection I draw with Adam being taught the names is not an identity of every detail. In both accounts, knowledge is given. Just as Adam making the names known does not make him their absolute source, the presence of a servant able to teach Moses does not show that this servant encompasses all of GOD’s knowledge.

I see another path between belittling a teacher and making the teacher unlimited: learning, attending to the grounds of what is learned, and preserving the boundaries of the teacher’s knowledge. Recognizing that someone can help in one matter does not require making every judgment that person offers unquestionable. Respect for a teacher does not remove the responsibility in 17:36.

I want the same standard for my own writing. If I can help someone reflect on a verse, that should be enough. I cannot make submission to my conclusion a condition of submission to GOD. If I repeat 2:32 at the end of my essays while exempting my own interpretations from its limit, my words and my posture diverge.

The teaching of names does not remove me from the position of a learner; it reminds me of the dignity of learning. As my knowledge grows, I want to remain a more honest student rather than become another master.

**Verse references:** 2:31; 2:32; 2:33; 18:65; 18:66; 17:36.

## 17. Arabic Word Roots: Names, Teaching, Making Known, and Wisdom

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="2:31" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>2:31</strong> — He  taught  Adam  all  the  names* then presented  them to the angels, saying, “Give me the names of these, if you are right.”</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="2:32" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>2:32</strong> — They  said,  “Be  You  glorified,  we have  no  knowledge,  except  that which  You  have  taught  us.  You  are the Omniscient, Most Wise.”</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote data-verse="2:33" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>2:33</strong> — He  said,  “O  Adam,  tell  them  their names.”  When  he  told  them  their names,  He  said,  “Did  I  not  tell  you that  I  know  the  secrets  of  the  heavens  and  the  earth?  I  know  what  you declare, and what you conceal.”</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

**Names — ٱلْأَسْمَآءَ.** The word in 2:31 is the plural of اِسْم, meaning name. The Quranic Arabic Corpus classifies it under the root س م و. This lexical classification is not an inventory of the individual names taught in the verse. Knowledge of a root and knowledge of a referent’s full scope are not interchangeable. The adjoining كُلَّهَا emphasizes completeness, but emphasis does not disclose an inventory that has not been supplied. <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/wordmorphology.jsp?location=(2:31:3)" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">2:31: the word for names</a>.

**Knowing and teaching — ع ل م.** In 2:31, وَعَلَّمَ, and in 2:32, عِلْمَ, عَلَّمْتَنَآ, and ٱلْعَلِيمُ belong to the same root family. Teaching, knowledge, and being Omniscient are thus connected within the account. Sharing a root, however, does not remove the differences in subject and scope. The being taught is not in the same position as the Omniscient. <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/wordmorphology.jsp?location=(2:31:1)" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">2:31: the verb of teaching</a>.

**Presenting — ع ر ض.** The form عَرَضَهُمْ in 2:31 combines a verb of presenting with a plural object pronoun. Grammatical analysis identifies that structure. The form of the pronoun cannot, by itself, give us a complete list of those presented. Grammar can illuminate a question without producing every detail the account leaves unspecified. <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/wordmorphology.jsp?location=(2:31:6)" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">2:31: the verb of presenting</a>.

**Making known — ن ب أ.** The forms أَنۢبِـُٔونِى in 2:31 and أَنۢبِئْهُم and أَنۢبَأَهُم in 2:33 are related to this root. Making known is requested and then narrated as having taken place. I should not expand this action into a claim that Adam independently creates a being or rules over it absolutely. The names communicated remain connected to their having been taught. <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/wordmorphology.jsp?location=(2:31:10)" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">2:31: the verb of informing</a>.

**Truthfulness — ص د ق.** The concluding صَٰدِقِينَ in 2:31 belongs to this root. Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation has “if you are right”; the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation has “Eğer haklıysanız”. A claim is brought before the demand for knowledge. The root does not require us to insert an explanation absent from the verse. <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/wordmorphology.jsp?location=(2:31:15)" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">2:31: the word concerning truthfulness</a>.

**Secrets and wisdom — غ ي ب; ح ك م.** The words غَيْبَ in 2:33 and ٱلْحَكِيمُ in 2:32 make me consider why the account cannot be exhausted by an inventory of names. Knowing names remains distinct from comprehensively knowing what is hidden, and the source of knowledge is acknowledged together with wisdom. The reflection I draw is not a word game; it follows the differences in subject and knowledge established by the verses: to value what I have learned without filling the place of what I have not learned with myself. <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/wordmorphology.jsp?location=(2:33:14)" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">2:33: the word ghayb</a>; <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/wordmorphology.jsp?location=(2:32:12)" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">2:32: the word concerning wisdom</a>.

**Verse references:** 2:31; 2:32; 2:33.

## 18. Other Claims

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="2:31" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>2:31</strong> — He  taught  Adam  all  the  names* then presented  them to the angels, saying, “Give me the names of these, if you are right.”</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

The verse itself and its explanatory note need to be distinguished when considering the scope of the names. The note accompanying 2:31 in Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation reads as follows. This is an explanatory note, not the verse itself:

<blockquote class="translation-note">
<p>*2:31  These  are  the  names  of  the  animals,  the  automobile,  the  submarine,  the  space satellite, the VCR, and all other objects to be encountered by the human beings on Earth.</p>
</blockquote>

<a href="https://kuranteyit.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">2:31 and its explanatory note</a>.

The note takes a broad view of the names as covering objects human beings will encounter on Earth; its examples include later technological devices. That is the note’s explicit approach. Yet this explanation of names is not identical to the claim that Adam was taught the complete engineering, operating principles, and subsequent history of every device. That second claim would require a scope extending beyond the note itself.

Al-Tabari’s commentary on 2:31 records different accounts: the names of all beings, the names of angels, and the names of Adam’s descendants. On grammatical grounds involving pronouns, he favors the names of the descendants and angels while also acknowledging the linguistic possibility of the broader reading. This is historical interpretive diversity, not a list supplied within the verse. <a href="https://quran.com/al-baqarah/31/tafsirs/ar-tafsir-al-tabari" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Al-Tabari on 2:31</a>.

Ibn Kathir interprets the names broadly through beings’ names, attributes, and actions, and considers the scene in relation to Adam’s distinction in knowledge. This is an interpretive account to be read within its own framework. It does not automatically establish that every human being is superior to every angel in every respect. <a href="https://quran.com/en/al-baqarah/31/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Ibn Kathir on 2:31–33</a>.

The teaching of names can also be considered through the human capacities for language and conceptualization. Such a reading, however, does not directly explain the biological mechanism of the teaching or the history of human languages. A theory of language, an account of cognition, and what the verse itself says are distinct levels of claim. None should quietly supply the missing details of the others as though they were established knowledge.

Restricting the names exclusively to GOD’s names, or to hidden formulas giving control over nature, would likewise require additional grounds. The reference to GOD’s names in 7:180 does not automatically establish the scope of 2:31. Discussing these possibilities is different from finding a definitive inventory.

Levels of explanation also matter in interpreting the representative in 2:30. The heading accompanying Rashad Khalifa’s text takes a Satan-centered approach. Combining it with a different interpretation of the representative and presenting the result as a single undisputed history would conflate distinct frameworks. Teaching and the limits of knowledge in the naming encounter can be examined separately from these additional details. <a href="https://kuranteyit.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">2:30–33 and accompanying explanations</a>.

**Verse references:** 2:30; 2:31; 2:32; 2:33; 7:180.

## 19. Conclusion: From What the Angels Did Not Know to the Posture I Need to Learn

### Verse

<blockquote data-verse="2:32" data-language="en">
<p lang="en"><strong>2:32</strong> — They  said,  “Be  You  glorified,  we have  no  knowledge,  except  that which  You  have  taught  us.  You  are the Omniscient, Most Wise.”</p>
</blockquote>

### Reflection on the Verse

I return to the question in the title by saying as much as the text expressly supports: The knowledge the angels cannot provide in this encounter is the names taught to Adam of what is presented to them. The verses state that Adam makes the names known. They do not give us the individual inventory, the language of instruction, or the manner in which the teaching occurred.

This answer does not fill in every detail I wonder about. Yet it leaves no ambiguity about something central: Adam’s knowledge is not an absolute possession that originated independently and without a source. The angels’ knowledge is not unlimited either. GOD knows the secrets of the heavens and the earth, what is disclosed and what is concealed.

I have to be prepared to give up two comforts here. One is speaking as though I know details I do not. The other is making what I know into moral immunity. Adam being taught the names did not prevent his later error. The angels being unable to answer a question did not make their honest response worthless. Nor was Satan’s refusal described merely as a shortage of information.

The direction I want to take from this scene is not to become the person who answers every question. It is to remember the source of what I learn, not conceal what I do not know, and return when I err without making my earlier knowledge a shield. It is to carry knowledge in a way that strengthens truthfulness in my witness and justice in my conduct.

Knowing names can help me attend to the world. But if I hide my desires, fears, and need for superiority behind those names, I may say much while understanding little. The responsibility in 17:36 calls me to investigate; the justice in 4:135 calls me to be honest even against myself; the witness in 3:18 calls me not to forget who stands at the center.

**I cannot individually list the names taught to Adam. But I can see that the angels’ honesty before what they did not know should also examine my own claims to knowledge.**

Trusting what GOD knows does not require me to speak with certainty where I do not know. It calls me to learn, distinguish, and remain truthful. Perhaps this is where knowledge can elevate a human being: not in declaring himself a master because he knows more, but in trying to live his servanthood more truthfully as he learns.

**Verse references:** 2:30; 2:31; 2:32; 2:33; 2:34; 2:35; 2:36; 2:37; 17:36; 3:18; 4:135.
