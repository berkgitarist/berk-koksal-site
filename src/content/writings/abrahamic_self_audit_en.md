## 1. From the Sky to the Screen on My Desk

### Verse

**6:76**

**Arabic**

<blockquote>
<p lang="ar" dir="rtl">فَلَمَّا جَنَّ عَلَيْهِ ٱلَّيْلُ رَءَا كَوْكَبًا قَالَ هَٰذَا رَبِّى فَلَمَّآ أَفَلَ قَالَ لَآ أُحِبُّ ٱلْـَٔافِلِينَ</p>
</blockquote>

**English — Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation**

> When  the  night  fell,  he  saw  a  shining planet.  “Maybe  this  is  my  Lord,”  he said.  When  it  disappeared,  he  said, “I  do  not  like  (gods)  that  disappear.”

**Turkish — the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation**

> Gece çöktüğünde, o parlayan bir gezegen gördü. “Belki bu benim Rabbimdir” dedi. O kaybolduğunda “Kaybolan (tanrıları) sevmem” dedi.

Source: <a href="https://kuranteyit.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">kuranteyit.com</a>

### Reflection on the Verse

I imagine sitting at my desk one evening, writing on a screen the things I rely on in my life. My work. The works I create. The people whose knowledge I consult. My plans for the future. Each appears inside its own circle. There are lines between them, but I have not yet drawn the most important one: which of these am I giving an authority it does not actually possess?

A job providing me with an income is not the same as my believing it can never let me down. Learning from someone is not the same as accepting that the person cannot be wrong. Yet, in everyday life, I can allow these boundaries to move closer together without admitting it to myself.

Reading 6:76, what draws my attention is not only the brightness in the sky. It is the possibility of changing a judgment about what is seen. Abraham’s words make me examine my own supports. Something being impressive does not, by itself, establish that the meaning I attach to it is true.

The **Abrahamic Self-Audit and Elimination Console** I am considering for KuranTeyit could make room for such an encounter. I am not announcing an existing feature. This is a research and design proposal I consider worth pursuing. I do not want the screen to judge me in my place. I want it to show me what my thinking rests on.

Before putting a person’s name in the first node, I should write my own sentence: “Even if this person is mistaken, I cannot question them.” What I need to examine may be the immunity I grant that person before it is the person’s identity.

The first question, then, is not whom I should remove from my life. It is: **To whom, or to what, am I giving authority in my life, and what kind of authority is it?**

## 2. The Care Required in Eliminating What Sets

### Verse

> **6:76** — When  the  night  fell,  he  saw  a  shining planet.  “Maybe  this  is  my  Lord,”  he said.  When  it  disappeared,  he  said, “I  do  not  like  (gods)  that  disappear.”

> **6:78** — When  he  saw  the  sun  rising,  he  said, “This  must  be  my  Lord.  This  is  the biggest.”  But  when  it  set,  he  said,  “O my  people,  I  denounce  your  idolatry.

### Reflection on the Verse

The easiest mistake for an elimination canvas would be to treat everything temporary as worthless. Work, family, friendship, and relationships of learning would disappear one by one, and the user might mistake an emptied screen for monotheism. Yet the issue in 6:76 and 6:78 is not the rejection of every value these heavenly bodies have in life. It is the claim of lordship directed toward them.

Something disappearing from view does not mean it never existed. Information being valid for a particular situation does not make it false. A person being mortal does not prove that everything the person says is mistaken. If I lose these distinctions, elimination stops being a form of attention that strengthens inquiry and becomes an easy habit of rejection.

What I am examining here is whether I place unlimited trust in something limited. Alongside “Is this support permanent?” another question should therefore appear: “Why do I want it to be permanent, and what exactly do I expect from it?”

Haste in the opposite direction is dangerous too. My not having seen something set does not establish that it is Lord. A system that operates for a very long time, an enduring institution, or a claim repeated across generations does not acquire absoluteness through duration alone. Reflecting on the elimination in 6:78 does not entitle me to dismiss a limit merely because I have not seen it.

Sometimes what should disappear from the canvas is not the object but the claim attached to it. The teacher can remain; the claim of infallibility is eliminated. The job can remain; the claim of absolute security is eliminated. Life does not have to become poorer. The burden each thing is expected to bear is simply reconsidered.

## 3. A Mind That Asks for Guidance

### Verse

> **6:77** — When  he  saw  the  moon  rising,  he said,  “Maybe  this  is  my  Lord!” When  it  disappeared,  he  said,  “Unless  my  Lord  guides  me,  I  will  be with the strayers.” 

### Reflection on the Verse

I do not want to read Abraham’s examination merely as the triumph of a powerful mind. In 6:77, I do not find the ease of someone declaring his own thinking sufficient. There is a need for guidance. For me, that humility belongs at the heart of inquiry.

After refuting one claim, I can imagine myself capable of refuting every claim. After discovering an authority’s mistake, I can think I no longer need anyone. At that moment, an old support may have weakened while the authority I give my own mind has grown beyond measure.

Before 6:77, I do not see a requirement to choose between thinking and turning to GOD. Acknowledging that I can be mistaken while investigating does not mean abandoning investigation. Recognizing the limits of my judgment does not mean declaring truth impossible to find.

In <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/how_can_i_follow_abrahams_path_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">my earlier article on following Abraham’s path</a>, I reflected on the relationship between turning oneself toward GOD and receiving guidance. Here, the same question reaches the tool on my desk: as I build a framework to help me, am I putting it in the place of the One who guides?

This is why I would want a place in the console where I can say, “I do not know yet.” Leaving a question open is not always evasion. Sometimes it means refusing to present a connection I have not established as though it were already secure.

The tool can remind me of the next question to investigate. It can make rereading a verse easier. But it cannot submit on my behalf. I am seeking a form of self-examination that reduces my excessive confidence in my own sufficiency without reducing my effort to think.

## 4. Does Greater Mean More Absolute?

### Verse

> **6:78** — When  he  saw  the  sun  rising,  he  said, “This  must  be  my  Lord.  This  is  the biggest.”  But  when  it  set,  he  said,  “O my  people,  I  denounce  your  idolatry.

### Reflection on the Verse

Not everything that impresses me arrives as a small mistake. Sometimes I stand before a genuine achievement, a powerful institution, or knowledge developed over many years. The question is not whether I should deny their greatness. It is which question that greatness actually answers.

The encounter in 6:78 leads me to consider this distinction. Being greater and being Lord are not two attributes of the same kind. One can be stated within a comparison; the other is a claim far beyond that comparison. Moving from the great to the greater does not, by itself, show that I am turning toward the right center.

The same haste can arise with a digital tool. I may be impressed by a system that offers many connections, speaks fluently, or remembers all my notes. But I need to examine separately the move from “It does this well” to “It should decide what is right for me.”

The console could make that move visible. Beneath a node, I could write “The quality that impresses me,” and elsewhere, “The conclusion I draw from that quality.” If a reason is missing between the two sentences, leaving the gap open would be more honest than concealing it with an impressive line.

Acknowledging someone’s expertise does not require treating them as an authority on everything. Recognizing a program’s success does not grant it the authority to issue religious rulings. Before 6:78, I want to learn to preserve my sense of proportion without losing my capacity for appreciation.

While searching for a greater support, I may simply be rebuilding the same mistaken relationship on a larger scale.

## 5. After Elimination: Not Emptiness, but Direction

### Verse

> **6:79** — “I  have  devoted  myself  absolutely  to the  One  who  initiated  the  heavens and  the  earth;  I  will  never  be  an  idol worshiper.”

### Reflection on the Verse

Elimination can bring relief. A person has lifted a burden from someone else’s shoulders, perhaps from his own, that those shoulders could not bear. Yet I cannot imagine a life completed by rejection alone. Knowing what I say no to does not remove the question of what I devote myself to.

This is why I pause before 6:79. The horizon of reflection is not merely the selection of the strongest remaining object. Rather than setting one being within the heavens and the earth against the others, there is devotion to the One who initiated them.

I am not deriving an algorithm that proves an entire faith through a handful of checkboxes. The elimination of one candidate does not automatically establish another. In particular, designing GOD as the last remaining item among the software’s options could diminish the meaning of this orientation.

The canvas I propose should help someone who wants to examine devotion to GOD within their own life. It should state this framework of faith openly rather than present steering toward a concealed outcome as impartial inquiry. Making room for a person to think and producing a predetermined image of victory on the final screen are different activities.

Nor does displaying GOD’s name at the center constitute a certificate of success. The center on the screen can be a reminder; it should not be understood as a representation or location of GOD, or as a measurable object.

My direction will become apparent after the screen is closed: in what I give priority to when interests conflict, in what I do when a cherished idea turns out to be wrong, and in whether I can return a tool to its proper limits. Reflecting on 6:79 makes me take that life seriously, more than a beautiful diagram.

## 6. Not Erasing What I Love, but Recognizing Its Place

### Verse

> **9:24** — Proclaim:  “If  your  parents,  your children, your siblings, your spouses, your family, the money you have earned, a business you worry about, and the homes you cherish are more beloved to you than GOD and His messenger,** and the striving in His cause, then just wait until GOD brings His judgment.” GOD does not guide the wicked people.

### Reflection on the Verse

I do not want a self-audit that condemns my family because I love them, my work because I care about it, or something I have created because it brings me joy. Before 9:24, I cannot flatten the verse’s comparison and place every love under the same judgment.

What is difficult for me here is not admitting that I value something. It is honestly recognizing when that value takes priority over my other commitments. My work may matter. But if I am prepared to defend something I know is wrong in order to protect it, what I need to examine is no longer merely my desire to work.

Deleting a node labeled “My work” would not resolve this on its own. A more meaningful question would be: “Which boundary am I prepared to cross for the sake of my work?” Sometimes what needs correction is not my profession but the unlimited obedience I attach to it.

In <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/loving-god-alone-en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">my article on loving GOD alone</a>, I considered the distinction between love and deification. Here, before 9:24, I feel the weight of that distinction in ordinary life. Writing down the name of what I love is not enough. I also need to see what the priority I give it may cost.

I am not turning the verse’s words about the messenger and striving in GOD’s cause into a personal relaxation program that merely says, “Reduce worldly attachments.” I read the statement in its entirety without substituting my own question for that whole.

Elimination should not be another name for becoming incapable of love. My need is not to disregard people and the work to which I give my effort. It is to consider how I can avoid giving any of them a place ahead of GOD.

## 7. What Happens Within Me When GOD ALONE Is Mentioned?

### Verse

> **39:45** — When GOD ALONE is  mentioned, the  hearts  of  those  who  do  not believe in the Hereafter shrink with aversion. But when others are mentioned  beside  Him,  they  become satisfied.*

### Reflection on the Verse

Sometimes I accept a claim not by examining it but through the comfort it gives me. The addition of a name makes me feel secure. A community’s approval makes a sentence feel complete. I may never have asked why I find it difficult to hold the same thought without that name or approval.

39:45 invites me to examine not only the sentences I defend but also the conditions under which I feel satisfied. While saying I turn to GOD, do I make some other approval indispensable within myself? Beside the center I profess, am I building another center whose absence makes everything feel incomplete?

This does not mean that I can measure someone’s faith by looking at their face. I cannot treat a moment of distress, silence, or an emotional reaction as a conclusive diagnosis under this verse. Nor should the console be a device that calculates whether someone believes in the Hereafter from a few answers.

There is a narrower question I can ask myself: “When I withdraw this name or support from my thinking, why does my foundation seem to collapse?” My answer is a note of self-examination, not a results screen reporting GOD’s judgment of me.

The verse should not be turned into a list of prohibitions declaring that no other human being may ever be mentioned in conversation. What I am investigating is the place given to a name, before the mere fact of its mention.

Perhaps the most difficult node is not the one I openly defend but the one whose presence beside me has become so familiar that I no longer notice it. That possibility matters not as a reason to label others but as a reason to attend more carefully to the conditions of my own satisfaction.

## 8. Learning from Someone and Making Them a Lord

### Verse

> **9:31** — They  have  set  up  their  religious leaders  and  scholars  as  lords,*  instead of GOD. Others deified the Messiah, son of Mary. They were all commanded  to  worship  only  one god. There is no god except He. Be He glorified, high above having any partners.

### Reflection on the Verse

Learning from a person and making that person unquestionable are not the same relationship. Without this distinction, I may either submit to everyone or become so closed that I learn from no one. In either case, I can lose the openness inquiry requires.

Reading 9:31, I direct my attention to the position people give religious leaders and scholars instead of GOD. Someone knowing a great deal does not require placing everything they say beyond examination. Acknowledging their work is not the same as placing their words on the same level as a verse.

Rather than writing a person’s name on my canvas as an “idol,” I would want to write an examinable sentence about my relationship to that person: “I do not know the reasoning behind this interpretation, but I accept it because of who said it.” I then move from passing judgment on another person’s inner life to examining the basis of my own acceptance.

This care cannot apply only to authorities preferred by other people. A researcher I admire, an approach I adopt, and my own writings are not exempt. Asking for an exception for a tool I develop under my own name may be another way of crossing the same boundary.

I am not claiming some superiority in needing no one. I do not want my need to become a reason for granting someone unlimited religious authority.

One of the console’s most honest questions could be: “Would I accept this statement on the same grounds if it came from someone I disliked?” The actual claim, buried beneath title and personal closeness, could then be investigated. For me, the examination opened by 9:31 begins not with diminishing human beings but with taking seriously the refusal to give them GOD’s place.

## 9. What If I Eliminate Every Authority but Leave My Ego?

### Verse

> **25:43** — Have you seen the one whose god is his own ego? Will you be his advocate?

### Reflection on the Verse

I can easily turn a canvas into an exhibition of my own correctness. I add names I dislike, eliminate ideas I consider weak, and feel relieved. The screen becomes simpler. But a center remains that makes every decision and is never opened to examination: the result I want.

25:43 makes me consider why self-examination cannot be completed by looking outward alone. Deciding not to make someone else my lord does not automatically make my own ego the right measure. After rejecting an authority, I may allow my desire to rise above every objection.

In <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/the_most_argumentative_creature_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">my article on the most argumentative creature</a>, I returned to the tension between seeking truth and needing to be right. This console could offer a new test of that tension: am I weighing myself against the verses, or collecting verses to defend myself?

The canvas should therefore hold more than supporting reasons. I should also be able to write down the question that weakens my interpretation. “Does this verse actually state the result I want?” must not remain an objection reserved for the other side.

I do not want the console to flatter me. But I do not mistake constant accusation for truthfulness either. Not every desire is wrong; making my desire an unquestionable measure is what requires further examination. An application always finding me right is no more, by itself, a sign of reliability than an application always finding me wrong.

Perhaps the last connection I need to remove is the sentence, “If I thought of it, it must be true.” Until that connection loosens, I can clear the entire screen without the self-audit having reached its most important point.

## 10. Can I Bear Witness Against Myself?

### Verse

> **4:135** — O  you who  believe,  you  shall  be absolutely  equitable,  and  observe GOD,  when  you  serve  as  witnesses, even  against  yourselves,  or  your parents,  or  your  relatives.  Whether the  accused  is  rich  or  poor,  GOD takes  care  of  both.  Therefore,  do not  be  biased  by  your  personal wishes.  If  you  deviate  or  disregard (this  commandment),  then  GOD  is fully  Cognizant  of  everything  you do.

### Reflection on the Verse

Being honest with myself may seem easy in a private notebook. But will I preserve that openness when the matter involves someone I love, a community to which I belong, or work to which I have given years?

Before 4:135, I feel that investigating what is true is not merely an intellectual skill. There is also a cost. A claim without a basis does not become sound because it protects my reputation. A valid objection does not become unjust simply because it is directed at people close to me.

This is why it would not be enough for the console to record only “What am I eliminating?” There should also be room for “Which claim in my own favor have I given up?” A record of correcting my own mistake may be a more serious act of self-examination than a list of ten mistakes made by someone else.

But I should not turn this space into a system of excessive self-accusation. Justice does not require inventing evidence against myself. Recording suspicion as proof, assuming I know someone’s intention, or converting a private grievance into a religious judgment is not honest examination.

If a node says, “I know this,” it should also show what that knowledge rests on. If it says, “I fear this,” the existence of my fear should be distinguished from the truth of the claim I fear.

For me, 4:135 means learning to bear the weight of testimony without changing its direction: not bending truth to protect myself, and not producing something untrue to punish myself. Such care could turn an elimination console from a platform that passes judgment on people into a desk at which I examine my own acceptance and conduct.

## 11. What Should a Node Contain?

### Verse

> **6:76** — When  the  night  fell,  he  saw  a  shining planet.  “Maybe  this  is  my  Lord,”  he said.  When  it  disappeared,  he  said, “I  do  not  like  (gods)  that  disappear.”

> **17:36** — You  shall  not  accept  any  information,  unless  you  verify  it  for  yourself. I  have  given  you  the  hearing,  the eyesight,  and  the  brain,  and  you  are responsible for using them.

### Reflection on the Verse

Let us make the canvas more concrete. Imagine adding a node labeled “Professional success.” The label alone is not clear enough for me to evaluate. What I mean by success, and what I attach to it, remain unspecified.

My own sentence should appear inside the node: “If I become successful, I will never feel insecure about anything again.” Beneath it, I should write why I think this. Is there an outcome I have actually observed, a promise someone made, or a strong wish of my own? When I put them in the same place, my wish can begin to look like evidence.

Before 6:76, I distinguish the object from the claim directed toward it. Before 17:36, I return to examining the basis of my acceptance for myself. I am not extracting a ready-made software specification from these two verses. I am designing a workspace that might help me as I reflect on them.

The node could contain separate spaces for “The real benefit it provides” and “The unlimited expectation I place on it.” I could then examine the promise that success will solve every problem without rejecting the entire wish to succeed.

Next, I ask myself: “What would I need to see that would require me to change this claim, and would I take it seriously?” If I can make room for no answer, the problem may not simply be that I have found too little information. I may say I am opening my claim to examination while protecting it from every possible result.

The canvas then becomes a notebook of reasons rather than a graveyard of objects. It becomes possible to see which claim I limited and why, which I did not accept, and which I have not yet been able to assess. What matters is not the disappearance of a line but the legibility of the reason behind it.

## 12. A Cross-Reference Is Not a Ruling

### Verse

> **17:36** — You  shall  not  accept  any  information,  unless  you  verify  it  for  yourself. I  have  given  you  the  hearing,  the eyesight,  and  the  brain,  and  you  are responsible for using them.

> **39:45** — When GOD ALONE is  mentioned, the  hearts  of  those  who  do  not believe in the Hereafter shrink with aversion. But when others are mentioned  beside  Him,  they  become satisfied.*

### Reflection on the Verse

Adding a verse number beside a node does not establish that the node has been judged correctly. Similar words may be present, and a connection in subject matter may be possible. But how that verse relates to a particular situation in my life still requires explanation.

For example, I would not want writing a teacher’s name to make 39:45 automatically produce the conclusion, “This person is an idol in your life.” The verse does not provide a list of names. Nor does a person appearing in my note explain the whole relationship I have with that person.

Here, 17:36 raises questions not only for the end user but also for my design choices. Can I examine a suggested connection for myself? Can I reach the complete verse? Is it clear which statement is the verse, which is my interpretation, and which is a proposed association from the software?

I would want an explanation beneath every connection: “Why might this be relevant?” Another question could appear alongside it: “What does this verse not establish on its own?” The tool would then help not only in making connections but in showing their limits.

The user should be able to reject a suggestion, postpone it, or say, “I cannot establish this connection yet.” Having these options is not the same as rejecting the verse. What is being examined is not the verse itself but the software’s claim that it applies to my note.

This is the innovation I would value for KuranTeyit: making the basis and limits of each connection readable rather than listing ever more verse numbers. A highly connected screen should not conceal poorly reasoned thinking. I should not confuse the appearance of research with research itself.

## 13. From a Removed Connection to a Changed Action

### Verse

> **6:79** — “I  have  devoted  myself  absolutely  to the  One  who  initiated  the  heavens and  the  earth;  I  will  never  be  an  idol worshiper.”

> **4:135** — O  you who  believe,  you  shall  be absolutely  equitable,  and  observe GOD,  when  you  serve  as  witnesses, even  against  yourselves,  or  your parents,  or  your  relatives.  Whether the  accused  is  rich  or  poor,  GOD takes  care  of  both.  Therefore,  do not  be  biased  by  your  personal wishes.  If  you  deviate  or  disregard (this  commandment),  then  GOD  is fully  Cognizant  of  everything  you do.

### Reflection on the Verse

Imagine that a line disappears from the canvas. I have noted that I no longer hold the claim, “Everything this person says is true.” The screen feels less burdened. But what will I do when I meet that person tomorrow?

Thinking about the devotion in 6:79 together with the testimony in 4:135, I do not want self-examination to remain an arrangement on a screen. I do not have to belittle the person. But if I keep postponing asking for the basis of something they say, I may be preserving in life the connection I removed from the canvas.

After each evaluation, I could therefore write a small, concrete note about conduct: “I will not pass this statement on as certain knowledge when I do not know its basis.” Or: “I will read the objection to my view with the same care.” These are not religious certificates issued to me by the application. They are decisions I am willing to undertake and can return to examine later.

Eliminating a claim is not the same as erasing its history. To the extent that users choose to keep it, they should be able to see their earlier acceptance and the reason it changed. They would not have to turn today’s judgment into a story of perfection, as though they had always thought the same thing.

Self-examination should not become a search for a new offense in every everyday choice. The particular claim I am considering, its basis, and the action I am thinking of changing should be clear. Leaving the tool, working, speaking with people, and continuing with life should not count as failure.

Even if only one center remains on the screen, that is not enough. The question remains: how does my devotion to that center affect my words and actions? Self-examination may begin where the display ends.

## 14. The Privacy of Self-Examination

### Verse

> **17:36** — You  shall  not  accept  any  information,  unless  you  verify  it  for  yourself. I  have  given  you  the  hearing,  the eyesight,  and  the  brain,  and  you  are responsible for using them.

### Reflection on the Verse

Some questions can only be asked honestly when there is no crowd watching. I would want a space in which I write my own assumptions to remain a space for my own thinking. Exposing every uncertainty to others or turning it into a performance table should not be the purpose of this design.

17:36 does not command a particular storage technology. While reflecting on acceptance and responsibility, I value preserving the user’s say over their own notes as a design choice.

The console could therefore be built so that notes and evaluations operate on the device. IndexedDB can be used for local records in a browser; that is one of its technical functions. But using IndexedDB does not demonstrate that software sends nothing elsewhere or that its records are automatically encrypted. (<a href="https://www.w3.org/TR/IndexedDB/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">W3C: Indexed Database API, introduction and security considerations</a>.)

In the console I envisage, work with verses should continue without personal notes being exported. Sending them to an external AI service, requiring an account, or secretly tracking use should not be the default. Decisions about backup and deletion should remain clearly in the user’s hands. These are not promises about an existing feature but boundaries I propose for a system to be built.

Nor does “local-first” generally mean the absence of all network connections. It prioritizes the local copy and the user’s control. A fully local space for self-examination is a narrower design choice within that approach. (<a href="https://www.inkandswitch.com/essay/local-first/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Ink & Switch: Local-first software</a>.)

For me, responsibility does not mean wanting to collect everything a user reveals. While helping someone examine what they accept, I also want to develop the care required not to treat that acceptance as my property.

## 15. Putting the Console Itself on the Canvas

### Verse

> **9:31** — They  have  set  up  their  religious leaders  and  scholars  as  lords,*  instead of GOD. Others deified the Messiah, son of Mary. They were all commanded  to  worship  only  one god. There is no god except He. Be He glorified, high above having any partners.

> **17:36** — You  shall  not  accept  any  information,  unless  you  verify  it  for  yourself. I  have  given  you  the  hearing,  the eyesight,  and  the  brain,  and  you  are responsible for using them.

### Reflection on the Verse

Perhaps the most serious test is this: while users examine everything they rely on, will they be able to examine the tool doing the examining?

If I design a space for self-audit in KuranTeyit, I cannot exempt my own software from that question. Before 9:31, I do not want to replace people with a program in an unquestionable position. Before 17:36, I cannot accept “The system said so” as a substitute for the user’s own verification.

In <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/should-a-quran-app-issue-rulings-tool-authority-boundary/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">“Should a Quran App Issue Rulings?”</a>, I explored the boundary between tool and authority. This console would present one of its hardest tests. A tool designed to challenge the supports people make absolute could become a new absolute support itself.

An opening disclaimer alone would not resolve this. How suggestions are produced should be understandable. Users should be able to flag a verse that has been connected incorrectly. At every step, it should be apparent that my interpretation is not the verse’s text. An unanswered question should not be covered over with a confident result.

Users should even be able to add a node called “This console.” Underneath, they could write: “It can offer me a space for examination, but it cannot believe on my behalf, fully know my intention, or declare me a verified believer.”

I would not consider the design a failure when someone can continue thinking without needing it. I do not want to create language that equates leaving the tool with leaving truth.

Understanding the console’s limits does not diminish its value. It returns the tool to its place of service. Nor should my measure as a developer be how tightly I can bind someone to me or to my software.

## 16. Success: Not How Many Idols I Deleted, but What I Weighed More Honestly

### Verse

> **17:36** — You  shall  not  accept  any  information,  unless  you  verify  it  for  yourself. I  have  given  you  the  hearing,  the eyesight,  and  the  brain,  and  you  are responsible for using them.

> **4:135** — O  you who  believe,  you  shall  be absolutely  equitable,  and  observe GOD,  when  you  serve  as  witnesses, even  against  yourselves,  or  your parents,  or  your  relatives.  Whether the  accused  is  rich  or  poor,  GOD takes  care  of  both.  Therefore,  do not  be  biased  by  your  personal wishes.  If  you  deviate  or  disregard (this  commandment),  then  GOD  is fully  Cognizant  of  everything  you do.

### Reflection on the Verse

If such a console is developed, measuring its success by the number of deleted nodes could be misleading. A user who deletes many may not have thought more carefully. Someone who deletes few may have preserved a claim’s genuine usefulness while limiting only an excessive conclusion.

Before 17:36 and 4:135, what I want to investigate is more concrete. Can users distinguish evidence from personal desire? Can they assess fairly a reason that challenges their own view? Can they recognize which claim a verse supports and which claim it does not establish on its own?

A small study could use fictional situations without classifying people’s private beliefs. Those reading the same verses on their own could be compared with those considering them through a reasoning canvas. Some examples could contain strong connections, others weak ones, and others insufficient clarity for a decision.

The assessment criteria, set in advance, should not be agreement with the designer’s preferred outcome. They should concern accurately describing the limits of a claim, showing its basis, and not concealing uncertainty. Increased confidence and better reasoning should be examined separately. Faith should not receive a score.

This is a research proposal, not a ready-made finding that a canvas is better than reading. The tool might even give people the feeling of investigating while leading them to defend the same opinion more rigidly. The research should make room for that possibility.

For me, meaningful success is not a user’s agreement with me. It is the ability to recognize a weakly supported claim even when it serves one’s own interests, and to consider treating the other person more fairly. If the console makes that easier, that is where it may be helpful.

## 17. Arabic Word Roots: Disappearance, Lordship, Orientation, and Responsibility

### Verse

> **6:76** — When  the  night  fell,  he  saw  a  shining planet.  “Maybe  this  is  my  Lord,”  he said.  When  it  disappeared,  he  said, “I  do  not  like  (gods)  that  disappear.”

> **6:79** — “I  have  devoted  myself  absolutely  to the  One  who  initiated  the  heavens and  the  earth;  I  will  never  be  an  idol worshiper.”

> **17:36** — You  shall  not  accept  any  information,  unless  you  verify  it  for  yourself. I  have  given  you  the  hearing,  the eyesight,  and  the  brain,  and  you  are responsible for using them.

### Reflection on the Verse

In 6:76, **أَفَلَ** is a verb derived from **أ ف ل**; **ٱلْـَٔافِلِينَ** in the same verse is also related to this root. Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation uses “disappeared”; the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation uses “kaybolduğunda.” This does not yield the conclusion that every temporary relationship is false. Attending to the encounter in which the word appears must come before turning it into a command to erase the whole of life. (<a href="https://corpus.quran.com/wordmorphology.jsp?location=(6:76:11)" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The morphology of أَفَلَ</a>; <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/wordmorphology.jsp?location=(6:76:15)" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">the morphology of ٱلْـَٔافِلِينَ</a>.)

In the same verse, **رَبِّى** combines a noun derived from **ر ب ب** with a first-person possessive pronoun. The statement does more than describe an object; it also brings a relationship into view. This is why, in my reflection, the question “What is this?” is accompanied by “What position does it hold for me?” That personal question is not a new translation substituted for the word. It is my way of attending to the relationship within the sentence. (<a href="https://corpus.quran.com/wordmorphology.jsp?location=(6:76:9)" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The morphology of رَبِّى</a>.)

In 6:79, **وَجَّهْتُ** is a first-person singular verb derived from **و ج ه**. Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation uses “devoted  myself  absolutely”; the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation places “kendimi mutlak şekilde” and “adadım” within the same sentence. I am not treating this rendering as a one-to-one correspondence with a single word. I read the verb’s personal orientation alongside the care needed not to hand my responsibility over to a tool. (<a href="https://corpus.quran.com/wordmorphology.jsp?location=(6:79:2)" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The morphology of وَجَّهْتُ</a>.)

**فَطَرَ** derives from **ف ط ر**. Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation uses “initiated”; the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation uses “başlatan.” What matters to me here is not confusing the One toward whom devotion is directed with an object within the heavens and the earth. I am not deriving a contemporary physical theory or a software model from the root. **حَنِيفًا** is a noun derived from **ح ن ف**. I do not isolate it from its context and turn it into a new label of affiliation or an automatic mark of correctness; I read it together with the devotion expressed in 6:79 as a whole. (<a href="https://corpus.quran.com/wordmorphology.jsp?location=(6:79:5)" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The morphology of فَطَرَ</a>; <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/wordmorphology.jsp?location=(6:79:8)" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">the morphology of حَنِيفًا</a>.)

In 17:36, **عِلْمٌ** is related to **ع ل م**, and **مَسْـُٔولًا** to **س أ ل**. The presence of knowledge and responsibility in the same verse makes asking about the basis of an accepted claim important to me. But I cannot declare every personal practice I call “verification” to be the only method commanded by the verse. (<a href="https://corpus.quran.com/wordmorphology.jsp?location=(17:36:7)" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The morphology of عِلْمٌ</a>; <a href="https://corpus.quran.com/wordmorphology.jsp?location=(17:36:16)" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">the morphology of مَسْـُٔولًا</a>.)

I am not extracting the meanings “console,” “node,” or “decision engine” from these words. Those belong to my present-day design vocabulary. Root analysis is not a source of rulings that replaces the verse. It is a separate linguistic exercise that helps me attend more carefully to the words within it.

## 18. Other Claims

### Verse

> **6:76** — When  the  night  fell,  he  saw  a  shining planet.  “Maybe  this  is  my  Lord,”  he said.  When  it  disappeared,  he  said, “I  do  not  like  (gods)  that  disappear.”

> **6:77** — When  he  saw  the  moon  rising,  he said,  “Maybe  this  is  my  Lord!” When  it  disappeared,  he  said,  “Unless  my  Lord  guides  me,  I  will  be with the strayers.” 

> **17:36** — You  shall  not  accept  any  information,  unless  you  verify  it  for  yourself. I  have  given  you  the  hearing,  the eyesight,  and  the  brain,  and  you  are responsible for using them.

### Reflection on the Verse

Calling this narrative “history’s first rational and empirical model of reflection” may be a powerful description, but historical priority is not information established by 6:76–79 alone. We can reflect on observation, evaluation, and orientation in the verses without presenting a ranking across the whole history of thought as a verified fact. Likewise, placing the modern experimental method, in all its details, inside this narrative may demand more from the text than it states.

Different readings may emphasize either Abraham’s own search or a debate he conducts with his people. For example, <a href="https://quran.com/al-anam/76/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">the abridged English rendering of Ibn Kathir’s commentary</a> emphasizes debate with his people and exposing the error in their understanding of worship. This is not the verse translation I use here but a separate historical interpretation. I do not treat it as a document that conclusively reports Abraham’s entire inner life, nor do I put it in place of the basis for my design proposal.

The statement “Today’s digital religious tools direct people toward ready-made assumptions rather than inquiry” should not be taken as a demonstrated generalization about every application either. What I am considering here is a possibility that needs to be investigated in a particular interface. Placing a tool that offers sources in the same category as one that issues judgments on the user’s behalf would reproduce the loss of distinctions I am criticizing.

On the technical side, it might also be claimed that local operation solves every problem. Yet keeping records on a device does not prove that an interface is impartial or that its verse associations are correct. Local storage and freedom of thought are not the same claim.

The historical descriptions, interpretations, and technological expectations in this section do not stand above the verses as measures determining their meaning. Taking 17:36 seriously in relation to myself means not making a claim certain merely because it supports an appealing project idea when I do not know its basis. I do not have to grant Abraham’s narrative an unverified title of “world’s first” in order to reflect on it.

## 19. Conclusion: Simplifying False Absolutes, Not Erasing My Life

### Verse

> **6:76** — When  the  night  fell,  he  saw  a  shining planet.  “Maybe  this  is  my  Lord,”  he said.  When  it  disappeared,  he  said, “I  do  not  like  (gods)  that  disappear.”

> **6:79** — “I  have  devoted  myself  absolutely  to the  One  who  initiated  the  heavens and  the  earth;  I  will  never  be  an  idol worshiper.”

> **17:36** — You  shall  not  accept  any  information,  unless  you  verify  it  for  yourself. I  have  given  you  the  hearing,  the eyesight,  and  the  brain,  and  you  are responsible for using them.

> **4:135** — O  you who  believe,  you  shall  be absolutely  equitable,  and  observe GOD,  when  you  serve  as  witnesses, even  against  yourselves,  or  your parents,  or  your  relatives.  Whether the  accused  is  rich  or  poor,  GOD takes  care  of  both.  Therefore,  do not  be  biased  by  your  personal wishes.  If  you  deviate  or  disregard (this  commandment),  then  GOD  is fully  Cognizant  of  everything  you do.

### Reflection on the Verse

I look again at the screen on my desk. My work is still there. So are the works I create, the people I love, and those whose knowledge helps me. The canvas is not emptier than before. But some of its lines now carry different words.

“Infallible” no longer appears beneath one of them. Beside another, the sentence “It protects me from everything” has been removed. Some connections carry the note “I do not know yet.” In one place, I have acknowledged that my own claim was wrong. None of this declares me a finished human being. Yet what I think, and why I think it, has become clear enough that it is harder to conceal from myself.

Before 6:76–79, I do not understand monotheism as erasing every relationship. I reflect on examining what I have invested with lordship, distinguishing greatness from absoluteness, and turning to GOD without forgetting my need for guidance. 9:24 helps me ask about the priority of my loves; 39:45, about the conditions of my satisfaction; 9:31, about the position I grant a person; and 25:43, about whether my own ego has escaped examination.

While 17:36 leaves me with responsibility for what I accept, 4:135 calls for that attention to remain bound to justice. Not concealing what counts against me matters, as does not producing an accusation against myself or someone else without a basis.

The point of considering such a console for KuranTeyit is not to build a decision engine that believes in place of a human being. It is to open a space for examination in which verse, interpretation, personal expectation, and evidence are not confused. Its success could be investigated not through its ability to bind someone to itself but through whether it helps a person weigh their own judgment more openly and honestly.

I cannot hand my commitment to GOD’s oneness over to a results screen. Nor can I assume that deleting a node has corrected the relationship within me. The truthfulness of my words, my justice, and what I am willing to accept continue to be tested in my life outside the application.

For me, Abraham’s narrative does not end with finding the brightest option and attaching myself to it. It reaches toward the courage to see what judgment brightness has become within me. Sometimes I truly need to give something up. Sometimes I need to put a relationship in a more appropriate place. Sometimes I need to acknowledge that I do not know enough and read again.

**I do not want to empty my life. I want to learn not to give anything in my life GOD’s place. A console can remind me of this, but it cannot live that orientation on my behalf.**
