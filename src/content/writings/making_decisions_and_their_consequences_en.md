# [3:159] Making Decisions: What Are the Responsibilities and Consequences of a Choice?

**Author:** Berk KÖKSAL  
**Date:** November 20, 2026  
**Place:** Mersin

Peace be upon you.

Some decisions feel simple when I first make them. The real difficulty often begins afterward. Will I keep my word? Will I notice if my choice harms another person? Can I admit a mistake and change direction? If the outcome I wanted never arrives, will I still hold to the principles I claimed to value? I also wonder how many years I may spend refusing to decide, without noticing that continued avoidance has become a decision of its own.

Verse 3:159 is where I begin. What holds my attention is that the decision is not left alone. Compassion, pardon, and consultation come before it; action and trust in GOD accompany what follows. As I ask what a decision can do to my own life, I also want to ask whose life it reaches beyond mine.

If I searched only for the literal word “decision,” I would miss verses about willing, choosing, judgment, testimony, verification, consultation, commitments, repentance, and accountability. I have therefore included passages that address choices directly and others that illuminate their reasons, practice, and consequences. Since nearly every moral instruction can bear upon a choice, I do not claim that any thematic catalogue could be the final, indisputable list of every related verse. My aim is a broad, textually accountable reflection, not a new doctrine announced under the Quran's authority.

Perhaps the most difficult part of deciding is not that I cannot see tomorrow. It is that I sometimes hesitate to live by a truth I already recognize. So I direct the inquiry first toward myself: **What standard guides my decisions, and to whom do I try to transfer responsibility when their consequences arrive?**

## 1. What Am I Actually Doing When I Decide?

### Verse

> **3:159**
>
> **العربية:** فَبِمَا رَحْمَةٍ مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ لِنتَ لَهُمْ وَلَوْ كُنتَ فَظًّا غَلِيظَ ٱلْقَلْبِ لَٱنفَضُّوا۟ مِنْ حَوْلِكَ فَٱعْفُ عَنْهُمْ وَٱسْتَغْفِرْ لَهُمْ وَشَاوِرْهُمْ فِى ٱلْأَمْرِ فَإِذَا عَزَمْتَ فَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُحِبُّ ٱلْمُتَوَكِّلِينَ
>
> **English:** It was mercy from GOD that you became  compassionate  towards them.  Had  you  been  harsh  and mean-hearted, they would have abandoned you. Therefore, you shall pardon them and ask forgiveness for them, and consult them. Once you make a decision, carry out your plan, and trust in GOD. GOD loves those who trust in Him.*
>
> **Türkçe:** Onlara karşı şefkatli olman TANRI’dan gelen merhametti. Onlara karşı haşin ve katı yürekli olsaydın seni terk ederlerdi. Bu nedenle onları affet; onlar için bağışlanma dile ve onlara danış. Bir karar verdiğinde planını yerine getir ve TANRI’ya güven. TANRI, O’na güvenenleri sever.*

Source: <a href="https://kuranteyit.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">kuranteyit.com</a>

### Reflection on the Verse

I often imagine decision-making as selecting one of two options. But when I read 3:159 carefully, a human relationship stands before the decision: compassion, pardon, asking forgiveness, and consultation. The decision does not appear in a moral vacuum. It grows out of a particular way of treating people. A more unsettling question may come before “Which option should I choose?”: **What kind of person am I becoming as I choose?** Am I listening, or only waiting for my turn to speak?

I do not lift the specific historical setting of this address and automatically turn it into a rule covering every possible situation. Yet I can attend to what its actual words connect: mercy, consultation, a decision, implementation, and trust in GOD. The verse does not praise endless hesitation. Nor does it say every quickly made decision is wrong. It does place action after deliberation. The difference between weighing a choice and hiding from the responsibility to act can sometimes be difficult to admit.

When making a piece of music or writing, I may spend a long time on one detail: a note, a sentence, a transition. If I give less attention to a decision affecting someone's rights, my careful craftsmanship does not make me a careful moral agent. Trusting GOD is not a declaration that my plan must produce precisely the outcome I want. The verse identifies the One in whom trust belongs; it does not hand me complete knowledge of tomorrow.

So my opening question is not just whether I feel certain. It is whether mercy, knowledge, and responsibility have had a place in the decision I am now prepared to carry out.

Verse references: 3:159.

## 2. Is Consultation the Same as Handing Over Responsibility?

### Verse

> **42:38** — They  respond  to  their  Lord  by  observing  the  Contact  Prayers  (Salat). Their  affairs  are  decided  after  due consultation  among  themselves, and  from  our  provisions  to  them they give (to charity).

> **27:32** — She said, “O my advisers, counsel me in this matter. I am not deciding anything until you advise me.”

> **2:233** — Divorced mothers shall nurse their infants two full years, if the father so wishes. The father shall provide the mother’s food and clothing equitably. No one shall be burdened beyond his ability. No mother shall beharmed on account of her infant, nor shall the father be harmed because of his infant. (If the father dies), his inheritor shall assume these responsibilities. If the infant’s parents mutually agree to part, after due consultation, they commit no error by doing so. You commit no error by hiring nursing mothers, so long as you pay them equitably. You shall observe GOD, and know that GOD is Seer of everything you do.

> **4:35** — If a couple fears separation, you shall appoint an arbitrator from his family and an arbitrator from her family; if they decide to reconcile, GOD will help them get together. GOD is Omniscient, Cognizant. 

> **4:130** — If  the  couple  must  decide  to  part, GOD  will  provide  for  each  of  them from  His  bounties.  GOD  is  Bounteous, Most Wise.

### Reflection on the Verse

In 42:38, decisions reached through consultation appear alongside responding to one's Lord, observing the Contact Prayers, and giving from one's provisions. Consultation is not presented as mere social decoration. It is part of how people conduct their affairs. I find that significant in ordinary relationships: a home, shared work, a difficult dispute, a decision whose consequences another person will have to carry.

The ruler in 27:32 asks for counsel before deciding. That is an account of conduct, not necessarily a timeless protocol specifying exactly whom to consult. Still, it asks an uncomfortable question of anyone who has power: Why should having authority mean I no longer need to listen? Verse 2:233 makes joint consideration concrete in decisions about the care of a child. It matters that mutual agreement and consultation are named. A decision that excludes the voice of the person affected deserves careful scrutiny.

Verse 4:35 provides for representatives from both sides when a couple fears separation; 4:130 also recognizes that separation may occur. I cannot turn consultation into a promise that every relationship will be restored or every difference resolved. Sometimes even sincere consideration leads to a difficult ending. That does not erase the obligation to act justly.

I have to ask whether I consult others because I want to learn or because I want witnesses who will endorse what I have already decided. Someone can share insight without becoming the owner of my conscience. Listening is a way of admitting that my perspective is limited; it is not a way of transferring every consequence to somebody else.

Verse references: 42:38; 27:32; 2:233; 4:35; 4:130.

## 3. The Price of Deciding without Knowing

### Verse

> **17:36** — You  shall  not  accept  any  information,  unless  you  verify  it  for  yourself. I  have  given  you  the  hearing,  the eyesight,  and  the  brain,  and  you  are responsible for using them.

> **49:6** — O you who believe, if a wicked person brings any news to you, you shall first investigate, lest you commit injustice towards some people, out of ignorance, then become sorry and remorseful for what you have done.

> **39:18** — They  are  the  ones  who  examine  all words,  then  follow  the  best.  These are  the  ones  whom  GOD  has  guided;  these  are  the  ones  who  possess intelligence.

> **4:83** — When  a  rumor  that  affects  security comes  their  way,  they  spread  it.  Had they  referred  it  to  the  messenger, and  those  in  charge  among  them, those  who  understand  these  matters would  have  informed  them.  If  it were  not  for  GOD’s  grace  towards you,  and  His  mercy,  you  would  have followed the devil, except a few.

> **27:27** — (Solomon) said, “We will see if you told the truth, or if you are a liar.

> **20:114** — Most  Exalted  is  GOD,  the  only true  King.  Do  not  rush  into  uttering the  Quran  before  it  is  revealed  to you,  and  say,  “My  Lord,  increase  my knowledge.”

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 17:36 is not comfortable reading when I am eager to act. I may have heard a story about someone, formed an opinion about their motives, or received information that suits my position. Precisely when a claim agrees with me, I may become less curious about whether it is true. Yet the demand for verification cannot apply only to views I dislike. My hearing, eyesight, and brain are not badges of superiority; they are faculties for whose use I am responsible.

Verse 49:6 describes a possible sequence with unmistakable human consequences: an unexamined report, injustice committed through ignorance, and regret afterward. Other people may bear the damage while I discover how unreliable my evidence was. Verse 4:83 warns about spreading reports involving security without proper understanding. Not forwarding a claim is also a choice. Asking for time to investigate can be more responsible than performing certainty.

In 39:18, examining words and following the best suggests that scrutiny can be directed toward a conclusion rather than endless doubt. Solomon's response in 27:27 is to test whether a report is truthful. Verse 20:114 joins restraint against haste to the request for increased knowledge. I cannot extract a rule that every decision requires perfect information; some choices occur under real time constraints. But uncertainty does not justify presenting guesses as established facts.

The question from my earlier reflection, <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/a_thousand_artificial_witnesses_same_lie_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">If a Thousand Artificial Witnesses Tell the Same Lie, Does Truth Change?</a>, returns here. Repetition can create confidence without creating evidence. Before I decide something about another person's life, I want to know whether I have verified what I am about to treat as truth.

Verse references: 17:36; 49:6; 39:18; 4:83; 27:27; 20:114.

## 4. The Majority, Inheritance, and My Desire to Be Right

### Verse

> **6:116** — If you obey the majority of people on earth, they will divert you from the path of GOD. They follow only conjecture; they only guess.

> **10:36** — Most  of  them  follow  nothing  but conjecture,  and  conjecture  is  no substitute  for  the  truth.  GOD  is  fully aware of everything they do.

> **53:28** — They had no knowledge about this; they only conjectured. Conjecture is no substitute for the truth.

> **2:170** — When  they  are  told,  “Follow  what GOD  has  revealed  herein,”  they  say, “We  follow  only  what  we  found  our parents  doing.”  What  if  their  parents  did  not  understand,  and  were not guided?

> **43:22** — The  fact  is:  they  said,  “We  found our  parents  carrying  on  certain practices,  and  we  are  following  in their footsteps.”

> **43:23** — Invariably,  when  we  sent  a  warner to  any  community,  the  leaders  of that  community  would  say,  “We found  our  parents  following  certain practices,  and  we  will  continue  in their footsteps.”

> **35:8** — Note  the  one  whose  evil  work  is adorned in his eyes, until he thinks that it is righteous. GOD thus sends astray whoever wills (to go astray), and He guides whoever wills (to be guided). Therefore,  do  not  grieve over them. GOD is fully aware of everything they do.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 6:116 warns against treating the majority as an infallible guide. Verses 10:36 and 53:28 distinguish conjecture from truth. Reversing the rule and declaring every minority position correct would be another unwarranted certainty. Numbers alone do not settle the question in either direction. What matters is whether the claim has a sound basis.

Reading 2:170 alongside 43:22–23, I see how easy it is to inherit a practice and then mistake the inheritance for a conclusion reached through examination. I may tell myself that I have freely chosen an idea when I have hardly questioned it. Did I arrive at this conviction because I searched for what is true, or because I wanted to remain accepted by a group? This is a question about my own choices, not permission to ridicule another person's parents or community.

Verse 35:8 describes the disturbing possibility that a bad deed may look good to the one committing it. That warning gives me no authority to diagnose everyone else's soul. It forces me to consider my own capacity for self-deception. Once I commit to a position, I may begin collecting only what confirms it. My research then ceases to examine the decision and starts defending it.

One consequence of choosing can be almost invisible: a person begins to interpret everything through the choice already made. I want to keep the possibility of correction open. Admitting that I may have been mistaken is not indifference to truth. It may be one way of showing that truth matters more to me than the pleasure of being proven right.

Verse references: 6:116; 10:36; 53:28; 2:170; 43:22; 43:23; 35:8.

## 5. Does Wanting Something Intensely Make It Good?

### Verse

> **53:24** — What is it that the human being desires?

> **25:43** — Have you seen the one whose god is his own ego? Will you be his advocate?

> **45:23** — Have  you  noted  the  one  whose  god is  his  ego?  Consequently,  GOD sends  him  astray,  despite  his  knowledge,  seals  his  hearing  and  his  mind, and  places  a  veil  on  his  eyes.  Who then  can  guide  him,  after  such  a decision  by  GOD?  Would  you  not take heed?

> **17:11** — The human being often prays for something that may hurt him, thinking that he is praying for something good. The human being is impatient.

> **2:216** — Fighting may be imposed on you, even though you dislike it. But you may dislike something which is good for you, and you may like something which is bad for you. GOD knows while you do not know.

> **31:34** — With GOD is the knowledge regarding  the  Hour  (end  of  the  world).*  He is the One who sends down the rain, and  He  knows  the  contents  of  the womb. No soul knows what will happen  to  it  tomorrow,  and  no  one knows in which land he or she will die. GOD is Omniscient, Cognizant.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 53:24 directs my attention toward desire itself. In 25:43 and 45:23, desire can occupy a position it should not hold. Wanting an opportunity, a relationship, security, or success is not automatically wrong. The danger appears when “I want it” quietly becomes “Therefore it must be right.” A decision can be made before any evidence is considered if I allow desire to supply the verdict.

Verse 17:11 warns that a person may pray for something harmful while imagining it to be good, and links this vulnerability to impatience. I do not take this as a prohibition against planning, hoping, or praying. It challenges my certainty about outcomes. The immediate context of 2:216 concerns fighting. I will not turn that setting into a universal slogan to explain away somebody's pain. Yet the statement about the gap between human preference and knowledge of what is good deserves to be faced within its actual wording.

In 31:34, tomorrow's gain is not something I fully know. I can build a plan, assess risks, consult others, and work seriously. None of those actions makes the result mine to guarantee. Another human being's response is not an item I can schedule simply because I have decided what I want.

My earlier reflections, <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/53-24-what-is-it-that-the-human-being-desires-en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">What Is It That the Human Being Desires?</a> and <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/the_impatient_human_being_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The Impatient Human Being</a>, meet at this question: Can I want something strongly without allowing that desire to become my judge? Perhaps a mature decision does not require extinguishing desire. It requires bringing desire before knowledge, justice, patience, and GOD.

Verse references: 53:24; 25:43; 45:23; 17:11; 2:216; 31:34.

## 6. Two Paths: What Does a Choice Really Change?

### Verse

> **76:3** — We  showed  him  the  two  paths,  then, he  is  either  appreciative,  or  unappreciative.

We  have  prepared for  the  transgressors  a  fire  that  will completely  surround  them.  When they  scream  for  help,  they  will  be given  a  liquid  like  concentrated  acid that  scalds  the  faces.  What  a  miserable  drink!  What  a  miserable  destiny!

### Reflection on the Verse

Verses 76:3 and 90:10 speak of paths shown to the human being. Verses 73:19 and 76:29 present the possibility of choosing the way toward one's Lord. These are more than descriptions of ordinary planning. They ask what direction a human life takes. Recognizing the distinction between paths does not mean I know every future step, yet uncertainty about details does not make moral direction meaningless.

Verse 18:29 addresses willingness to believe or disbelieve while also speaking of consequences. The two parts must remain together. Being able to choose does not imply that all choices have the same moral character. Verses 17:18–21 place temporary worldly priorities and the Hereafter in contrast. The point is not merely what a person acquires but what that person's efforts have come to serve.

A day's decisions can each seem small and defensible: a little more money, a little more recognition, one more compromise to be liked. Together, however, they may form a direction. I sometimes ask myself, “If I lived this particular week again and again, what path would my choices trace?” The question is not answered by one public declaration of faith alone.

I cannot pronounce any particular person's ultimate destiny, including my own. But the limits of that knowledge do not prevent me from examining my priorities now. To choose is sometimes not merely to do one thing. It is to place that thing above others—and to live with the shape those priorities give my life.

Verse references: 76:3; 90:10; 73:19; 76:29; 18:29; 17:18; 17:19; 17:20; 17:21.

## 7. Human Will and GOD’s Will: Where Is the Boundary?

### Verse

> **81:28** — For those who wish to go straight.

> **81:29** — Whatever you will is in accordance with the will of GOD, Lord of the universe.

> **76:30** — Whatever you will is in accordance with GOD’s will. GOD is Omniscient, Wise. 

> **74:55** — For those who wish to take heed.

> **74:56** — They  cannot  take  heed  against GOD’s will. He is the source of righteousness;  He  is  the  source  of forgiveness.

> **28:68** — Your Lord is the One who creates whatever He wills, and chooses; no one else does any choosing. Glory be to GOD, the Most Exalted. He is far above needing partners.

> **57:22** — Anything  that  happens  on  earth,  or to  you,  has  already  been  recorded, even  before  the  creation.  This  is easy for GOD to do.

> **64:11** — Nothing  happens  to  you  except  in accordance with GOD’s will. Anyone who believes  in GOD, He  will guide  his  heart.  GOD  is  fully  aware of all things.

> **10:100** — No  soul  can  believe  except  in  accordance  with  GOD’s  will.  For  He places  a  curse  upon  those  who refuse to understand.

### Reflection on the Verse

When I read 81:28–29 together with 76:30, human willing and GOD's will appear in relation to one another. It would be simpler to remove one side and construct a theory from the other. The text does not permit that shortcut. Human direction is addressed seriously, while GOD's will is also affirmed. I am cautious about presenting a complete mechanical explanation of destiny where the verses have not supplied one.

Verses 74:55–56 return to the relationship between taking heed and GOD's will. Verse 28:68 speaks of GOD as Creator and Chooser. I should not convert that divine authority into a certificate that every personal preference of mine was selected and endorsed by GOD. Verses 57:22 and 64:11 bring prior recording and divine will into view. Verse 10:100 adds the serious matter of belief and the refusal to understand.

In my earlier reflection <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/destiny_free_will_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The Great Mystery of Destiny: Are We Free, or Is Everything Already Written?</a>, I examined a tension that remains here. If events do not escape GOD's knowledge, can I use that fact to excuse an injustice I commit? These verses do not give me such permission. Using destiny to silence someone who is suffering, or to make my own wrongdoing disappear, moves beyond what the words establish.

**I do not possess GOD's exhaustive knowledge, and I am not released from examining my own intention and conduct.** Perhaps part of submission is refusing to fill the space between these statements with a theory presented as if GOD had explicitly dictated every detail of it.

Verse references: 81:28; 81:29; 76:30; 74:55; 74:56; 28:68; 57:22; 64:11; 10:100.

## 8. When a Decision Concerns Faith: The Limit of Coercion

### Verse

> **2:256** — There shall be no compulsion in religion: the right way is now distinct from the wrong way. Anyone who denounces the devil and believes in GOD  has  grasped  the  strongest bond; one that never breaks. GOD is Hearer, Omniscient.

> **10:99** — Had  your  Lord  willed,  all  the  people  on  earth  would  have  believed.* Do you  want to  force  the people  to become believers?

> **50:45** — We are fully aware of everything they utter, while you have no power over them. Therefore, remind with this Quran, those who reverence My warnings.

> **88:21** — You  shall  remind,  for  your  mission is to deliver this reminder. 

> **88:22** — You have no power over them. 

> **10:108** — Proclaim:  “O  people,  the  truth  has come  to  you  herein  from  your  Lord. Whoever  is  guided  is  guided  for  his own  good.  And  whoever  goes astray,  goes  astray  to  his  own  detriment. I am not a guardian over you.” 

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 2:256 must remain central when we discuss religious choice. Verse 10:99 questions the desire to force people into belief. I may explain a verse, respond to doubts, or present evidence. But when I begin treating another person's conscience as an extension of my own will, I risk confusing invitation with domination.

Verses 50:45 and 88:21–22 distinguish reminding others from exercising power over them. I do not read these statements to mean that nobody may speak about faith. The duty to remind is expressly mentioned. The question concerns the difference between communicating a message and coercing a person into an outward declaration. However deeply I hope someone embraces what I regard as true, I cannot believe on that person's behalf.

In 10:108, the benefit of guidance and the detriment of straying are linked to the person concerned; the speaker is not appointed as a guardian over the audience. This leaves me with two obligations in a religious exchange: to be accurate in what I present and not to appoint myself as the ultimate owner of another human being's soul.

Publishing an article raises a related question. Do I want readers to attach themselves to my authority, or to examine the verses and take responsibility for their understanding? If I claim the latter, I must allow questions to remain genuine questions. Persuasion and coercion are different things; the distinction matters most when it is preserved in conduct rather than merely praised in words.

Verse references: 2:256; 10:99; 50:45; 88:21; 88:22; 10:108.

## 9. Expecting Change without Deciding to Change

### Verse

> **13:11** — Shifts  (of  angels)  take  turns,  staying with each one of you—they are in front  of  you  and  behind  you.  They stay with you, and guard you in accordance  with  GOD’s  commands. Thus,  GOD  does  not  change  the condition of  any  people  unless  they themselves  make  the  decision  to change.  If  GOD  wills  any  hardship for any people, no force can stop it. For  they  have  none  beside  Him  as Lord and Master.

> **8:53** — GOD does not change a blessing He has  bestowed  upon  any people unless  they  themselves  decide  to change.  GOD  is  Hearer,  Omniscient.

> **91:7** — The soul and Him who created it. 

> **91:8** — Then showed it what is evil and what is good.

> **91:9** — Successful is one who redeems it. 

> **91:10** — Failing is one who neglects it. 

> **20:115** — We  tested  Adam  in  the  past,  but  he forgot, and we found him indecisive.

> **8:24** — O you who believe, you shall respond to GOD and to the messenger when he invites you to what gives you life.* You should know that GOD is closer to you than your heart, and that before Him you will be summoned.

> **41:17** — As for Thamoud, we provided them with guidance, but they preferred blindness over guidance. Consequently, the disastrous and shameful retribution annihilated them, because of what they earned.

> **6:110** — We control their minds and their hearts. Thus, since their decision is to disbelieve, we leave them in their transgressions, blundering.

> **10:33** — This  is  what  your  Lord’s  decision does  to  those  who  choose  to  be wicked: they cannot believe.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verses 13:11 and 8:53 connect a people's change within themselves to changes in their circumstances. I cannot conclude from these statements that every illness, disaster, hardship, or poverty is the punishment for the sufferer's bad decisions. Such a claim would outrun the passages and could make me cruel toward people whose situation I do not understand. But there is a question I can ask myself: **What am I willing to change in the situation I keep wishing would change?**

Verses 91:7–10 bring the soul's moral orientation, purification, and corruption into view. Recognizing an unhealthy tendency is not yet the same thing as refusing to nourish it. Verse 20:115 describes Adam's forgetting and lack of resolve. I can reflect on the gap between knowing and holding fast without labeling every ordinary lapse of memory as the same spiritual failure.

Verse 8:24 speaks of responding to a call that gives life. Verse 41:17 describes choosing blindness over guidance. Verses 6:110 and 10:33 address the grave consequences connected with persistent rejection. Read with care, they make me consider how a choice may form the person who repeats it. I am not deriving a psychological law about every habit or every human brain from these verses. I am considering their moral warning: decisions are not always isolated events.

I would rather identify one honest change than announce that I have completely transformed myself. A lie I refuse to repeat, a right I return, an apology I stop postponing—each may reveal more than an impressive speech about self-improvement. The distance between talking about change and deciding to change may be one action, or it may take a lifetime to confront.

Verse references: 13:11; 8:53; 91:7; 91:8; 91:9; 91:10; 20:115; 8:24; 41:17; 6:110; 10:33.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verses 18:23–24 caution against speaking about tomorrow without remembering GOD's will. I understand that as more than a formula to attach to a calendar. What kind of certainty am I claiming when I speak of my plans? I can arrange work, take precautions, and make commitments while recognizing that I do not control every condition on which success depends.

Verse 65:3 identifies GOD as sufficient for the one who trusts Him; 3:160 addresses the source of support. Verse 8:46 warns that internal conflict can lead to failure and loss of strength. Verses 2:153, 3:200, 14:12, and 16:42 bring steadfastness and trust into difficult circumstances. I do not read them as a guarantee that every well-intentioned plan will unfold on a schedule I prefer. The passages connect effort, patience, and reliance, not a personal promise of immediate worldly success.

It is tempting to treat an achieved result as proof that all the means were righteous. Equally, a painful outcome can make a conscientious decision feel mistaken. These are different questions. A dishonest deal may produce income. Defending someone's right may initially cost opportunities. That observation is not a prediction about anyone's ultimate fate; it is a reason to examine the method as well as the outcome.

I ask myself: If my plan fails, do the principles I claimed to follow fail with it? Trust may mean continuing to act responsibly without claiming ownership of the future. Carrying out a decision belongs within my responsibility. Governing every consequence does not.

### Reflection on the Verse

Not every decision affects me alone. Verse 4:58 joins returning trusts to judging equitably. Verse 4:135 demands justice even when it bears against ourselves or those closest to us. Verse 5:42 speaks of equitable judgment. Verse 38:26 warns against allowing desire to corrupt judgment. Verse 16:90 connects justice with giving and honoring relatives' rights. Verse 45:21 challenges a judgment that treats wrongdoing and righteous conduct as though they were morally identical. When I choose someone for a job, attempt to resolve a dispute, or write critically about a person, I can examine my standard. ## 13. Does Giving My Word Finish the Decision?

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 4:128 allows for reconciliation when a relationship is strained. Verse 65:2 insists on fairness and testimony even when separation occurs. I cannot conclude that deciding to end a relationship cancels the other person's rights. Sometimes the hardest part of a decision begins after the choice itself has been made.

Verses 17:34 and 5:1 speak of fulfilling obligations and covenants. The long verse 2:282 includes concrete attention to recording debts, time, and testimony. I do not derive from it a single unchangeable form for every modern agreement. But the emphasis on clarity and accountability is difficult to ignore. Making a promise can feel effortless in a hopeful moment; honoring the rights created by that promise years later is another matter.

Verse 4:29 distinguishes consensual trade from illegitimate appropriation. Verse 33:15 reminds us of responsibility for commitments. A decision made in my own mind may become another person's schedule, expense, expectation, or source of trust. I cannot erase those consequences merely by saying, “I genuinely meant it at the time.”

Circumstances can change, and there may be difficult reasons why a commitment cannot proceed as imagined. Such cases require care rather than easy condemnation. But that complexity is no reason to forget the other person's perspective. Faithfulness is not merely a feeling present when a decision is announced. It is an ethical demand that may continue long after the announcement.

Verses 3:173 and 29:2 raise the question of fear and testing. Verse 9:24 asks where attachments to family, possessions, and comfort stand when compared with devotion to GOD. Sometimes a righteous decision may cost something. Yet the fact that I suffer for a choice does not prove it was right. **I must not confuse cost with truth.** The hard task is to refuse both easy intimidation and the temptation to glorify hardship as if hardship itself were moral evidence.

Making a mistake is not the same as deciding to defend it forever. ## 19. Arabic Roots: Are Decision, Counsel, and Judgment One Thing?

### Verse

> **12:67** — And  he  said,  “O  my  sons,  do  not enter  from  one  door;  enter  through separate  doors.  However,  I  cannot save  you  from  anything  that  is  predetermined  by  GOD. > **12:80** — When  they  despaired  of  changing his  mind,  they  conferred  together. Their eldest said,  “Do you realize that your father has taken a solemn pledge  from  you  before  GOD?  In the past you lost Joseph. I am not leaving  this  place  until  my  father gives me permission, or until GOD judges for me; He is the best Judge.

> **43:79** — Have they schemed some scheme? We too are scheming.

> **74:18** — For he reflected, then decided.

> **74:19** — Miserable is what he decided.

> **74:20** — Miserable  indeed  is  what  he  decided.

### Reflection on the Verse

In 12:67, a father offers a practical precaution while acknowledging that all judgment belongs to GOD. Verse 12:80 places consultation among brothers alongside an individual's resolve to remain and wait for GOD's ruling. Verse 43:79 addresses planning. Verses 74:18–20 describe a decision reached after thought and then condemn it. In English, all of these can be discussed under “decision.” The underlying Arabic does not necessarily use the same word. That matters: I should not collapse different actions into one imagined mechanism.

The word **عَزَمْتَ** (*ʿazamta*) in 3:159 belongs to the root **ع-ز-م (ʿ-z-m)**, associated in this setting with firm resolve. **شَاوِرْهُمْ** (*shāwirhum*) in the same verse and **شُورَىٰ** (*shūrā*) in 42:38 belong to **ش-و-ر (sh-w-r)**, the language of consultation. The instruction **فَتَوَكَّلْ** (*fa-tawakkal*) in 3:159 is related to **و-ك-ل (w-k-l)** and entrusting oneself to GOD. These are not interchangeable stages: consulting, resolving, and trusting remain distinct.

The verb **تَحْكُمُوا۟** (*taḥkumū*) in 4:58 and **ٱلْحُكْمُ** (*al-ḥukm*) in 12:67 are connected with **ح-ك-م (ḥ-k-m)**. Context distinguishes equitable human judgment from GOD's ultimate ruling. **شَآءَ** (*shā'a*) in 76:29 is associated with **ش-ي-ء (sh-y-ʾ)**, the language of willing. **يَخْتَارُ** (*yakhtāru*) in 28:68 is connected to **خ-ي-ر (kh-y-r)**, choosing. Wishing, choosing, judging, and resolving to act should not be blurred just because one modern word can cover them.

In 74:18, **فَكَّرَ** (*fakkara*) from **ف-ك-ر (f-k-r)** expresses thinking. The following **وَقَدَّرَ** (*wa-qaddara*), associated with **ق-د-ر (q-d-r)**, adds the field of assessing or devising. Here careful thought does not make the resulting decision morally sound; the following verses condemn it. Nor does every occurrence of this root family settle an entire doctrine of destiny. Roots can deepen attentive reading, but they cannot replace a verse's sentence and context. The wording of **Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation** remains the governing translation reference; when I refer to Turkish wording, I mean **the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation**.

Verse references: 12:67; 12:80; 43:79; 74:18; 74:19; 74:20.

## Other Claims

Some religious traditions attach special significance to seeking guidance through practices such as istikhara, dreams, inward impressions, or anticipated signs. People may find meaning in such practices, but I cannot derive a universally binding decision technique of this kind from 3:159, 42:38, 17:36, or 18:23–24. In particular, a dream cannot simply be presented as Quranic authorization to judge another person, violate a promise, or act unjustly. Distinguishing a tradition's possible interpretation from the verse's explicit wording matters here.

Other approaches explain divine will as though the human being has no meaningful responsibility. At the opposite extreme, some treat the human will as entirely independent. Reading 81:28–29, 76:29–30, 57:22, and 53:39–42 together, I am wary of either neat simplification. Human direction, GOD's will, and personal accountability all appear. I do not want to erase one by turning the others into a closed system that claims more than the text provides.

Some accounts reduce consultation to a courtesy, while others treat every opinion given in consultation as binding. Verses 3:159 and 42:38 speak clearly to the value of consultation, but do not specify a universal number of advisers, a mandatory voting rule, or one procedure for every disagreement. There may be different practical approaches, but their details should not be presented as the explicit words of revelation.

Another claim promises visible success whenever a decision is righteous and immediate failure whenever it is wrong. Verses 17:18–21, 2:216, and 53:39–42 do not supply that simple schedule of worldly outcomes. My caution about these readings is not a rejection of human experience. It is a refusal to assign an interpretation the authority of the verse itself.

## Conclusion

I began by asking which option I should choose. Now I see that the question is not sufficient on its own. Verse 3:159 brings consultation, carrying out a plan, and trusting GOD together. Verse 17:36 asks how I know what I claim to know. Verse 4:135 refuses to let a decision escape justice simply because it benefits me. Verses 76:29–30 prevent me from discussing the human will as though GOD's will were absent.

The question continues after a choice is made. Verse 17:34 places responsibility on my promises. Verses 25:27–29 bring the influence of companionship into view. Verse 13:11 challenges me to consider what I am willing to change. Verses 39:53–54 leave open the seriousness of returning after wrongdoing. Some consequences arrive at once. Others extend across years. Some may remain beyond my sight throughout this life.

I do not expect to reach a point where I can guarantee that every decision is perfect. I may hesitate, fail to understand, ask for help, or discover that I was wrong. None of these experiences authorizes me to turn a lie into truth, injustice into fairness, or my desire into revelation. I want to keep examining whose rights my words and decisions affect.

Verses 53:39–42 and 99:7–8 remind me that actions are not limited to their immediate visible results. I do not know the final account in its detail, and I cannot assign another person's place in Paradise or retribution. But while waiting to see what a decision produces, I can recognize that **the manner in which I decide already has moral consequences**. I may be practicing honesty, justice, and responsibility, or practicing new excuses for abandoning them.

Perhaps the deepest test of decision-making is not proving that I am always right. It is **investigating when I do not know, listening to those affected, holding to what is just, returning when I err, and never allowing my will to occupy the place that belongs to GOD**. My decision appears in my conduct. The final judgment does not belong to me.
