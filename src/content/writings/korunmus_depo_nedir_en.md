# [77:21] What Is the Well-Protected Repository? The Womb, Creation, Measure, and 19 Reflections on Responsibility

*What can an origin we cannot remember teach us about the life we must now live?*

**Author:** Berk KÖKSAL  
**Date:** November 27, 2026  
**Place:** Mersin

## Introduction

**Greetings. Peace be upon you.**

While working through the repeated warnings in Sura 77, *Dispatched (Al-Mursalaat)*, I kept returning to a brief description tucked between them. It speaks about human beginnings and then names a place: a **well-protected repository**. The phrase unsettled me. A repository for what? Why is it protected? Who placed something there, and for how long?

My first answer was simple: the mother's womb. But a simple answer may be the beginning rather than the end of careful thought. Verse 77:21 does not contain the explicit Arabic noun for a womb. Other verses do. How can I read these passages together without pretending they all use identical words? That question matters beyond anatomy. It concerns the difference between what a verse states, what another verse helps me infer, and what my own explanation adds.

The adjective *protected* creates another risk. I could take it to mean that mothers and children are promised a life without injury or loss. That would be an ungrounded and potentially painful claim to make to someone facing illness, miscarriage, or grief. The surrounding verses speak of a period and a measure, not a lifetime guarantee of safety.

I begin with **77:21** and follow its immediate context in **77:20–24**, its close counterpart in **23:12–16**, and the explicit references to wombs and mothers in **22:5, 39:6, and 3:6**. From there, the inquiry extends to parents, gratitude, knowledge, and the question of resurrection. My personal reflection is present throughout, but it remains separate from the wording of the verses.

I want to know what the protected place means. I also want to ask what changes in me when I recognize that the life I now call my own began through protection I did not provide.

## 1. A Human Beginning Behind an Ordinary Word

### Verse

**77:21**

**Arabic**

> فَجَعَلْنَاهُ فِي قَرَارٍ مَكِينٍ

**Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation**

> Then we placed it in a well-protected repository.

**The Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation**

> Sonra onu iyi korunmuş bir depoya yerleştirdik.

<a href="https://kuranteyit.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Source: kuranteyit.com</a>

### Reflection on the Verse

The word *repository* usually evokes something impersonal: a building, a container, a place where objects are kept. But I encounter it in a passage about the beginning of a human life. Suddenly the word is no longer cold. What was placed there? What makes the place protected? And why should I think about my own origin through an image that sounds so ordinary?

The first thing I notice in 77:21 is an action: something was placed. There is a difference between the one who places and what is placed. I did not arrange my own beginning. I did not prepare the environment in which my life first developed. I can work, plan, compose music, and sometimes change the direction of my life. None of those abilities makes me the author of my own origin.

I could rush from this statement toward a complete scientific explanation. Yet that would risk losing the first question before I have properly asked it. The verse gives a brief description; it does not present a medical textbook. Its words invite close examination, but they also challenge my pride. The life I call mine began under conditions I neither selected nor understood. That simple realization is already enough to disturb the illusion of absolute self-sufficiency.

**Verse references:** 77:21.

## 2. What Was Placed There? Read the Sentence Before It

### Verse

> **77:20** — Biz sizi basit bir sıvıdan yaratmadık mı?*

> **77:21** — Sonra onu iyi korunmuş bir depoya yerleştirdik.

### Reflection on the Verse

A single verse can become so familiar that I forget to look at the sentence preceding it. In 77:21, the object is described simply as “it.” If I want to understand the pronoun, I must return to 77:20. The previous verse speaks of our creation from a simple liquid. The next verse then speaks of placing it in a protected location. The relationship between the two sentences matters more than any speculative image I might add.

I can infer that a liquid associated with the beginning of human creation is being placed into a secure environment. I cannot infer, from these two sentences alone, a list of cell structures, a complete account of reproduction, or the anatomical name of every layer surrounding a developing child. Those may be topics for careful study. They are not all contained in this particular wording.

The question in 77:20 also turns my attention toward myself. It does not allow me to pretend that my present abilities existed from the beginning. I remember achievements more readily than dependence; I remember decisions more readily than the conditions that made deciding possible. There is no need to deny genuine human effort. I only need to remember that effort belongs to a life whose foundations I did not create.

**Verse references:** 77:20; 77:21.

## 3. Protection Is Given for a Period

### Verse

> **77:22** — Spesifik bir süre için.

> **77:23** — Onu tam olarak ölçtük.* Biz en iyi tasarımcılarız.

### Reflection on the Verse

Security is not the same as permanence. That distinction becomes clear when 77:22 places the protected environment within a specified period. Then 77:23 speaks of precise measure. The passage presents a movement rather than a timeless storage facility: an origin, placement, duration, and measure.

I should resist the temptation to replace “a specific period” with a single modern number and declare that the verse has established an identical timetable for every human pregnancy. The wording does not do that. It tells me that the process is limited and measured, but it does not supply the complete chronology of every individual case. Recognizing this is not a retreat from inquiry. It is part of being faithful to what I actually read.

There is also a more personal question. I often want protection to mean that nothing will ever change: no illness, no separation, no uncertainty, no loss. Yet here even the protected place belongs to a period that ends. What does it mean to live responsibly within a limited time? Perhaps being protected at the beginning is related to becoming capable of life beyond that first shelter. That is my reflection, not an additional description secretly contained in the verse. The distinction matters because I want to contemplate scripture without making my own thought its unspoken sentence.

**Verse references:** 77:22; 77:23.

## 4. The Same Expression Appears Again

### Verse

> **23:12** — Biz insanı belirli bir tür çamurdan yarattık.

> **23:13** — Ardından, onu iyi korunan bir depoya yerleştirilen küçücük bir damladan ürettik.

### Reflection on the Verse

Another passage helps keep my imagination close to the language. In 23:13, a tiny drop is also said to be placed in a well-protected repository. The wording corresponds closely to the expression in 77:21. Here again the subject is human creation, not an ordinary warehouse, an information archive, or a hidden chamber in the sky.

Reading the passages together does not erase their separate contexts. One gives a short reference to placement and a measured period; the other appears within a longer account of the human being's origin and development. Their shared expression makes the natural direction of interpretation more secure. I no longer have to give the word *repository* whichever symbolic meaning I happen to prefer.

But a secure beginning should not be made into a promise that every later stage of life will be free of pain. The same chapter that describes the repository continues toward death and resurrection. This is a crucial boundary for me. A verse about protection at a particular beginning does not tell a suffering mother, child, or family that hardship cannot occur. If the text teaches me wonder, that wonder should not prevent me from seeing people's real difficulties.

**Verse references:** 23:12; 23:13.

## 5. Development Is More Than a Label

### Verse

> **23:14** — Sonra o damlayı asılı bir (embriyoya) dönüştürdük, sonra o asılı (embriyoyu) bir çiğnemlik (fetüs) hâline getirdik, sonra bu çiğnemlik (fetüsü) kemikler hâlinde yarattık, sonra da o kemikleri etle kapladık. Biz böylece yeni bir yaratığı üretiriz. En kutlu olan TANRI’dır, en iyi Yaratıcı.

> **23:15** — Sonra, ilerleyen zamanlarda, ölürsünüz.

> **23:16** — Sonra, Diriliş Günü’nde, siz diriltileceksiniz.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 23:14 follows a succession of changes. Its language moves from the drop through embryonic and fetal descriptions to the production of a new creature. The sentence does not pause to offer a complete laboratory diagram. It concludes with praise of the Creator. The emphasis, as I read it, lies not only in naming stages but in recognizing the significance of a living being coming into existence.

I could turn each expression into a technical claim and then attempt to map every contemporary biological category onto it. But doing so would require decisions made by me, not supplied in full by the verse. A careful reader ought to distinguish the textual sequence from a modern scientific model. To respect the text is not to keep it away from serious investigation; it is to avoid pretending that a particular investigation and the wording of revelation are identical.

The following verses widen the horizon. Life proceeds toward death, and death is followed by the question of resurrection. This movement changes what I am asking. Knowing the probable physical referent of the repository answers one question. It does not answer why scripture returns to the beginning when addressing human destiny. I cannot choose my origin; I cannot command my life to continue indefinitely. How, then, ought I use the period entrusted to me? That ethical question remains even after a biological explanation becomes clearer.

**Verse references:** 23:14; 23:15; 23:16.

## 6. Where Does the Quran Explicitly Say Wombs?

### Verse

> **22:5** — Ey insanlar, eğer yeniden diriliş hakkında herhangi bir şüpheniz varsa, (hatırlayın ki) sizi topraktan ve sonrasında küçücük bir damladan yarattık; ki o, bir asılı (embriyoya) dönüşür, sonra o, hayat verilen veya cansız sayılan bir fetüs olur. Biz böylece size koşulları açıklığa kavuşturuyoruz. Biz irade ettiğimizi rahimlere önceden belirlenmiş bir süre için yerleştiririz.* Sonra sizi bebekler olarak dışarı çıkarırız, sonra olgunluğa erişirsiniz. Bazılarınız genç ölürken, sırf belirli bir sınırın ötesinde daha fazla bilgi edinilemeyeceğini öğrenmeleri için diğerleri en kötü yaşa kadar yaşar. Ayrıca, ölü olan bir toprağa bakarsın, sonra, onun üzerine su yağdırdığımız anda, o hayatla titreşir ve her türden güzel bitkiler yetiştirir.

### Reflection on the Verse

I want to be precise about a small but decisive distinction. The Arabic text of 77:21 does not contain the explicit noun for the womb. Yet 22:5 does speak of placing what is willed into wombs for a predetermined period. This verse also discusses the drop, embryonic development, birth, maturity, and differing lengths of life. It provides a strong connection between the protected location in 77:21 and the mother's womb.

This does not authorize me to edit the wording of 77:21. A contextual identification is not the same thing as a word-for-word translation. One verse emphasizes the quality of the location; another directly identifies wombs within a closely related account. To preserve both differences and connections is better than silently merging them into a sentence neither verse actually states.

The argument in 22:5 begins with doubt about resurrection. That setting deserves attention. Human beginnings are not introduced merely to satisfy curiosity about anatomy. They are part of a much larger reflection on creation and the possibility of life restored. I can investigate biology and still acknowledge that the verse is asking a question biology, by itself, does not frame as a theological conclusion. For me, responsible thinking means neither rejecting observation nor pretending scientific study automatically answers every metaphysical question.

**Verse references:** 22:5.

## 7. Within Mothers: The Place Is Not an Abstraction

### Verse

> **39:6** — O sizi tek bir kişiden yarattı, sonra ondan da eşini yarattı. Size sekiz tür çiftlik hayvanı indirdi. Sizi annelerinizin karınlarında, yaratılıştan yaratılışa, üç karanlık dönemde yaratmaktadır. İşte Rabbiniz TANRI böyledir. Bütün hükümranlık O’na aittir. O’nun yanında başka tanrı yoktur. Nasıl olur da saparsınız?

### Reflection on the Verse

In 39:6 I encounter a more concrete description: creation occurring in mothers' bodies, through three periods of darkness. The protected repository is no longer a free-floating metaphor in my imagination. The passage brings me close to the physical reality of pregnancy, while also speaking of GOD's authority and the call to worship Him alone.

The mention of three dark periods raises questions. I can picture anatomical layers or stages of development, and I can examine different explanations. But the verse does not identify those layers or stages with a technical list. An exact identification beyond the text needs its own evidence. If I call my preferred model the only possible meaning, I risk substituting confidence for knowledge.

There is something else that can get lost in technical debate: a mother's lived reality. In the womb, a human life begins to develop while the mother herself continues to live, work, hope, and sometimes suffer. I should not turn the phrase *protected place* into a picture in which the person carrying the child disappears. This moral insight arises as I bring the verse into conversation with the verses on parents and care; it is not a claim that 39:6 alone lists every family responsibility. The verse places creation before me. I want that awareness to make me less arrogant, not more certain about matters it leaves unstated.

**Verse references:** 39:6.

## 8. I Was Formed; I Did Not Form Myself

### Verse

> **3:6** — İrade ettiği gibi rahimlerde size biçim veren O’dur. O’ndan başka tanrı yoktur; Kudretlidir, En Bilgedir.

### Reflection on the Verse

I have never designed my own face. I did not choose the basic features of my voice or determine the circumstances in which my body first took shape. In 3:6, the shaping of human beings in wombs is related to GOD's will. The verse makes me reconsider the way I speak of being entirely self-made.

But it would be a mistake to convert dependence in creation into exemption from responsibility. My physical characteristics and my later moral decisions are not the same thing. I may not have chosen my body's beginnings. I still have choices about truthfulness, consideration, and whether to repair a wrong I have done. The verse does not excuse injustice by reminding me who gave me life.

It also gives me a reason to hesitate before ranking others by appearance or ability. I do not know everything another person faces in their own body or circumstances. If remembering my beginning produces only pride in a claim of privileged knowledge, perhaps I have missed the humility the verse invites. I would rather let the recognition of being formed lead me toward more careful speech and less effortless contempt. That is an ethical contemplation drawn from the verse, not a detailed social statute attributed to its few words.

**Verse references:** 3:6.

## 9. Two Parents, a Human Life, and a Test

### Verse

> **76:2** — Biz insanı, onu test etmek amacıyla sıvı bir karışımdan, iki ebeveynden yarattık. Böylece onu işiten ve gören yaptık.

> **76:3** — Ona iki yolu da gösterdik, sonra o, ya minnettardır ya da nankör.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 76:2 describes human creation through a fluid mixture associated with two parents. This helps me resist an incomplete picture in which the mother is mentioned only as a location and the father is absent from human beginnings. Yet mentioning two parents does not, by itself, establish that every family distributes care fairly. To draw a practical ethic, I need to consider the passages that actually address conduct, gratitude, and responsibility.

The following verse speaks of guidance and of human gratitude or ingratitude. That transition interests me deeply. I begin life entirely dependent on conditions I cannot provide for myself; later I become someone capable of responding to what has been given. Receiving life does not automatically determine the moral direction of that response.

I must also resist romanticizing every family. People do not all grow up in safety. Some know neglect, absence, or conflict. The protected beginning in the passage is not a certificate that all homes are protected or that every parent acts well. I can honor the importance of parents while remaining honest about human differences and suffering. The more carefully I separate what is described about creation from what I know about actual lives, the less likely I am to use a religious reflection to silence someone else's experience.

**Verse references:** 76:2; 76:3.

## 10. Hearing, Seeing, Thinking: The Gift After the Beginning

### Verse

> **32:7** — Yarattığı her şeyi mükemmelleştiren ve insanın yaratılışını çamurdan başlatan O’dur.

> **32:8** — Sonra O, onun çoğalmasını belirli basit bir sıvıyla devam ettirdi.

> **32:9** — O, ona biçim verdi ve ona, O’nun ruhundan üfledi. Ve O, size işitme, görme ve beyinler verdi; nadiren müteşekkir oluyorsunuz.

### Reflection on the Verse

After passages about the origins of human reproduction, 32:9 names hearing, sight, and brains. Those words draw my attention away from the moment of placement and toward what a person may later become capable of doing. A life beginning in profound dependence grows into a life that can listen, perceive, learn, and speak. The passage also questions how little gratitude people show.

As a musician I rely upon hearing; as a writer, upon attention; in relationships, upon the patience to understand another person rather than simply waiting to speak. I can use these abilities and forget that I did not manufacture their beginnings. I can also mistake ability for moral worth. Being able to say something persuasively does not make it true. Producing something beautiful does not make every purpose behind it good.

I do not claim 32:9 contains a complete philosophy of art or communication. It does, however, connect created capacities with gratitude. That relation opens questions I cannot avoid: Do I use the capacities I have to help or to manipulate? Does my skill make me more receptive to correction or more resistant to it? The protected repository is not the end of the story. It is the earliest part of a life whose later actions matter.

**Verse references:** 32:7; 32:8; 32:9.

## 11. Why Does the Passage Return to Resurrection?

### Verse

> **75:37** — Atılan meniden bir damla olan o değil miydi?

> **75:38** — Sonra ondan da bir embriyo yarattı!

> **75:39** — Onu erkek ya da dişi hâline getirdi!

> **75:40** — Öyleyse O’nun ölüleri yeniden canlandırmaya gücü yetmez mi?

### Reflection on the Verse

Verses 75:37–40 move quickly. They recall the drop, embryonic development, the human being's sex, and finally the question of whether the Creator can bring the dead back to life. In their movement, I recognize why reducing the protected repository to an anatomical term would leave the reflection unfinished.

The identification of the womb is important. Yet the verses bring another issue before me: the distance between an inconspicuous beginning and a person who can now ask questions about life and death. The passage treats that distance as something worth contemplating. I do not have to claim that one laboratory observation directly proves a complete doctrine of resurrection in order to take that invitation seriously.

Nor should I use the promise of resurrection to dismiss the pain of death. I can acknowledge the theological question and still recognize grief as real. A person who has just lost a child or parent does not need me to turn a verse into an uninvited speech explaining every hidden reason. The passage is weighty enough without my adding certainty about another person's suffering. I want to answer its question by thinking carefully, not by speaking as though I know more than I do.

**Verse references:** 75:37; 75:38; 75:39; 75:40.

## 12. Does a Protected Beginning Guarantee a Protected Life?

### Verse

> **40:67** — Sizi topraktan, sonrasında küçücük bir damladan, sonra asılı duran bir embriyodan yaratan O’dur; sonra sizi bir çocuk olarak dışarı çıkarır, sonra olgunluğa ulaşmanıza izin verir, sonra yaşlanırsınız—bazılarınız daha erken ölür. Önceden belirlenmiş bir yaşa ulaşırsınız ki anlayasınız.

> **40:68** — Hayatı ve ölümü kontrol eden O’dur. Bir şeyin yapılması için O ona sadece “Ol” der o da olur.

### Reflection on the Verse

The human lifespan described in 40:67 is not a single uniform journey. The passage moves through origin, infancy, maturity, and old age; it also mentions that some die earlier. This matters for my reading of 77:21. If I treat the protected repository as a promise that the entire earthly life of every person will remain unharmed, I have added something the text does not say.

That false promise can become cruel. Illness, miscarriage, premature death, and loss are not abstractions to those who experience them. I cannot claim to know why each of these tragedies happens. Verse 40:68 speaks of GOD's power over life and death; it does not give me the undisclosed reason behind every individual event. Saying “I do not know” may be more faithful, and certainly more humane, than producing explanations the passage does not supply.

A reflective article ought not end at intellectual caution. If recognizing a fragile beginning makes me more alert to the fragility of other people, perhaps it should also make me more willing to help. Words of gratitude have a moral dimension when someone nearby needs patience, care, or practical assistance. This is not a medical law extracted from the verse. It is the responsibility I hear when I read human vulnerability alongside divine power.

**Verse references:** 40:67; 40:68.

## 13. Why Is the Warning Placed After Human Creation?

### Verse

> **77:20** — Biz sizi basit bir sıvıdan yaratmadık mı?*

> **77:21** — Sonra onu iyi korunmuş bir depoya yerleştirdik.

> **77:22** — Spesifik bir süre için.

> **77:23** — Onu tam olarak ölçtük.* Biz en iyi tasarımcılarız.

> **77:24** — O gün, reddedicilerin vay hâline.

### Reflection on the Verse

Immediately after the description of human origin, placement, time, and measure, Sura 77 repeats its stern warning about rejectors. It is easy to read the creation verses in isolation and lose that structure. But the sequence seems to press a question: What happens when a person refuses to consider the beginning of the life they now take for granted?

I know how attractive self-sufficiency can be. When I complete a demanding project, effort and personal responsibility are real. Yet I can overstate what those accomplishments mean. I may forget that I did not choose the body, mind, or time through which I pursued them. Remembering my origin need not diminish work. It may simply correct my tendency to turn achievement into an absolute claim about myself.

I recently asked who the rejectors are. The question returns here, but I should not use this context as permission to judge every individual who disagrees with me. I know too little about another person's intentions and knowledge. The warning first asks what *I* am willing to investigate and what I refuse to consider. If a passage about my origin makes me more interested in condemning others than in examining myself, something has gone wrong in my response to it.

**Verse references:** 77:20; 77:21; 77:22; 77:23; 77:24.

## 14. An Inner Shelter and an Earthly Home

### Verse

> **77:25** — Biz yeryüzünü bir mesken yapmadık mı?

> **77:26** — Hem diriler için hem de ölüler için?

> **77:27** — Onun üzerine yüksek dağlar yerleştirdik ve size içmeniz için tatlı su sağladık.

> **77:28** — O gün, reddedicilerin vay hâline.

### Reflection on the Verse

After the protected location, Sura 77 turns to the earth as a home for the living and the dead, then mentions mountains and fresh water. Two environments appear in succession: a place of early human development and the wider world in which human life continues. The movement from one to the other is striking.

Yet I do not want to flatten the Arabic into a single recurring technical term. The phrase for the protected abode and the wording describing the earth are not simply the same word repeated. Their relationship is conceptual, not proof of identical roots. One scene emphasizes a secure place; the other reminds us of the earth's capacity to accommodate life and death.

The mention of drinking water also moves the reflection toward daily responsibility. Water sustains me now just as I once depended upon conditions I could not create. I did not build either environment from nothing. Does this recognition make me less careless with resources and more attentive to other people's needs? The verse does not provide an environmental regulation manual. But it does invite gratitude for concrete provisions. For me, gratitude that never touches conduct remains too small a response to such a large reminder.

**Verse references:** 77:25; 77:26; 77:27; 77:28.

## 15. The Mother Must Not Disappear Behind the Metaphor

### Verse

> **31:14** — Biz insana ebeveynlerine hürmet göstermesini tembih ettik. Annesi onu taşıdı ve yük ağırlaştıkça ağırlaştı. Sütten kesilinceye kadar iki yıl (yoğun bakımı) sürer. Bana ve ebeveynlerine minnettar ol. Nihai varış Banadır.

### Reflection on the Verse

There is a risk in speaking endlessly about a “repository.” The mother's body can become a mere container in the way we talk, while the mother herself becomes invisible. Verse 31:14 interrupts that tendency. It speaks of the increasing burden of carrying a child, a period of care, and gratitude toward GOD and one's parents.

The protected place is not an object without a human life around it. A mother may experience strain, fear, responsibility, joy, and exhaustion. No two lives are identical, and the verse should not be used to claim that all mothers experience the same conditions. What I can see clearly is that the account of birth does not end with an impersonal location. It calls attention to a relationship and to gratitude.

I also need to ask what gratitude looks like when it leaves the page. Respect toward parents is not demonstrated only by praising them in a paragraph. It has something to do with the way I speak to them, whether I make room for their needs, and how I respond when they are vulnerable. I would rather allow the verse to disturb my indifference than use it to compose a beautiful thought that changes nothing.

**Verse references:** 31:14.

## 16. Birth Is a Moment; Care Takes Time

### Verse

> **46:15** — Biz insana ebeveynlerine hürmet göstermesini tembih ettik. Annesi onu zahmetle taşıdı, zahmetle doğurdu ve otuz ay boyunca yakın bakımını üstlendi. O, olgunluğa erişince ve kırk yaşına* varınca şöyle söylemelidir, “Rabbim, beni, bana ve ebeveynlerime ihsan ettiğin nimetlere minnettar olmaya ve Seni memnun edecek olan doğru işleri yapmaya yönelt. Evlatlarım da doğru kimseler olsun. Ben Sana tövbe ettim; ben bir teslimolanım.”

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 46:15 speaks of the mother's difficulty in carrying and giving birth and of prolonged care. That sequence prevents me from narrowing the whole story of beginning to one instant. A human being does not become independent the moment the protected period ends. Much of life begins through the work and attention of other people.

I am especially moved by the prayer later in the verse. The speaker asks for gratitude toward blessings given to self and parents, for the ability to do what is right, and for the children to be upright. Repentance is mentioned as well. A reflection on the womb has become a reflection on the moral maturity of someone who has grown up.

There is a modest comparison I can make with my creative work, without confusing it with childbirth. An idea's arrival and its responsible development are not the same event. Something may begin beautifully and then require patience, correction, and care. Human life is far more significant than a project, of course. But this contrast helps me recognize that the work of sustaining life matters. The verse draws gratitude out of abstraction and toward people whose effort made my own growth possible.

**Verse references:** 46:15.

## 17. Respect Is Not Unlimited Human Authority

### Verse

> **17:23** — Senin Rabbin, O’ndan başkasına tapmamanızı ve ebeveynlerinize hürmet gösterilmesini hükme bağlamıştır. Onlardan biri ya da her ikisi yaşadığı sürece, onlara asla “Öf” deme (rahatsız edici en ufak bir davranış), onlara bağırma da; onlara dostça davran.

> **17:24** — Ve onlar için alçak gönüllülüğün ve iyiliğin kanatlarını indir ve de ki: “Rabbim, onlara merhamet et, çünkü onlar beni bebeklikten itibaren yetiştirdiler.”

> **31:15** — Eğer seni Benim yanımda herhangi bir put edinmeye zorlamaya çalışırlarsa, onlara itaat etme. Ancak bu dünyada onlara dostça davranmaya devam et. Yalnızca Bana teslim olmuş olanların yolunu takip et. Nihayetinde, hepinizin geri dönüşü Banadır, sonra Ben size yapmış olduğunuz her şeyi bildiririm.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verses 17:23–24 speak about the manner in which parents are treated: even a small expression of irritation matters; shouting and harshness are rejected; kind words and a prayer for mercy are included. When I remember that I began by depending on other people, these instructions become more concrete.

But gratitude must not be turned into blind obedience. Verse 31:15 places a limit on following parents when they pressure someone toward associating others with GOD, while still requiring friendly conduct toward them in this life. The tension is not between caring and thinking, but between honoring people and assigning them authority that belongs elsewhere. I can decline a demand without despising the person who makes it.

I must also avoid pretending that these brief passages settle every complicated family circumstance from a distance. I do not know the private history of a household or the safety of each person involved. I can preserve the principle of respect and the principle of limits without turning either into a weapon. A protected beginning is not an argument for unlimited control over the one who was born. It should make me more grateful and more just, not less attentive to boundaries.

**Verse references:** 17:23; 17:24; 31:15.

## 18. Contemplate Your Creation Without Inventing Certainty

### Verse

> **86:5** — İnsan kendi yaratılışı üzerine derinlemesine düşünsün.

> **86:6** — Dışarı atılan sıvıdan yaratıldı.

> **86:7** — Omurga ile iç organlar arasından.

> **86:8** — O elbette onu diriltmeye muktedirdir.

> **17:36** — Kendin için teyit etmediğin sürece, hiçbir bilgiyi kabul etme. Ben sana işitmeyi, görmeyi ve beyni verdim ve sen onları kullanmaktan sorumlusun.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 86:5 asks the human being to examine their creation. The following verses speak of the fluid from which human life begins and then return to GOD's power to resurrect. Here, study and contemplation appear together. I want to take that invitation seriously without confusing study with the obligation to declare my first explanation flawless.

Discussions of 86:6–7 sometimes move quickly toward modern anatomical diagrams. Such comparisons can be explored, but they require evidence beyond a confident assertion. The verse does not become stronger when I quietly replace its wording with the terminology of a contemporary field. Verse 17:36 is valuable to me here: I should not follow claims without knowledge. It reminds me that faithfulness includes restraint about what I have not established.

Nor is 86:5–8 simply a dictionary entry explaining the repository in 77:21. It belongs here because it asks us to think about human origins and resurrection. The passages may illuminate each other without being made to say identical things. My purpose is not to keep knowledge from the text. I want to keep my own conclusions distinguishable from it, so that a future correction of my understanding does not become an accusation that revelation has changed.

**Verse references:** 86:5; 86:6; 86:7; 86:8; 17:36.

## 19. From a Protected Beginning to a Life Accountable

### Verse

> **23:15** — Sonra, ilerleyen zamanlarda, ölürsünüz.

> **23:16** — Sonra, Diriliş Günü’nde, siz diriltileceksiniz.

> **77:13** — Karar Günü’dür.

> **77:38** — Bu, Karar Günü’dür. Sizi de önceki nesilleri de topladık.

### Reflection on the Verse

After considering the protected repository for so long, I return to the Day of Decision. Verses 23:15–16 connect human life with death and resurrection; 77:13 and 77:38 speak of the appointed decision and the gathering of people. The beginning is not the whole human story. It is a starting point within a life whose end and accountability are not under my private control.

This perspective does not reduce the value of earthly action. On the contrary, it makes the time between beginning and end matter more. I did not choose the first environment of my existence, yet I now have words to speak, work to do, people to treat fairly, and errors I can still correct. A beginning beyond my control does not make my later conduct irrelevant.

The main question can finally be answered without claiming more than the text supports. The protected repository is best understood, in light of the related verses, as the mother's womb. Its precise Arabic phrase speaks of a secure place; the explicit term for wombs is found elsewhere in a related account. That identification should deepen, not end, my reflection. It ought to lead toward gratitude for life, respect for parents without surrendering judgment, humility about knowledge, and responsibility for how I live. I do not know everything about my beginning, nor can I declare a final judgment about myself. I can, however, refuse to treat the life entrusted to me as something trivial.

**Verse references:** 23:15; 23:16; 77:13; 77:38.

## Arabic Word Roots

The phrase in 77:21 can sound like a single familiar expression when translated, but it contains two Arabic words: **قَرَارٍ مَكِينٍ** (*qarārin makīn*). Each contributes to the meaning, and their distinction matters.

**قَرَارٍ — qarār; ق-ر-ر (q-r-r).** This root belongs to the semantic field of settling, remaining, stability, and an abode. In 77:21 the noun refers to a place in which something settles. The same phrase appears in 23:13, again within an account of human creation. A *repository* conveys the idea of placement and protection, but the Arabic does not require an image of a modern warehouse with shelves and doors. Nor should the familiar Turkish word *karar*, meaning a decision, override the Arabic context. **(77:21; 23:13)**

**مَكِينٍ — makīn; م-ك-ن (m-k-n).** The root is associated with being firmly established, secure, and capable of remaining in place. Here the adjective describes the abode. Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation uses **“well-protected”**; the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation uses **“iyi korunmuş.”** Neither wording, by itself, names a particular anatomical layer or guarantees that every pregnancy will be free of harm. **(77:21; 23:13)**

**جَعَلْنَاهُ — jaʿalnāhu; ج-ع-ل (j-ʿ-l).** The verb belongs to the field of making, setting, and placing. Its final pronoun means *it*. The preceding verse helps establish its reference: the simple fluid mentioned in 77:20. I should not detach a pronoun from its sentence and then invent an entirely new object for it. **(77:20–21)**

**قَدَرٍ — qadar; ق-د-ر (q-d-r).** In 77:22 this word is associated with a specified period, while 77:23 continues the theme of measure. This is **not** the same root as *qarār* in 77:21. Similar transliteration does not make two Arabic roots identical. Here, place and duration are distinct parts of the passage. **(77:21–23)**

**رَحِم — raḥim; ر-ح-م (r-ḥ-m).** The explicit Arabic term for the womb occurs in 22:5 and is also central to 3:6. It does not appear in the wording of 77:21. That is why I call the identification of the protected repository with the mother's womb a strong *cross-verse interpretation*, not a word-for-word replacement. **(22:5; 3:6; 77:21)**

**نُطْفَة — nuṭfah; ن-ط-ف (n-ṭ-f).** Used for a tiny drop in 23:13 and 22:5, this term helps contextualize the simple fluid in 77:20. Their meanings overlap in the discussion of human origins, but I cannot treat every Arabic word describing the beginning of life as linguistically identical.

The wording about the earth as a dwelling in 77:25 is also different from the phrase in 77:21. There can be a meaningful thematic connection between the inner beginning and the wider home of life without claiming identical Arabic roots. For me, this is the value of linguistic study: not to manufacture a secret meaning out of similar sounds, but to preserve the distinctions the text actually makes.

## Other Claims

The most familiar interpretation identifies the **well-protected repository with the mother's womb**. Classical commentators, including al-Ṭabarī, make this identification in discussing 77:21. Read alongside the explicit mention of wombs in 22:5 and the parallel phrase in 23:13, the interpretation is well supported. Nevertheless, a commentator's explanatory noun is not literally present in every verse being explained. <a href="https://quran.com/ar/al-mursalat/21/tafsirs/ar-tafsir-al-tabari" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Al-Ṭabarī's commentary on 77:21</a> illustrates that distinction.

Some modern explanations turn the adjective *well-protected* into a catalogue of medical and anatomical features, sometimes claiming that one modern biological model has been proven directly by the verse. Such proposals can be examined, but they require evidence external to the sentence. Identifying the womb as the probable referent and claiming that all details of embryology are explicitly encoded in 77:21 are very different propositions.

The explanatory note accompanying 77:20–23 in the supplied Turkish translation refers to an embryology textbook and discusses **266 days** and **38 weeks**, noting their numerical relation to 19. That claim belongs to the explanatory note rather than to the actual wording of 77:22. The verse does not supply those numbers. It should not be made to guarantee that every pregnancy follows one exact number of days.

Other readings treat the repository as a symbolic storehouse of human destiny rather than a physical setting of early development. I find that suggestion less directly supported by the immediate connection with a simple fluid in 77:20 and by the closely parallel passage in 23:13. Yet I should neither dismiss the question without investigation nor judge another person's faith by whether they agree with my inference.

The essential distinction is between **the wording of the verse, a conclusion supported by other verses, a translator's note, traditional commentary, and contemporary scientific investigation**. Each can be discussed, but none should quietly replace the others.

## Conclusion

My question was concrete: **What is the well-protected repository?** Verse 77:21 speaks of something being placed in a secure location. The preceding verse recalls our origin from a simple fluid, and the following verses describe a specified period and precise measure. Verse 23:13 contains the same Arabic expression; 22:5 and 3:6 explicitly refer to wombs; 39:6 describes creation within mothers. Taken together, these passages make **the mother's womb a strongly supported interpretation** of the protected repository.

But a strong interpretation does not change the literal wording of 77:21. The verse does not itself use the explicit noun for the womb. I can learn from other verses without silently rewriting the one I began with. This may be one of the most important lessons of the whole inquiry: to keep the authority of revelation distinct from the confidence of my explanation.

The word *protected* also cannot be made to promise that every mother and child will remain safe throughout life. Verse 40:67 acknowledges different life spans; 23:15–16 speaks of death and resurrection. I should not meet someone in pain with a confidently invented account of why it happened. The passage concerns a protected beginning and invites reflection on life, not denial of suffering.

Nor should the mother disappear behind an anatomical metaphor. Verses 31:14 and 46:15 mention the burden of carrying, childbirth, care, and gratitude. A beginning in a protected place is connected with human labor and love. Verses 17:23–24 and 31:15 remind me that honoring parents must coexist with moral limits. Respect does not require surrendering judgment or overlooking injustice.

This topic also speaks to earlier reflections. In <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/duties-toward-parents-in-the-quran-en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">“Duties toward Parents in the Quran”</a>, I examined respect and conscience within family relationships. In <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/destiny_free_will_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">“The Great Mystery of Destiny”</a>, I asked whether being measured or known in advance erases responsibility. Those questions remain relevant here: I did not choose my origin, but does that free me from what I do now? No such escape follows from the verses considered.

The phrase no longer sounds impersonal to me. Behind the apparently ordinary word is a beginning I cannot remember and could never have managed by myself. From that beginning I have been given a life in which I can investigate, listen, love, make mistakes, and correct them. **I want gratitude for that protected beginning to become responsibility in the life I am living now.**
