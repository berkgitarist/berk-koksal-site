# [68:36] What Is Wrong with Your Logic? The Distance Between Being Right and Judging Rightly

**Author:** Berk KÖKSAL
**Date:** November 22, 2026
**Place:** Mersin

The purpose of this reflection is to consider each verse alongside related verses, and to explore what bearing witness to the oneness of GOD means for knowledge, justice, truthfulness, and submission in human life.

Peace be upon you.
If someone asked me, "What is wrong with your logic?" I might instinctively defend myself. But the question in 68:36 is not a clever way to silence an opponent. Its neighboring verses ask whom we are treating as equivalent, what book authorizes our judgment, and who could possibly guarantee the outcome we expect. The conscience is under examination alongside the mind.
I want to think about reasoning without reducing it to an elegant arrangement of arguments. What do I know? What am I only guessing? Whose voice have I allowed to become decisive? And can I still recognize truth when it cuts against my own interest?

## 1. A Problem with Logic, or a Problem with Judgment?

### Verse

> **68:36**
> **Arapça:** مَا لَكُمْ كَيْفَ تَحْكُمُونَ
> **English:** What is wrong with your logic?
> **Türkçe:** Mantığınızdaki sorun nedir?

Source: <a href="https://kuranteyit.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">kuranteyit.com</a>

### Reflection on the Verse

I hear 68:36 less as a verdict on someone's intelligence than as a question about the foundations of a judgment. Isolated from its surroundings, it might sound like a general rebuke to anyone who thinks badly. Read alongside the preceding verse, however, it concerns treating Submitters and criminals alike. The following questions turn to a book, assurances, a guarantor, and idols. The sequence matters. The issue is not only how a conclusion was reached, but what entitles anyone to pronounce it. (68:35–41)

A person can reach a decision first and employ reason afterward to protect it. Arguments may sound orderly; each sentence may follow smoothly from the last; the speaker may even win the room. Yet elegance cannot redeem a false starting assumption. This is why the verse unsettles me. Is my mind moving toward what is true, or is it becoming a talented advocate for what I already want?

I do not want to turn GOD's question into an insult aimed at other people. The verse does not appoint me the examiner of their minds. Its first honest application, for me, is to examine my own judgments.

Verse references: 68:35–41.

## 2. Submitters and Criminals: Where Does Justice Begin?

### Verse

> **68:34** — The  righteous  have  deserved,  at their Lord, gardens of bliss.

> **68:35** — Shall we treat the Submitters like the criminals?

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 68:34 speaks of gardens of bliss for the righteous. Verse 68:35 immediately asks whether the Submitters should be treated like criminals. Only then comes the question about logic. This is a moral distinction before it is an abstract dispute about reasoning. Can different choices be made equivalent merely because those making them desire the same outcome?

That does not authorize me to label everyone around me as righteous or criminal, still less to claim complete knowledge of their final standing before GOD. The sharper question concerns my own life. Can I profess submission, neglect its demands, and simply assume that the outcome I prefer is assured?

Justice is not the art of promising everyone whatever feels comforting. Read together, 68:34–35 call me to distinguish between my wishes and a judgment grounded in what has actually been disclosed. The distinction should make me more accountable, not more eager to condemn others.

Verse references: 68:34–35; 68:36.

## 3. Another Book, or Only What We Want to Find in a Book?

### Verse

> **68:37** — Do you have another book to uphold?

> **68:38** — In  it,  do  you  find  anything  you want?

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 68:37 asks about another book. Verse 68:38 then asks whether that book contains whatever one desires. In that movement I recognize an uncomfortable possibility: seeking guidance and searching for confirmation are two very different activities. A book can be approached as a measure against which I test myself, or as a shelf from which I take only the sentences I already wanted to hear.

This is not an argument against reading widely or learning from other people. The question concerns the grounds for a claim made with religious certainty. When someone says GOD guarantees a particular outcome, is that assurance really there? Where does the claim come from, and what might it have left out?

My own writing is not exempt. I can decorate a cherished opinion with a verse instead of allowing the verse to question the opinion. The first method makes me feel right; the second might help me become more truthful.

Verse references: 68:37–38; 68:36.

## 4. Mistaking a Desire for a Guarantee

### Verse

> **68:39** — Or, have you received solemn assurances  from  us  that  grant  you whatever you wish on the Day of Resurrection?

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 68:39 asks about assurances extending to the Day of Resurrection. There is a tremendous difference between hoping for something and having a promise from GOD that it will happen. Repetition, confidence, and the agreement of people around me do not turn a hope into a divine guarantee.

I understand the longing for a good end. I share the desire for mercy. But hope and knowledge are not identical. I do not read the verse as forbidding hope. I read it as questioning our right to declare an assurance that has not been given. That distinction makes hope more honest, not less meaningful.

Without it, the reasoning I call "logic" may simply become a method of comforting myself. Submission also involves refusing to distribute promises in GOD's name when I cannot show that He made them.

Verse references: 68:39; 68:36.

## 5. Who Guarantees It? The Limits of Invented Authority

### Verse

> **68:40** — Ask them, “Who guarantees this for you?”

> **68:41** — Do they have idols? Let their idols help them, if they are truthful.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 68:40 asks the question that every unsupported assurance would rather avoid: Who guarantees this? Verse 68:41 proceeds to the supposed help of idols and demands truthfulness. A claim does not acquire authority merely because the person repeating it appears important or speaks with conviction.

Reputation can fill the space where evidence ought to be. A teacher may be admired, a tradition may be old, and a community may have repeated the same statement for generations. None of those facts alone establishes a promise from GOD. The question about truthfulness presses beyond tone and prestige: can the claim be accounted for?

I am not calling every respected human being an idol. My concern is the authority I assign to a human voice. Have I turned an explanation into an unquestionable guarantee? If so, the problem begins not merely in what was said, but in what I surrendered to the person saying it.

Verse references: 68:40–41.

## 6. The Garden Owners: When Certainty Meets What It Refused to See

### Verse

> **68:18** — They were so absolutely sure.

> **68:25** — They were so absolutely sure of their harvest.

> **68:26** — But when they saw it, they said, “We were so wrong!

> **68:28** — The righteous among them said, “If only you had glorified (God)!”

> **68:29** — They said, “Glory be to our Lord. We have transgressed.”

### Reflection on the Verse

Earlier in the same sura, the owners of the garden are repeatedly certain of what will happen. Then the sight before them drives them to admit that they were wrong. A righteous person among them recalls the need to glorify GOD, and they acknowledge their transgression. The movement from certainty to confession is striking. (68:18, 25–29)

Planning is not itself a failure. We must make decisions with the knowledge available to us. The failure begins when a plan is promoted into an infallible judgment. The owners of the garden discover how short the distance can be between "we were sure" and "we were so wrong." The scene makes me uneasy because I recognize the same temptation in myself.

What if a project, an expectation, or a religious opinion that I have protected with great confidence is mistaken? The garden account does not supply a technique for predicting the future. It gives me something more searching: a reason to stay open to correction, and a reminder that acknowledging error can be more truthful than blaming everyone else for it.

Verse references: 68:18; 68:25–29; 68:36.

## 7. Verification Is a Responsibility We Cannot Delegate

### Verse

> **17:36** — You  shall  not  accept  any  information,  unless  you  verify  it  for  yourself. I  have  given  you  the  hearing,  the eyesight,  and  the  brain,  and  you  are responsible for using them.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 17:36 places the responsibility for accepting information on me. Hearing, eyesight, and brain are gifts for whose use I am answerable. Another person's sincerity, expertise, or reputation cannot simply take that responsibility away. "Someone told me" does not by itself establish that I know.

Verification does not mean pretending to be an expert on everything. I may need to ask questions, consult people who know more, compare evidence, and admit what remains uncertain. What I must not do is conceal from myself the difference between a reason I have examined and an assertion I have merely inherited.

In my earlier reflection [The Most Argumentative Creature](https://berkkoksal.com/writings/the_most_argumentative_creature_en/), I considered the difference between seeking truth and needing to be right. Here, 17:36 draws that distinction into everyday responsibility: after hearing a confident answer, have I actually verified what I am claiming?

Verse references: 17:36; 68:36–38.

## 8. The Distance Between Knowledge and Conjecture

### Verse

> **53:28** — They had no knowledge about this; they only conjectured. Conjecture is no substitute for the truth.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 53:28 draws a hard line: conjecture is no substitute for truth. An idea can seem plausible to me. Several guesses can support one another. A room full of people can repeat them. But none of this automatically transforms conjecture into knowledge.

I do not think the verse prevents us from considering possibilities while searching. Questions and hypotheses may be part of inquiry. The moral problem starts when I change the label on my uncertainty. If I mean "I suspect" but speak as though I mean "I know," I may influence another person's choice with certainty I do not possess.

This makes the question of 68:36 especially demanding. An argument can be perfectly arranged and still rest on an unverified premise. No skill in drawing conclusions can replace honesty about what the premises actually establish.

Verse references: 53:28; 68:36.

## 9. When Unverified Information Becomes Injustice

### Verse

> **49:6** — O you who believe, if a wicked person brings any news to you, you shall first investigate, lest you commit injustice towards some people, out of ignorance, then become sorry and remorseful for what you have done.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 49:6 makes verification a matter of justice. A false report is not merely an intellectual error when another person may be harmed by what we do with it. By the time I say, "It sounded reasonable," the injury may already have happened.

There is a certain comfort in reaching a quick verdict. Uncertainty is tiring. An accusation supplies a story, and a story is often easier to live with than unanswered questions. Investigation interrupts that comfort. It requires patience, the willingness to suspend judgment, and sometimes the courage to disappoint people expecting me to choose a side.

The verse specifically addresses news brought by a wicked person. I should not stretch it into a claim that every messenger is wicked or that every report is false. Its immediate lesson is already serious enough: do not let deficient knowledge lead you to act unjustly toward others.

Verse references: 49:6; 68:36.

## 10. Examining Every Word: From Listening to Following

### Verse

> **39:18** — They  are  the  ones  who  examine  all words,  then  follow  the  best.  These are  the  ones  whom  GOD  has  guided;  these  are  the  ones  who  possess intelligence.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 39:18 describes people who examine words and then follow what is best. Listening is not the end of the process. Examination leads to a choice, and a choice carries responsibility. It is possible to collect quotations without ever allowing them to challenge one's first conclusion.

I notice how much patience I can devote to evidence that supports a position I like, and how quickly I can dismiss evidence that troubles it. At such moments my investigation may be theater: the judgment was reached before the hearing began. The verse does not tell me that all claims are equally true. It praises examination and following the best, not the refusal to decide.

There is a quiet discipline here. A mind willing to hear what it would rather not hear is less easily turned into the servant of its own pride. That discipline is essential when I ask what might be wrong with my logic.

Verse references: 39:18; 68:36.

## 11. Can the Majority Decide What Is True?

### Verse

> **6:116** — If you obey the majority of people on earth, they will divert you from the path of GOD. They follow only conjecture; they only guess.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 6:116 refuses to let the majority stand in for truth. When enough people repeat a claim, disagreement begins to feel improper. Consensus can become so loud that we mistake the number of voices for the weight of the evidence.

Yet the verse also identifies conjecture and guessing as the danger. It does not support the opposite vanity: "I am in the minority, therefore I must be right." Being alone is no proof of accuracy, just as being surrounded by agreement is no proof of it. The question is what the belief rests upon.

I imagine hearing 68:36 in the middle of a crowd. If my entire answer is "Everyone thinks so," then I have not yet given the kind of account the surrounding verses demand. Numbers cannot do the work of truthfulness for me.

Verse references: 6:116; 68:36.

## 12. We Found Our Parents Doing This: Inheritance Is Not Proof

### Verse

> **2:170** — When  they  are  told,  “Follow  what GOD  has  revealed  herein,”  they  say, “We  follow  only  what  we  found  our parents  doing.”  What  if  their  parents  did  not  understand,  and  were not guided?

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 2:170 distinguishes what has been inherited from what has been understood. We learn language, habits, and much of our first moral vocabulary from our parents. To acknowledge this is not to accuse them. But discovering that something was practiced in my family does not establish that GOD commanded it.

The verse's question allows for the possibility that one's parents lacked understanding or guidance. I can respect their lives without surrendering my judgment to every practice I received. Gratitude for what was handed down and responsibility to examine it need not be enemies.

In [We Found Our Parents Doing This](https://berkkoksal.com/writings/we_found_our_parents_doing_this_en/), I reflected at greater length on inherited belief. Read alongside 68:37–38, the question now becomes still more personal: am I listening for what revelation says, or searching revelation for what I have always expected it to say?

Verse references: 2:170; 68:37–38.

## 13. Witnessing the Oneness of GOD: Knowledge and Justice

### Verse

> **3:18** — GOD  bears  witness  that  there  is  no god except He, and so do the angels and  those  who  possess  knowledge. Truthfully  and  equitably,  He  is  the absolute  god;  there  is  no  god  but He, the Almighty, Most Wise.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 3:18 draws knowledge, truthful witness, justice, power, and wisdom into a single statement of GOD's oneness. Testimony is not presented merely as a sound made by the mouth. It is inseparable from the truth and equity with which the verse speaks.

I cannot derive a complete map of every person's faith from this one verse. Yet I cannot ignore the challenge it poses to my own confession. What does it mean to bear witness that GOD is one while knowingly making room for injustice in the way I deal with others? The verse does not permit me to treat the ethical content of witness as an afterthought.

GOD is the Almighty, Most Wise. My knowledge is not His knowledge. I reach judgments, reconsider them, and sometimes discover I was wrong. That contrast gives 68:36 a spiritual depth: my reasoning is exercised before the One to whom final wisdom belongs.

Verse references: 3:18; 68:36.

## 14. Can I Testify Against My Own Interest?

### Verse

> **4:135** — O  you who  believe,  you  shall  be absolutely  equitable,  and  observe GOD,  when  you  serve  as  witnesses, even  against  yourselves,  or  your parents,  or  your  relatives.  Whether the  accused  is  rich  or  poor,  GOD takes  care  of  both.  Therefore,  do not  be  biased  by  your  personal wishes.  If  you  deviate  or  disregard (this  commandment),  then  GOD  is fully  Cognizant  of  everything  you do.

### Reflection on the Verse

What disturbs me most in 4:135 is its insistence on justice even against oneself. The human mind can become a brilliant defense attorney when its own interests are on trial. I may call my failure an understandable exception and another person's similar failure a moral outrage.

The verse mentions parents, relatives, wealth, poverty, and personal wishes. It refuses to let any of these decide the truthfulness of testimony. Here the question about judgment stops being theoretical. Can I acknowledge a fact that costs me something? Do I apply a different name to the same act depending on who committed it?

A fault in logic is not always caused by lack of information. Sometimes the information is present, but desire has been allowed to select the verdict. Verse 4:135 brings the ethical discipline of reasoning into the place where I am most tempted to evade it: my own case.

Verse references: 4:135; 68:36.

## 15. Can I Remain Just toward Someone I Dislike?

### Verse

> **5:8** — O  you  who  believe,  you  shall  be  absolutely  equitable,  and  observe GOD,  when  you  serve  as  witnesses. Do  not  be  provoked  by  your  conflicts  with  some  people  into  committing  injustice.  You  shall  be  absolutely  equitable,  for  it  is  more  righteous. You  shall  observe  GOD.  GOD  is fully  Cognizant  of  everything  you do.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 5:8 reaches beyond fairness toward family and friends. Conflict with someone must not provoke us into injustice. Resentment can make an accusation look bigger, a person's rights look smaller, and contrary evidence seem unworthy of attention.

There are moments when I think I am speaking on behalf of truth but may actually be giving my anger an articulate voice. Even if my sentences fit together flawlessly, the picture may have been distorted by what I chose to omit. The problem is no longer merely logical consistency. It is whether my judgment remains answerable to justice when I dislike the person concerned.

I read 5:8 as the social test of 68:36. Rather than claiming to know another person's ultimate standing, I can ask a question within my own reach: am I bearing witness fairly, especially when doing so is difficult?

Verse references: 5:8; 68:36.

## 16. When Religious Leaders Become Lords

### Verse

> **9:31** — They  have  set  up  their  religious leaders  and  scholars  as  lords,*  instead of GOD. Others deified the Messiah, son of Mary. They were all commanded  to  worship  only  one god. There is no god except He. Be He glorified, high above having any partners.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 9:31 describes a serious danger: religious leaders and scholars may be set up as lords instead of GOD. It raises a distinction I cannot afford to blur—the difference between learning from another person and granting that person's words ultimate religious authority.

A knowledgeable teacher can help me discover what I did not know. Nothing here requires contempt for teachers or scholars. But if I elevate an interpretation to the status of GOD's own judgment simply because a respected person pronounced it, I may have surrendered more than I realized. At that point, "He said so" begins to replace the work of inquiry and accountability.

The question of 68:40 returns: who guarantees this? Who has authority to promise it? These verses do not require me to accuse every teacher of idolatry. They require me to keep the boundary between the servant and GOD from disappearing inside my admiration for another human being.

Verse references: 9:31; 17:36; 68:40.

## 17. Whose Judgment Is Final: Revelation or a Human Voice?

### Verse

> **6:114** — Shall I seek other than GOD as a source of law, when He has revealed to you this book fully detailed?* Those who received the scripture recognize that it has been revealed from  your  Lord,  truthfully.  You shall not harbor any doubt.

> **7:3** — You  shall  all  follow  what  is  revealed to  you  from  your  Lord;  do  not  follow any  idols  besides  Him.  Rarely  do you take heed.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 6:114 asks why anyone would seek a source of law other than GOD when the book has been revealed fully detailed. Verse 7:3 calls people to follow what is revealed by their Lord rather than idols beside Him. Together they give substance to the question about another book in 68:37. What, finally, am I treating as my authority?

It would go beyond these verses to claim that every human institution, every interpretation, and every practical arrangement is automatically forbidden. The text does not provide such an exhaustive list. The narrower and more demanding issue is this: when I assert a binding religious judgment in GOD's name, am I showing where it comes from, or concealing a personal decision behind a sacred label?

Submission is not an ornament attached to a conclusion I have already chosen. It means being willing to have that conclusion measured against what GOD has revealed. This, I think, is why the question of 68:36 cannot be separated from the question of authority.

Verse references: 6:114; 7:3; 68:36–38.

## 18. The Ethics of Debate and the Courage to Say I Do Not Know

### Verse

> **16:125** — You shall invite to the path of your Lord  with  wisdom  and  kind  enlightenment, and debate with them in the best possible manner. Your Lord knows best who has strayed from His path, and He knows best who are the guided ones.

> **2:32** — They  said,  “Be  You  glorified,  we have  no  knowledge,  except  that which  You  have  taught  us.  You  are the Omniscient, Most Wise.”

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 16:125 does not abolish debate. It calls for wisdom, kind enlightenment, and the best possible manner of discussion. Seeing a weakness in another person's argument does not entitle me to humiliate them. Volume, ridicule, and the pleasure of winning are not measures of truth.

Verse 2:32 provides a spiritual counterweight: the speakers acknowledge that their knowledge extends only to what GOD has taught them. Knowing something matters. Knowing that my knowledge has limits matters too. Before exposing another person's error, I should be willing to recognize the boundary of my own understanding.

That returns me to a final personal question. When I ask, "What is wrong with your logic?" do I desire truth, or the feeling of standing above someone else? Reason detached from justice, speech detached from kindness, and confidence detached from humility may serve my ego very well. They will not necessarily serve the truth.

Verse references: 16:125; 2:32; 68:36.

## 19. Arabic Roots: Judgment, Scripture, Guarantee, and Partners

### Verse

> **68:35** — Shall we treat the Submitters like the criminals?

> **68:36** — What is wrong with your logic?

> **68:37** — Do you have another book to uphold?

> **68:40** — Ask them, “Who guarantees this for you?”

> **68:41** — Do they have idols? Let their idols help them, if they are truthful.

### Reflection on the Verse

The Arabic of 68:36 includes **مَا لَكُمْ** (*mā lakum*) and **كَيْفَ** (*kayfa*), expressions forming the question. It would be misleading to force them into invented three-letter roots. The pivotal verb is **تَحْكُمُونَ** (*taḥkumūn*), from **ح ك م** (*ḥ-k-m*), a root associated with judging and ruling. Rashad Khalifa's Authorized English Translation uses "logic"; the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation uses "mantık." There is no separate Arabic noun meaning "logic" in this verse. The wording points toward the act of rendering judgment. That nuance does not cancel the translation; it reminds us to read the question in its context.

In 68:35, **ٱلْمُسْلِمِينَ** (*al-muslimīn*) is connected to **س ل م** (*s-l-m*) and the field of submission. In 68:37, **كِتَٰبٌ** (*kitābun*) belongs to **ك ت ب** (*k-t-b*), the field of writing and a written book. The same verse contains **تَدْرُسُونَ** (*tadrusūn*), from **د ر س** (*d-r-s*), associated with reading or studying. Judgment, submission, and engagement with a book appear within one chain of questions. None of these lexical observations is a license to replace the actual verse with a private dictionary-based interpretation.

In 68:40, **زَعِيمٌ** (*zaʿīm*), from **ز ع م** (*z-ʿ-m*), points toward someone undertaking or guaranteeing a claim. In 68:41, **شُرَكَآءُ** (*shurakāʾ*), from **ش ر ك** (*sh-r-k*), concerns partners, while **صَٰدِقِينَ** (*ṣādiqīn*), from **ص د ق** (*ṣ-d-q*), concerns truthfulness. These roots do not reveal a secret code. They help me listen more carefully to the questions already present.

What emerges is not a diagnosis of intelligence. It is a question about the grounding of judgment, the claimed source of a guarantee, and the integrity of the person speaking. Root study is useful only insofar as it returns me to those responsibilities.

Verse references: 68:35–41; 6:114.

## Other Claims

Different approaches to 68:35–41 may emphasize different parts of the exchange. Some read the questions chiefly as a challenge concerning the justice of the final recompense. Others focus on the human tendency to read a book selectively or to declare assurances without adequate grounds. These emphases need not be identical, and I would not present either as a complete, certain reconstruction of every intended detail.

A narrower claim is that the word "logic" in 68:36 refers only to formal logical rules. Another approach takes the Arabic verb **تَحْكُمُونَ**, with its field of judging or ruling, as an invitation to consider moral judgment and authority as well. The nearby questions make that broader reading worth considering, though a lexical observation alone cannot establish an entire historical interpretation.

Religious authority also produces opposite overstatements: that a respected teacher's explanations must be accepted without question, or that learning anything from a teacher is itself impermissible. Read with 9:31, 17:36, 6:114, and 7:3, neither extreme should be asserted as though these verses supplied every detail needed to justify it. These outside positions do not determine the argument here; the wording and sequence of the quoted verses do.

## Conclusion

I return to 68:36. At first it is tempting to answer, "What is wrong with your logic?" by listing the mistakes of others: unsupported claims, conjecture, conformity, self-interested judgments. Each concern becomes serious when read through the verses we have considered. Yet the hardest demand is not to identify another person's inconsistency. **It is to allow the same standard to examine my own words.**

Verse 68:35 prevents me from flattening every moral distinction. Verses 68:37–38 ask whether I have made scripture say what I want it to say. Verses 68:39–41 challenge the guarantees I claim to possess. Verse 17:36 makes verification my responsibility; 53:28 separates conjecture from truth; 49:6 reminds me that poor judgment can injure other people. Verse 39:18 calls for examination, while 4:135 and 5:8 demand fairness when it costs something. In 3:18, testimony to the oneness of GOD stands with truth and equity.

For this reason, I cannot understand sound judgment as an intellectual achievement alone. Reasoning is tested by truthfulness, truthfulness by testimony, testimony by justice, and justice by submission to GOD. A person may arrange sentences flawlessly while reaching a judgment that those duties cannot sustain.

Perhaps the most difficult question is this: **When truth contradicts my cherished conclusion, am I prepared to change?** If not, my reasoning may be working very hard. I still need to ask whom it is serving. I want to hear 68:36 first as a question placed before my own conscience, not as a weapon held against somebody else's.

Verse references: 68:35–41; 17:36; 53:28; 49:6; 39:18; 3:18; 4:135; 5:8; 9:31; 16:125.
