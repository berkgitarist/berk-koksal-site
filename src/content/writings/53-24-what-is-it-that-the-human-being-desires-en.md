# [53:24] What Is It That the Human Being Desires?

**Author:** Berk KÖKSAL  
**Date:** October 18, 2026  
**Place:** Mersin

The central purpose of this article is to reflect on 53:24 together with other related verses, and to consider, with sincerity and depth, what bearing witness to the oneness of GOD means in human life in terms of knowledge, justice, truthfulness, and submission.

Peace. Peace be upon you.

It is easy to ask what a human being wants. It is harder to ask why that thing matters so much. Harder still is another question: because the desire is mine, does the outcome belong to me as well?

53:24 leaves a very short question before me. Yet it is wide enough to reach almost every part of life. I may desire money, health, love, success, peace, visibility, an open door, another person’s understanding, acceptance for a work I have made, or even the feeling of being proven right in a religious matter. The object changes; the desiring self remains.

I am not trying to make desire the enemy in this reflection. The verses do not tell me that every desire is evil. They also speak of Paradise as a place where people receive what they wish for. The issue is not the existence of desire, but its place. Does desire walk behind guidance, or does it try to make guidance walk behind it? Is a wish submitted to GOD, or has it become a claim that makes itself the measure?

In earlier reflections I considered the impatient human being, the ego, true wealth, trust in GOD, and what it means to love GOD above all. Here I want to return to a simpler question standing among all of them: **What is it that the human being desires?**

## 1. Facing Desire Itself: What Do I Really Want?

### Verse

> **53:24**
>
> أَمْ لِلْإِنسَٰنِ مَا تَمَنَّىٰ
>
> What is it that the human being desires?
>
> İnsanın arzuladığı şey nedir?

Source: <a href="https://kuranteyit.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">kuranteyit.com</a>

### Reflection on the Verse

The question “What is it that the human being desires?” first turns my attention not to the object outside me, but to the direction inside me. Two people may want the same thing for entirely different reasons. Money can be a means of caring for a family, or a source of superiority. Knowledge can be a way toward truth, or a tool for ruling over others. Even religious visibility can serve remembrance of GOD, or it can serve the enlargement of the self.

The verse does not immediately condemn desire. It asks. That matters to me. A person can become so accustomed to his own desire that he no longer thinks it needs examination. “I want this” begins to sound like a sufficient argument by itself. Yet 53:24 invites me to examine the quality of the desire before merely defending its existence. What do I want? Why do I want it? Where would its fulfillment take me? If it is not fulfilled, will my relationship with GOD change?

This is where the reflection becomes uncomfortable. Even when I pray to GOD, is GOD at the center of the prayer, or only the result I want from Him? If I am grateful when I receive what I want but lose all sense of submission when I do not, perhaps my deepest attachment is not to GOD but to the outcome of my request.

I cannot derive from this verse alone that a human being should want nothing. But I can ask something more honest: does desire close me inside myself, or does it help me place myself correctly before the knowledge and judgment of GOD?

In Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation, the word used is “desires”; in the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation, it is rendered as “arzuladığı.” The wording suggests an inward orientation toward something not yet possessed. But the existence of that orientation does not prove that it is right. The question itself calls it into examination.

Verse reference: 53:24.

## 2. The Limit of Desire: Who Owns This World and the Hereafter?

### Verse

> **53:25** — To  GOD  belongs  both  the  Hereafter, and this world.

### Reflection on the Verse

When 53:25 follows 53:24, the direction of the question changes for me. The human being desires, but both this world and the Hereafter belong to GOD. Desire may arise within me; ownership of reality does not pass to me.

This distinction is fundamental to submission. Wanting something is not the same as possessing a right to receive it. Wanting a door to open does not mean that it must open. Being convinced that an opportunity is good for me does not mean that I truly know its consequences. Desiring another person’s love does not give me ownership of that person’s will. The world is not mine; neither are other hearts, the future, outcomes, or the Hereafter.

This also changes the way I look at my own work. I may compose, write, build software, research, and labor seriously. My effort is real. But I do not own the final response of the world. I cannot fully determine how many people will care, how long a work will live, or which doors it will open. Desire can energize effort; when it turns into a claim of ownership, it can also become a burden.

53:25 does not seem to erase desire. It places desire under a larger reality. If the world belongs to GOD, worldly desire cannot take GOD’s place. If the Hereafter belongs to GOD, I cannot manufacture guarantees of salvation through status, intermediaries, or my own preferred system. Ownership belongs to Him. My place is the place of a servant who asks, works, bears witness, and ultimately submits.

Verse reference: 53:25.

## 3. When Desire Moves Ahead of Guidance

### Verse

> **53:23** — These  are  but  names  that  you  made up,  you  and  your  forefathers.  GOD never  authorized  such  a  blasphemy. They  follow  conjecture,  and  personal  desire,  when  the  true  guidance  has  come  to  them  herein  from their Lord.

### Reflection on the Verse

53:23, immediately before 53:24, shows a dangerous form of desire: following conjecture and personal desire after true guidance has come.

The problem is not that desire exists. The issue is the order between desire and guidance. If I first decide what I want to believe and then make the verses serve that conclusion, personal desire may be directing the research even while the research looks serious. If I enlarge what supports me and reduce what challenges me, the line between knowledge and conjecture can become blurred.

This matters especially when writing about the Quran. I may hold a strong view. I may have defended it for years. I may have published an article, recorded a video, or built an explanation into software. Then I may encounter a verse or evidence that forces me to reconsider. At that moment, what am I protecting: truth, or my previous self?

Desire is sometimes obvious: money, status, pleasure. Sometimes it is quiet: the desire to remain right. The desire not to admit error. The desire to protect a group identity. The desire never to discover that someone I admire was mistaken. These too can become forms of personal desire.

Reading 53:23 with 53:24 therefore sharpens the question: what do I want—guidance, or guidance that says what I already want it to say?

Verse reference: 53:23.

## 4. Desire Cannot Replace Knowledge: Conjecture, Verification, and Responsibility

### Verse

> **53:28** — They had no knowledge about this; they only conjectured. Conjecture is no substitute for the truth.

> **17:36** — You  shall  not  accept  any  information,  unless  you  verify  it  for  yourself. I  have  given  you  the  hearing,  the eyesight,  and  the  brain,  and  you  are responsible for using them.

### Reflection on the Verse

53:28 is direct: conjecture is no substitute for truth. 17:36 adds the responsibility of verifying information and connects that responsibility with hearing, eyesight, and the brain. When I place these verses inside the question of desire, I see the need to draw a boundary between what I want and what I know.

Wanting something to be true does not make it true. Information supporting my position is not automatically reliable. A religious claim feeling comforting does not turn it into revelation. Loving a person does not make every statement of that person correct; disliking someone does not make every statement false.

In my earlier reflection “Do Not Accept Without Verifying,” I considered how responsibility for knowledge removes the human being from passive acceptance. Here that responsibility meets desire. Verification is hardest precisely where I already want a certain conclusion. We naturally lower the threshold for information that confirms us and raise it for information that disturbs us.

So I ask myself: when evidence moves against my desire, does my seriousness in examining it decrease? If it increases, is that because I love truth, or because I refuse to lose?

Knowledge should not become the servant of desire. Its task is not to decorate what I already want; it must sometimes correct what I want. Verification can therefore be more than an intellectual method. It can be an ethical discipline: remaining fair to truth even when truth is inconvenient.

Verse references: 53:28, 17:36.

## 5. When Worldly Life Fills the Mind, the Horizon of Knowledge Can Narrow

### Verse

> **53:29** — You shall disregard those who turn away  from our message, and become preoccupied with this worldly life.

> **53:30** — This is the extent of their knowledge. Your Lord is fully aware of those who strayed away from His path, and He is fully aware of those who are guided.

### Reflection on the Verse

In 53:29, turning away from the message stands beside becoming preoccupied with worldly life. 53:30 then says, “This is the extent of their knowledge.”

I do not read this as contempt for worldly knowledge. A person can study science, build technology, trade, create art, and develop expertise. The verse does not authorize me to call such activity worthless. But when the world becomes the entire horizon, a person may know a great deal and still miss the direction of life itself.

Someone may master a profession and never ask what the profession serves. A person may know how to make money but never ask what money is for. He may know how to attract attention but never ask what visibility is enlarging. He may understand algorithms and remain unaware of how his own desires are directing him.

For me, the danger is not production in the world; it is allowing a worldly project to occupy the whole mind. A project can begin to feel like the final measure of my existence. Failure threatens my entire identity; success tempts me to exaggerate myself. 53:30 breaks that expansion: knowledge has a limit, and the Lord knows best who has strayed and who is guided.

So the question is not only “What do I want?” It is also “How much of my mind has this desire occupied?” The world is a field of responsibility. When it becomes the final horizon, knowledge may look wide while direction becomes narrow.

Verse references: 53:29-30.

## 6. Personal Wishes Must Not Bend Testimony: Justice and Truthful Speech

### Verse

> **4:135** — O  you who  believe,  you  shall  be absolutely  equitable,  and  observe GOD,  when  you  serve  as  witnesses, even  against  yourselves,  or  your parents,  or  your  relatives.  Whether the  accused  is  rich  or  poor,  GOD takes  care  of  both.  Therefore,  do not  be  biased  by  your  personal wishes.  If  you  deviate  or  disregard (this  commandment),  then  GOD  is fully  Cognizant  of  everything  you do.

> **61:2** — O you who believe, why do you say what you do not do?

> **61:3** — Most  abominable  in  the  sight  of GOD is that you say what you do not do.

### Reflection on the Verse

4:135 carries desire directly into the field of testimony: do not become biased by personal wishes. This shows me that desire is not merely an inner, private matter. It can distort judgment, the story I tell about someone, and the way I present facts.

It is easy to take my own side. I know the context of my successes and I have explanations for my failures. With another person, I may ignore context and see only the error. I may apply one standard to someone I love and another to someone I dislike. 4:135 interrupts that instinct and puts justice before personal preference even when testimony turns against myself or those close to me.

61:2-3 then places speech beside action. Truthfulness is not only the absence of a technically false sentence. If I constantly speak about high standards while living by another standard, my words and my life split apart.

Writing about the Quran therefore cannot be a comfortable position. If I tell other people to restrain desire while refusing to examine my own desires, the article reads me too. If I write about justice but abandon justice when criticized, information does not rescue me.

Desire must be disciplined in two places: when I judge others and when I narrate myself. Testimony is not a public-relations project for the self. Truth cannot be bent according to whether it favors me.

Verse references: 4:135, 61:2-3.

## 7. After Ownership Comes Accountability: Works, Not Self-Image, Receive Recompense

### Verse

> **53:31** — To GOD belongs everything in the heavens  and  everything  on  earth. He will requite those who commit evil for their works, and will reward the  righteous  for  their  righteousness.

> **53:32** — They avoid gross sins and transgressions,  except  for  minor  offenses. Your Lord’s forgiveness is immense. He has been fully aware of you since He initiated you from the earth, and while you were embryos in your mothers’ bellies. Therefore, do not exalt yourselves; He is fully aware of the righteous.

### Reflection on the Verse

53:31 returns to ownership: everything in the heavens and on earth belongs to GOD. Then it moves to recompense for works. 53:32 speaks of avoiding gross sins, the vastness of forgiveness, and the command not to exalt ourselves.

This movement takes me from desire to action. I may desire to see myself as righteous. I may want others to see me as truthful, clean, knowledgeable, or sincere. But 53:32 reminds me that the story I tell about myself cannot replace GOD’s knowledge of me.

That can also be liberating. I do not need to prove myself constantly. Being misunderstood may hurt, but final knowledge does not belong to the crowd. At the same time, praise is not a safe refuge. Other people exalting me does not change what I actually am.

Desire is not only the desire to possess something. It can be the desire to appear good. In religious work, that desire can hide behind noble language. “I am more informed, more sincere, more righteous” may never be spoken aloud and still become an inner identity.

53:31-32 lead me back to a simpler responsibility. In a reality owned by GOD, my task is not to declare my own worth. It is to pay attention to my works, avoid what is wrong, seek forgiveness, and refuse to manufacture spiritual superiority for myself.

Verse references: 53:31-32.

## 8. Could What I Want Harm Me?

### Verse

> **17:11** — The human being often prays for something that may hurt him, thinking that he is praying for something good. The human being is impatient.

### Reflection on the Verse

17:11 is one of the verses that most slows down my desire. A human being can pray for something harmful while thinking he is asking for something good, and the verse connects this with impatience.

In my earlier reflection “The Impatient Human Being: Is What We Want Really Good for Us?” I stayed with this verse for a long time. Returning to 53:24 now gives the question another angle. What do I desire, and can I actually see the consequences of what I desire?

A door that closes today may protect me from something I could not see. A delayed desire may reshape me in ways I understand only years later. An opportunity I desperately wanted could have brought responsibilities, dependencies, or losses I did not know how to imagine.

This does not mean a person should stop planning. I value careful planning, detailed work, and persistence. But between planning and outcome, I must leave room for the knowledge of GOD. Otherwise submission becomes nothing more than gratitude when my preferred result arrives.

Impatience can make desire feel like truth: “It must happen now. It must be this person. This opportunity must be mine. If this does not happen, everything is wrong.” The verse pulls me away from that certainty. Sometimes I do not know whether what I am asking for is truly good. Admitting that does not make desire meaningless. It places my knowledge in proportion.

Verse reference: 17:11.

## 9. What I Like Is Not Necessarily Good, and What I Dislike Is Not Necessarily Bad

### Verse

> **2:216** — Fighting may be imposed on you, even though you dislike it. But you may dislike something which is good for you, and you may like something which is bad for you. GOD knows while you do not know.

### Reflection on the Verse

2:216 speaks in a specific context about fighting. Without removing that context, I pay attention to the explicit contrast it states: a person may dislike something good for him and like something bad for him; GOD knows while we do not know.

This does not make emotion the enemy. Liking and disliking are part of being human. But feeling and truth are not the same thing. A decision feeling peaceful does not automatically make it right. A truth making me uncomfortable does not make it false.

Desire often produces a sense of certainty from within. When I want something intensely, my mind can begin collecting reasons for it. When I do not want an option, I can enlarge its risks. This does not always mean deliberate self-deception, but it does reveal a field where self-examination is necessary.

Submission here is not passivity. It is refusing to give my own feeling absolute authority. I can love something while admitting it may be bad for me. I can fear something while leaving open the possibility that there is good within it.

In “Do You Really Trust GOD? The Word of Trust, the Obsession with Control, and the Hard Test of Submission,” I reflected on the tension between control and trust. 2:216 reaches the center of that tension: I do not know. GOD knows. The whole human desire for control is tested between those two sentences.

Verse reference: 2:216.

## 10. When Desire Begins to Function as a God

### Verse

> **25:43** — Have you seen the one whose god is his own ego? Will you be his advocate?

> **45:23** — Have  you  noted  the  one  whose  god is  his  ego?  Consequently,  GOD sends  him  astray,  despite  his  knowledge,  seals  his  hearing  and  his  mind, and  places  a  veil  on  his  eyes.  Who then  can  guide  him,  after  such  a decision  by  GOD?  Would  you  not take heed?

### Reflection on the Verse

25:43 and 45:23 use severe language: the ego becomes a person’s god. A human being does not need to bow before a statue for something inward to take governing authority.

When does desire approach that boundary? One measure for me is this: when my desire conflicts with guidance, which one do I modify? If I continually bend text, evidence, justice, and responsibility toward the conclusion I want, desire is no longer merely an impulse. It has become a ruler.

The phrase “despite his knowledge” in 45:23 is especially disturbing. Knowledge alone does not protect a person. Someone may know a great deal and still follow the ego. That is a serious warning for anyone engaged in religious research. Knowing verse numbers, examining roots, comparing sources, or building technical research tools does not prove that the self has submitted.

In “Deep Reverence for GOD: Ego, Disrespect, and the Inner Reality of Crossing the Boundary,” I reflected on the way ego can place its own measure above the measure of GOD. The same problem appears here through desire.

So I ask myself: am I a human being who has desires, or has my desire begun to have me?

Verse references: 25:43, 45:23.

## 11. Can My Own Opinion Replace Guidance?

### Verse

> **28:50** — If they fail to respond to you, then know that they follow only their own opinions. Who is farther astray than those who follow their own opinions, without guidance from GOD? GOD does not guide such wicked people.

### Reflection on the Verse

28:50 places personal opinions opposite guidance from GOD. This makes me think that desire has not only emotional forms but intellectual ones as well.

When a person invests effort in a position, attachment grows. He researches, writes, defends, perhaps persuades others. Over time, an opinion can become part of identity. Then changing the opinion can feel like losing part of oneself.

This is one place where the problem of religious authority begins for me. A knowledgeable person can be useful, but that person’s opinion cannot replace guidance. The same applies to me. None of my reflections are verses. A conclusion I hold today must remain open to correction if stronger evidence appears tomorrow.

Truthfulness enters here as well. If I discover that something I previously said was wrong, loyalty to truth should outweigh loyalty to my archive. “I already said this publicly” cannot become a reason to defend error.

When my desire is to remain right, I can end up following my own opinion. When my desire is to find guidance, my opinion must be able to change. Outwardly both can look like serious research. Inwardly they move in different directions.

Verse reference: 28:50.

## 12. Obeying Another Person’s Desire: The Limit of Religious Authority

### Verse

> **18:28** — You  shall  force  yourself  to  be  with those  who  worship  their  Lord  day and  night,  seeking  Him  alone.  Do not  turn  your  eyes  away  from  them, seeking  the  vanities  of  this  world. Nor shall you obey one whose heart we  rendered  oblivious  to  our  message;  one  who  pursues  his  own desires,  and  whose  priorities  are confused.

> **9:31** — They  have  set  up  their  religious leaders  and  scholars  as  lords,*  instead of GOD. Others deified the Messiah, son of Mary. They were all commanded  to  worship  only  one god. There is no god except He. Be He glorified, high above having any partners.

### Reflection on the Verse

18:28 warns not only about my own desires but also against obeying someone who pursues his own desires and whose priorities are confused. 9:31 sets an even clearer boundary by describing religious leaders and scholars being set up as lords instead of GOD.

I do not derive from this that no one should be listened to. Human beings learn from teachers, benefit from specialists, use the work of researchers, and compare translations. I myself take Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation as the primary English text and work with the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation. But using a text or benefiting from a person is not the same as moving that person into the place of GOD.

In “Should a Quran App Issue Rulings? The Boundary Between Tool and Authority,” I reflected on the danger of turning a technological tool into a religious authority. The same boundary applies to human beings. A person may be highly knowledgeable and still remain human. Making someone beyond criticism, beyond examination, or functionally infallible damages the architecture of monotheism.

18:28 also speaks of confused priorities. That leads me to examine not only what a person says but what sits at the center of the person’s direction. Religious language does not guarantee the absence of desire. Status, visibility, belonging, money, or the need to remain right can all enter religious speech.

And the same standard must return to me. I should not want readers to treat my writings or research as revelation. Reflection should not attach people to me. It should send them back to the verse.

Verse references: 18:28, 9:31.

## 13. Are the Adornments of the World Evil, or Is Their Place the Real Question?

### Verse

> **3:14** — Adorned  for  the  people  are  the worldly  pleasures,  such  as  the  women,  having  children,  piles  upon  piles of  gold  and  silver,  trained  horses, livestock, and crops. These are the materials  of  this  world.  A  far  better abode is reserved at GOD.

> **3:15** — Say,  “Let  me  inform  you  of  a  much better  deal:  for  those  who  lead  a righteous  life,  reserved  at  their Lord,  are  gardens  with  flowing streams,  and  pure  spouses,  and  joy in GOD’s blessings.” GOD is Seer of His worshipers.

### Reflection on the Verse

3:14 names worldly pleasures; 3:15 points to a better abode. Read together, they give me a balanced picture of desire. Worldly materials exist and they are attractive, but they are not the final measure.

That matters because religious reflection can sometimes force an artificial choice: either reject the world completely or become absorbed in it. These verses leave a more measured space. The problem is not automatically the existence of possessions, family, beauty, work, or comfort. The question is where they are placed in the heart and in the hierarchy of life.

In “True Wealth According to the Quran: Material Wealth or Not Being Rich?” I reached a similar point. Money by itself is not a moral identity. Wealth does not automatically make a person corrupt, and poverty does not automatically make a person righteous. The deeper question concerns what I do with what I have and what position I give it.

I can love a home, hope my work succeeds, enjoy making art, love my family, and benefit from good things. But these remain the materials of this world; they must not become the final purpose of existence.

3:15 does not extinguish desire. It enlarges and redirects it toward something better. Perhaps the challenge is not simply to want less, but to want better things in a better order.

Verse references: 3:14-15.

## 14. The Hierarchy of Love and Desire: What Am I Most Afraid to Lose?

### Verse

> **9:24** — Proclaim:  “If  your  parents,  your children, your siblings, your spouses, your family, the money you have earned, a business you worry about, and the homes you cherish are more beloved to you than GOD and His messenger,** and the striving in His cause, then just wait until GOD brings His judgment.” GOD does not guide the wicked people.

### Reflection on the Verse

9:24 gathers some of the strongest human attachments in one place: parents, children, siblings, spouses, family, money, business, and homes. The verse does not say these things are evil. The question is heavier: do they become more beloved than GOD?

In “Loving GOD Alone: Loving Nothing but GOD, or Loving Nothing as if It Were GOD?” I reflected on the relationship between love and monotheism. Desire meets the same hierarchy here. A person may want many things, but nothing should be moved into the place of GOD.

Applied to my own life, the measure cannot remain verbal. Saying “I love GOD most” is easy. What happens when loss becomes possible? Would I conceal what I know to be true in order to protect a job? Would I postpone responsibility before GOD to avoid losing someone I love? Would I soften truth to preserve reputation?

What I fear losing most can sometimes reveal the center of gravity in my heart. That does not mean every fear is idolatry; the verse does not authorize such an easy accusation. But fear and desire can reveal my hierarchy of values.

The discipline of desire is therefore not lovelessness. It does not require loving family less. It requires refusing to place anything on the same level as GOD.

Verse reference: 9:24.

## 15. Whatever I Seek, I Begin to Build My Life Around It

### Verse

> **42:20** — Whoever  seeks  the  rewards  of  the Hereafter,  we  multiply  the  rewards for  him.  And  whoever  seeks  the materials  of  this  world,  we  give  him therefrom,  then  he  receives  no share in the Hereafter.

### Reflection on the Verse

42:20 places two directions before the human being: seeking the rewards of the Hereafter and seeking the materials of this world. The verse makes desire look not merely like an inner emotion, but like a search that organizes life.

If I truly seek something, I arrange time, attention, decisions, and labor around it. I may say that I want the Hereafter, but if my entire practical life revolves only around worldly outcomes, a distance opens between what I say and where I am actually going.

This does not mean abandoning worldly responsibility. Work, income, learning, and production remain part of life. But what larger direction holds them? Do I see income as a means to sustain life and make beneficial work possible, or as the source of my worth? Does visibility serve the message, or has visibility become its own reward?

42:20 gives desire an agricultural shape: what harvest am I seeking? Today’s choices prepare tomorrow’s crop. Desire is therefore not only a wish; it resembles investment. Where do I sow my time? Where do I sow my attention? What am I training my character to pursue?

Sometimes my calendar may reveal the direction of my desire more honestly than my prayers do.

Verse reference: 42:20.

## 16. Not Destroying Desire, but Restraining Sinful Lusts

### Verse

> **79:40** — As  for  the  one  who  reverenced  the majesty  of  his  Lord,  and  enjoined the self from sinful lusts.

> **79:41** — Paradise will be the abode.

### Reflection on the Verse

79:40-41 make an important distinction. The self is restrained from sinful lusts, and this restraint is connected with Paradise. The verses do not say that every desire must disappear. They speak about moral discipline when desire moves toward sin.

That distinction matters to me. To be human is to want. We want food when hungry, love and safety, learning, creativity, family, achievement, and peace. The promise that wishes are fulfilled in Paradise also prevents me from treating desire itself as a defect of existence.

The problem begins when desire moves across a moral boundary. At that point, freedom may not mean “doing whatever I feel.” Freedom can mean being able to say no when desire tries to rule me.

Deep reverence here is not paralysis by fear. It is an inward boundary: even when no one sees, even when applause disappears, even when an opportunity is very attractive, I can say, “I want this, but I do not want to cross the boundary of GOD for it.”

This is not the killing of desire. It is the education of desire. It does not erase the self; it teaches the self its place.

Verse references: 79:40-41.

## 17. Desire in Paradise: The Place Where Wishes Are Fulfilled

### Verse

> **41:31** — “We  are  your  allies  in  this  life,  and in  the  Hereafter.  You  will  have  in  it anything  you  wish  for;  you  will  have anything you want.

> **41:32** — “(Such  is  your)  ultimate  abode, from a Forgiver, Most Merciful.”

### Reflection on the Verse

41:31-32 complete the balance of this article. After reflecting on the limits, ignorance, impatience, and moral danger of desire in worldly life, the description of Paradise says that people will have anything they wish for and anything they want.

This reminds me again that desire itself is not the enemy. The problem may be absolute demand made in a temporary world with limited knowledge. Paradise is presented as an ultimate abode from a Forgiver, Most Merciful. Desire appears there inside a safe and gracious context.

In this world, one person’s desire can collide with another person’s rights. I can unknowingly ask for something harmful. A fulfilled wish may feed arrogance. Scarcity, rivalry, death, and loss shape the world. The promise of Paradise should therefore not be read as if desire there operates inside the same broken conditions.

So the answer to 53:24 cannot be reduced to “people do not get what they want.” The conditions of desire differ between this world and the Hereafter. Here desire exists inside a field of testing; there the verse speaks of a place where desire is fulfilled as a gift.

Perhaps submission is not the destruction of desire, but the willingness to place it in trustworthy hands now.

Verse references: 41:31-32.

## 18. “Anything They Wish” and “Even More”: Grace Beyond Desire

### Verse

> **50:35** — They get anything they wish therein, and we have even more.

### Reflection on the Verse

The second half of 50:35 especially affects me: “and we have even more.” People receive what they wish for, yet what GOD gives is not limited to what they were able to wish for.

This reveals another limit of desire. A human being can only desire what he knows, imagines, remembers, or feels the absence of. “Even more” opens a space beyond the horizon of human wanting.

In worldly life I can sometimes approach prayer as if presenting a requirements list: give me this, repair that, open this door. Prayer certainly includes asking. But 50:35 reminds me that the generosity of GOD is wider than my imagination.

This makes submission richer, not poorer. I do not have to accept goodness only when it appears in the form I designed. “My plan did not happen” does not necessarily mean “nothing good happened.” The boundary of my desire is not the boundary of GOD’s grace.

53:24 asks about human desire. 50:35 speaks of something beyond even fulfilled desire. Between them, my place becomes clear: I am a servant who asks, but I am not the one who knows every possible form of good.

Verse reference: 50:35.

## 19. The Final Measure: Not Only What I Wanted, but What I Did and Where I Am Going

### Verse

> **53:39** — Every human being is responsible for his own works.

> **53:40** — And  everyone’s  works  will  be shown.

> **53:41** — Then they will be paid fully for such works.

> **53:42** — To your Lord is the final destiny.

### Reflection on the Verse

For me, the movement from the question of desire in 53:24 to 53:39-42 within the same sura creates a powerful closing. Every human being is responsible for his own works. The works will be shown. Recompense will be full. The final destiny is to the Lord.

The difference between desire and responsibility becomes clear here. Wanting to be good is not enough; something has to be done for righteousness. Wanting to be just is not enough; justice must appear in testimony. Wanting to submit to GOD is not enough; what I do when desire conflicts with guidance reveals the direction.

The verses also change the way I look at failure. I can want an outcome intensely and still not receive it. That does not automatically make the effort meaningless. Responsibility is tied to works; total ownership of outcomes is not handed to me.

This is close to the way I try to work: plan, strive, and leave the result to GOD. But this sentence must never become an excuse for passivity. 53:39 reminds me of responsibility; 53:42 reminds me of direction.

Perhaps the deepest human desire is a desire for destination hidden behind many smaller desires: security, peace, permanence, meaning, acceptance. Yet the verse draws the final line with extraordinary simplicity: the final destiny is to your Lord.

So the question is no longer only, “What do I want?”

It becomes: “How is what I want preparing me for my final destination?”

Verse references: 53:39-42.

## A Reflection on Arabic Roots

The verb **تَمَنَّىٰ (tamannā)** in 53:24 is generally connected with the **م ن ي (m-n-y)** root family and carries a field of wishing, longing, or desiring. Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation uses “desires”; the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation renders it as “arzuladığı.” I do not treat the root as a dictionary ruling that can determine the whole verse by itself. The sentence, the surrounding verses, and the authorized wording must remain together. Still, the lexical field strongly conveys an inward orientation toward something not yet possessed.

In 53:23 and 79:40, **هَوَىٰ / ٱلْهَوَىٰ (hawā)** comes from the **ه و ي (h-w-y)** root family. Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation renders it according to context as “personal desire” and “sinful lusts.” The Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation gives “kişisel arzu” and “günahkar arzular.” This distinction matters: the verses do not put every form of wanting into one moral category. Personal desire that competes with guidance is not the same as the fulfilled wishes described in Paradise.

In 53:28, **ٱلظَّنَّ (al-zann)** belongs to the **ظ ن ن (z-n-n)** root family, while **ٱلْحَقِّ (al-haqq)** belongs to **ح ق ق (h-q-q)**. The verse itself supplies the controlling principle: conjecture is no substitute for truth. Root analysis may make the contrast more visible, but it cannot replace the verse.

In 53:39, **سَعَىٰ (sa‘ā)** belongs to the **س ع ي (s-ʿ-y)** root family and carries a field of striving or directed effort. Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation says, “Every human being is responsible for his own works.” This keeps the reflection safely focused on responsibility rather than using morphology to manufacture an additional doctrine.

In 53:42, **ٱلْمُنتَهَىٰ (al-muntahā)** is connected with the **ن ه ي (n-h-y)** root family and carries a field of end, limit, or final point. The Authorized English Translation renders the verse, “To your Lord is the final destiny.” That line measures the whole article: however far desire travels, the final destination does not belong to the self.

## Other Claims

Traditional commentaries have placed different emphases on 53:24. Some classical readings understand it especially within the immediate 53:19-26 context of idols and hoped-for intercession: a human being does not obtain the intercession or outcome he desires merely because he wants it. Readings associated with Ibn Kathir also express the point more generally as the denial that every person simply receives whatever he wishes. One example can be reviewed here: <a href="https://quran.com/53%3A24/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">53:24 tafsir page</a>.

Other approaches may read the verse more broadly as a statement about the human condition: desire does not create reality and wishing does not equal possession. The brevity of the question naturally allows that reflective direction. Yet none of these outside readings control the argument of this article. The main reflection above has been built only from the verses quoted in the article and the relationships among them.

I therefore prefer caution rather than assigning certainty among these claims. What is explicit in the immediate sequence is sufficient for the central reflection: 53:23 places personal desire against guidance; 53:24 asks what the human being desires; 53:25 declares that both this world and the Hereafter belong to GOD.

## Conclusion

I cannot answer “What is it that the human being desires?” with the name of a single object. Human desires change. They are different in childhood and adulthood, in poverty and wealth, in loneliness and public visibility. Even religious research contains desire: sometimes I want truth; sometimes I want to be proven right.

The verses together give me a deeper answer.

The human being desires, but this world and the Hereafter belong to GOD. Personal desire must not replace guidance. Conjecture does not replace truth. Knowledge brings responsibility to verify. Personal wishes must not bend testimony. What I like is not automatically good, and what I dislike is not automatically bad. The ego can grow until desire functions like a god. Religious leaders and scholars must not be moved into the place of GOD. The world can contain beauty without becoming the final abode. Sinful lusts can be restrained. In Paradise, desire is not the enemy; it belongs to a field of fulfilled wishes and even “more” beyond what was imagined.

Then 53:39-42 bring me back to responsibility. I am responsible for my own works. The works will be shown. Recompense will be full. The final destiny is to my Lord.

So when I read 53:24 now, I do not want to ask only, “What do I want?” I also want to ask:

Does what I want make me more just or more biased?  
More truthful or more defensive?  
More willing to verify, or more determined to protect conjecture?  
Does it move me toward GOD, or enlarge my ego?  
Am I placing a person, leader, scholar, or my own opinion into a place that belongs to GOD?  
Do I lose submission when the desire is not fulfilled?  
Do I exalt myself when it is fulfilled?

Perhaps the deepest discipline of desire is not receiving everything I want. It is teaching even my desire to submit to the guidance of GOD.

I work. I ask. I pray. I research. If I am wrong, I correct. I try to bear witness to what I know to be true. But I do not own the outcome.

Because this world and the Hereafter belong to GOD.

And to my Lord is the final destiny.
