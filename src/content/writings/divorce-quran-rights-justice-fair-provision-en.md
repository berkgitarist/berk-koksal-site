## Introduction

Greetings. Peace be upon you.

When I think about divorce, my first question is not who wins the argument. It is what I still owe a person with whom I can no longer continue a shared life. Does truthfulness end when a marriage ends? Can a pledge, a home, a child's needs, or another person's dignity suddenly become worthless because a decision has been made?

I begin with 2:226. The four-month interval opens into an extended series of questions about reconciliation, a final decision, women's rights, dowries, housing, compensation, nursing, consultation, and equitable witnesses. What moves me is not merely the level of detail. It is the refusal to let the vulnerability of separation become permission for injustice.

In <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/mizan-mixing-mastering-and-measure-in-the-quran/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Mizan: Mixing, Mastering, and Measure in the Quran</a>, I reflected on measure as something deeper than a number. Here measure touches a person's livelihood and future. And the question I explored in <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/responding_to_evil_with_what_is_best_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Responding to Evil with What Is Best: Where Does Justice End and Forgiveness Begin?</a> becomes painfully intimate: Can I still protect the rights of someone I no longer wish to live with?

I want to think about bearing witness to the oneness of GOD not as an abstract declaration but as a responsibility to act justly when a relationship breaks. I do not want to smooth over the difficult wording of 2:228, or replace the practical demands in 2:229–237 and 65:1–7 with pleasant generalities. Nor will I invent a universal percentage for dividing assets, a civil procedure, or a final answer for every life from words that do not actually specify one. Submission to GOD includes knowing when my knowledge ends.

## 1. Four Months and an Uncomfortable Question: Decision or Anger?

### Verse

> **2:226** — Those  who  intend  to  divorce  their wives  shall  wait  four  months  (cooling  off);  if  they  change  their  minds and  reconcile,  then  GOD  is  Forgiver, Merciful.

### Reflection on the Verse

When a marriage is shaken, I can mistake the intensity of my pain for the clarity of my decision. Verse 2:226 interrupts that confusion. The intention to divorce is followed by four months of waiting, a cooling-off period, while the possibility of changing one's mind and reconciling remains open. That interval asks something of the person who is ready to announce that everything is over: Have I really understood what I am choosing?

But I do not want to turn waiting into a sentimental command to reconcile at any cost. The verse leaves room for reconciliation; it does not promise that every marriage can or should be restored. An interval meant for reflection must not become an excuse to keep someone trapped in uncertainty or to evade responsibilities.

I keep returning to a personal question: If I am considering ending a shared life, can I still respect the person whose life has been tied to mine? The reference to GOD's forgiveness and mercy makes me examine my own hardness, without pretending I have the right to decide the future on another person's behalf.

Verse connections: 2:226.

## 2. Going Through With Divorce: No Word Goes Unheard

### Verse

> **2:227** — If  they  go  through  with  the  divorce, then GOD is Hearer, Knower.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 2:227 completes the choice left open in 2:226. Reconciliation is possible. Divorce may also go through. In either case, GOD is described as Hearer and Knower. The decision is not reduced to a public statement or a document. What I say and what I intend are not separate worlds before Him.

There is a peculiar kind of cruelty in leaving a relationship permanently unfinished: never clearly choosing to stay, yet never honestly releasing the other person. I cannot claim that this verse offers an entire legal procedure for every such situation. It does, however, make me confront the weight of speech and knowledge when a decision is made.

For me the question is no longer simply, 'May I leave?' It is, 'What kind of truthfulness accompanies my leaving?' If I do not intend to reconcile, I should not manufacture hope as a means of control. If I decide to separate, I do not gain the right to destroy the other's dignity. These are moral questions I bring before 2:227, not extra commandments I insert into its text.

Verse connections: 2:227.

## 3. Waiting, Pregnancy, Rights, and the Danger of Easy Answers

### Verse

> **2:228** — The  divorced  women  shall  wait three menstruations (before marrying another man). It is not lawful for them to conceal what GOD creates in their wombs, if they believe in GOD and the Last Day. (In case of pregnancy), the husband’s wishes shall supersede the wife’s wishes, if he wants to remarry her. The women have rights, as well as obligations, equitably. Thus, the man’s wishes prevail (in case of pregnancy). GOD is Almighty, Most Wise.

### Reflection on the Verse

I cannot read 2:228 as if it were an effortless slogan. It speaks of three menstruations, of not concealing what GOD creates in the womb, of a particular provision in a case of pregnancy concerning the husband's wish to remarry, and of women's rights alongside their obligations. To select the portions I find comfortable and pretend the rest is absent would not be faithful reflection.

I find the explicit acknowledgment of women's rights and obligations important. I also see the pregnancy-related provision, which gives precedence to the husband's wishes in the stated circumstance. I should neither erase that expression nor expand it into an unlimited rule of male superiority in matters the verse does not address. Resisting both shortcuts is part of the discipline of reading.

The warning against concealing what is in the womb brings truthfulness into an intimate situation where information can affect more than one life. It does not give me permission to invent disclosure rules for every private matter. It does ask me to take seriously the connection between rights, responsibilities, and the truth on which decisions depend.

Verse connections: 2:228.

## 4. Limits on Returning and the Trust of What Was Given

### Verse

> **2:229** — Divorce  may  be  retracted  twice. The divorced woman shall be allowed to live in the same home amicably, or leave it amicably. It is not lawful for the husband to take back anything he had given her. However, the couple may fear that they may transgress GOD’s law. If there is fear that they may transgress GOD’s law, they commit no error if the wife willingly gives back whatever she chooses. These are GOD’s laws; do not transgress them. Those who transgress GOD’s laws are the unjust.

> **4:20** — If you wish to marry another wife, in place of your present wife, and you had given any of them a great deal, you shall not take back anything you had  given  her.  Would  you  take  it fraudulently,  maliciously,  and  sinfully?

> **4:21** — How could you take it back, after you have been intimate with each other, and they had taken from  you a solemn pledge? 

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 2:229 sets a limit on retracting divorce and describes amicable ways of living together or parting. It also forbids the husband to reclaim what he has given, while describing a distinct situation in which the wife willingly returns what she chooses. I cannot glide past that word 'willingly.' A concession extracted through fear cannot become righteous simply because someone calls it voluntary.

Verses 4:20–21 sharpen the same question. Even when much has been given, taking it back is challenged; the relationship of intimacy and the solemn pledge are remembered. Money can make a person forget how much a promise once meant. I hear these verses asking me not to turn a shared history into an inventory to be seized when affection is gone.

This does not amount to a formula assigning a fixed share of all marital assets to every person in every case. The text here is more specific. The rights attached to what was given, the seriousness of the pledge, and the conditions of voluntary return matter. The limits set by GOD cannot become bargaining chips for the stronger party.

Verse connections: 2:229, 4:20, 4:21.

## 5. The Third Divorce: Taking a Decision Seriously

### Verse

> **2:230** — If he divorces her (for the third time), it is unlawful for him to remarry her, unless  she  marries  another  man, then he divorces her. The first husband can then remarry her, so long as they observe GOD’s laws. These are GOD’s laws; He explains them for people who know.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 2:230 brings me to a difficult boundary: After the third divorce, remarriage to the first husband is made conditional on a subsequent marriage and its ending. I read that boundary as a challenge to the casual confidence that a decision can always be undone without consequences.

I do not want to build hasty conclusions around that limit. The verse states a condition for returning to the earlier marriage; it does not provide an exhaustive account of every human intention or circumstance. I must acknowledge the explicit boundary without adding instructions that its words do not contain.

Sometimes limits are precisely what prevent a person's power from becoming careless. If I assume I can end and restore another person's life together with mine whenever I wish, what does the word commitment still mean? I do not know every reason behind the rule. I do know that the verse refuses to treat the third decision as weightless.

Verse connections: 2:230.

## 6. Keeping Someone Out of Revenge Is an Injustice

### Verse

> **2:231** — If you divorce the women, once they fulfill their interim (three menstruations), you shall allow them to live in the same home amicably, or let them leave amicably. Do not force them to stay against their will, as a revenge.  Anyone  who  does  this wrongs  his  own  soul.  Do  not  take GOD’s revelations in vain. Remember GOD’s blessings upon you, and that He sent down to you the scripture and wisdom to enlighten you. You shall observe GOD, and know that GOD is aware of all things.

### Reflection on the Verse

Keeping someone in the home can look, from a distance, like protecting a marriage. Verse 2:231 asks what the action is really for. It speaks directly against making a woman stay against her will as revenge. When that is the purpose, the language of family preservation can become a disguise for control.

That is what unsettles me most. I can call an act 'patience' while using it to punish. I can say I am waiting for reconciliation while making daily life unbearable. The verse will not let me hide behind the respectable name I give the act. It offers amicable continuation or amicable departure, not revenge.

The warning against taking GOD's revelations in vain matters here. I do not want to borrow sacred language to make another person's suffering seem compulsory. Submission to GOD must be visible, at the very least, in my willingness to take the other's will and dignity seriously even when the relationship has failed.

Verse connections: 2:231.

## 7. Do Not Block Those Who Reconcile

### Verse

> **2:232** — If you divorce the women, once they fulfill their interim, do not prevent them  from  remarrying  their  husbands, if they reconcile amicably. This  shall  be  heeded  by  those among you who believe in GOD and the Last Day. This is purer for you, and more righteous. GOD knows, while you do not know.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 2:232 is not addressed only to the two people whose marriage ended. It also confronts those who might stand in their way. If the waiting period is complete and the parties reconcile amicably, their remarriage must not be obstructed. That is a profound restriction on the pride of bystanders.

Families and friends may believe they are defending someone while actually defending their own resentment. I do not have to approve every decision two adults make. But I have to question whether my disappointment gives me the right to obstruct a reconciliation that belongs to them. The reminder that GOD knows while we do not know is especially uncomfortable when I am convinced my judgment is perfect.

The verse does not command everyone to reunite. It addresses the obstacle placed before those who have reached an amicable reconciliation. Forcing people to return and forbidding them to return can be two versions of the same mistake: taking possession of a decision that is not mine.

Verse connections: 2:232.

## 8. A Child Cannot Disappear Inside the Parents’ Conflict

### Verse

> **2:233** — Divorced mothers shall nurse their infants two full years, if the father so wishes. The father shall provide the mother’s food and clothing equitably. No one shall be burdened beyond his ability. No mother shall beharmed on account of her infant, nor shall the father be harmed because of his infant. (If the father dies), his inheritor shall assume these responsibilities. If the infant’s parents mutually agree to part, after due consultation, they commit no error by doing so. You commit no error by hiring nursing mothers, so long as you pay them equitably. You shall observe GOD, and know that GOD is Seer of everything you do.

### Reflection on the Verse

When divorce is discussed, the adults' injuries can fill the room. The child's needs may scarcely be heard. Verse 2:233 places those needs in the center. Nursing, food and clothing, the father's provision, each person's ability, consultation, payment, and the prohibition against harm to either parent appear together.

A child cannot be treated as evidence that one parent has won. The verse protects the mother from being harmed on account of the infant, and the father as well. That does not erase the different responsibilities the verse names. It means the responsibilities must be carried without turning the child into an instrument of injury.

I am struck by the explicit mention of mutual consultation in decisions about weaning. The ability to speak responsibly to one another is not a luxury once children are involved. When the verse reminds me that GOD sees everything I do, I cannot imagine justice being complete if the smallest member of the family is left outside it.

Verse connections: 2:233.

## 9. Separation Before Consummation and Equitable Compensation

### Verse

> **2:236** — You commit no error by divorcing the women before touching them, or before setting the dowry for them. In this case, you shall compensate them—the rich as he can afford and the poor as he can afford—an  equitable compensation. This is a duty upon the righteous.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 2:236 speaks of equitable compensation in a separation before touching or before a dowry has been set. The wealthy are addressed according to what they can afford, and the poor according to what they can afford. Even when married life has not unfolded in its familiar form, the separation is not treated as something devoid of moral consequence.

How often does a dispute begin with the question, 'What is the least I must give?' I would rather begin where the verse directs my attention: what constitutes equitable compensation in the circumstances? It provides neither one universal amount nor a license to disregard the duty because means differ.

The verse identifies this as a duty upon the righteous. That detail matters to me. The possibility of separating without error is not the same thing as having no obligation to act well. A bond may fail to continue; my responsibility to treat the other person with care does not disappear at that instant.

Verse connections: 2:236.

## 10. When the Dowry Is Set: Entitlement, Waiver, and Free Choice

### Verse

> **2:237** — If you divorce them before touching them,  but after  you had set the dowry for them, the compensation shall be half the dowry, unless they voluntarily forfeit their rights, or the party  responsible  for  causing  the divorce chooses to forfeit the dowry. To forfeit is closer to righteousness. You  shall  maintain  the  amicable relations among you. GOD is Seer of everything you do.

### Reflection on the Verse

The setting of 2:237 is specific: A dowry was fixed, yet the marriage ended before sexual intimacy. Half the dowry is prescribed, with room for voluntary forfeiture in the circumstances described. First the entitlement is identified. Only then does the possibility of relinquishing it come into view.

A person who may freely give up a right must not be turned into someone expected to give it up under pressure. 'You would be better if you waived it' can become a particularly cruel sentence when the person hearing it is frightened, alone, or financially vulnerable. I want to protect the freedom that gives a generous act its moral worth.

The verse also calls for the maintenance of amicable relations. That does not mean denying the financial account. It means refusing to humiliate someone for claiming a right and refusing to exploit someone who chooses generosity. Perhaps the hardest kind of kindness is the one that remains honest about what is owed.

Verse connections: 2:237.

## 11. Provision for a Divorcee and What Cannot Be Taken Back

### Verse

> **2:241** — The  divorcees  also  shall  be  provided for, equitably. This is a duty upon the righteous.

> **4:19** — O you who believe, it is not lawful for you to inherit what the women leave behind, against their will. You shall not force them to give up anything you had given them, unless they commit a proven adultery. You shall treat them nicely. If you dislike them, you may dislike something wherein GOD has placed a lot of good.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 2:241 is brief, but it will not allow me to treat a divorced woman's needs as an afterthought. Equitable provision is called a duty upon the righteous. Verse 4:19 adds the refusal to force a woman to give up what was given to her, along with an instruction to treat her nicely. Together, these verses keep the human being in view after the relationship changes.

I need to be precise here. They do not give me a universal formula by which every home, vehicle, debt, or shared saving must automatically be divided into fixed percentages. The stated principles concerning provision, compensation, property given, and protection against coercion are not the same thing as a complete asset-division code.

That distinction does not weaken the obligation. It prevents me from claiming authority the text does not give me. Being the stronger negotiator or holding a particular document does not itself answer the moral question. If another person has a right, I cannot call delay, pressure, or a manufactured 'voluntary' waiver a victory.

Verse connections: 2:241, 4:19.

## 12. Not Every Waiting Period Is the Same

### Verse

> **33:49** — O  you  who  believe,  if  you  married believing  women,  then  divorced them  before  having  intercourse  with them,  they  do  not  owe  you  any  waiting  interim  (before  marrying  another man).  You  shall  compensate  them equitably, and let them go amicably.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 33:49 stops me from applying one waiting period to every divorce. It describes a particular situation: A believing woman is divorced before sexual intercourse, and she does not owe the former husband a waiting interim before another marriage. Equitable compensation and amicable departure still remain in the same verse.

When I read related verses separately, I can too easily take a rule from one situation and impose it on another. That may restrict someone's future without warrant, or cause me to overlook a right that still exists. The details of the circumstances are not footnotes to justice. They are part of what makes a judgment fair.

I do not turn this specific exception into a universal rule. But I do hear a clear challenge to the desire to keep someone's life on hold by default. Before speaking confidently about what another person must wait for, I need to understand which circumstances the verse actually addresses.

Verse connections: 33:49.

## 13. A Home Must Not Become an Instrument of Pressure

### Verse

> **65:1** — O  you  prophet,  when  you  people divorce  the  women,  you  shall  ensure that  a  divorce  interim  is  fulfilled. You  shall  measure  such  an  interim precisely.*  You  shall  reverence GOD  your  Lord.  Do  not  evict  them from  their  homes,  nor  shall  you make  life  miserable  for  them,  to force  them  to  leave  on  their  own, unless  they  commit  a  proven  adultery.  These  are  GOD’s  laws.  Anyone who  transgresses  GOD’s  laws  commits  an  injustice  against  himself. You  never  know;  maybe  GOD  wills something good to come out of this.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 65:1 speaks of precisely measuring the waiting interval and, in the same breath, of housing. The women are not to be evicted or made so miserable that they leave on their own, with the specific exception of proven adultery stated in the verse. The house cannot simply become a means of exerting power.

It is possible to force someone out without formally ordering them to leave. Threats, humiliation, and a daily life made unlivable can accomplish the same thing. That is why the verse troubles me. 'I did not evict her' is no complete defense if my conduct was meant to drive her away.

The verse is not a detailed ruling on the legal ownership of the home or an automatic transfer of title. I cannot extract such an arrangement from it. What I can see is a serious demand to respect the waiting period and not weaponize the place where someone lives. And I cannot know what GOD may bring about afterward; my present anger is not the whole future.

Verse connections: 65:1.

## 14. Two Equitable Witnesses: Telling the Truth About Separation

### Verse

> **65:2** — Once  the  interim  is  fulfilled,  you may  reconcile  with  them  equitably, or  go  through  with  the  separation equitably.  You  shall  have  two  equitable  witnesses  witness  the  divorce before  GOD.  This  is  to  enlighten those  who  believe  in  GOD  and  the Last  Day.  Anyone  who  reverences GOD, He will create an exit for him.

### Reflection on the Verse

In 65:2, reconciliation and separation alike are to be equitable. The instruction is then given for two equitable witnesses to witness the divorce before GOD. Those details belong together. Witnessing an event without any concern for truth can deteriorate into ceremony; the verse expressly asks for witnesses who are equitable.

I think of how difficult that becomes when one side is a friend or a relative. My instinct is to protect the people I love. Yet the role of a witness cannot be reduced to telling a more convincing version of their story. Standing before GOD gives this duty a seriousness that convenience cannot remove.

I would not claim that 65:2 alone describes every civil registration process or every document that might be required in a particular country. Those are separate questions. Here I want to retain what the verse actually commands: a just path through the decision, and testimony that is not corrupted by partiality.

Verse connections: 65:2.

## 15. Reading the Waiting Period in Its Actual Circumstances

### Verse

> **65:4** — As for the women who have reached menopause,  if  you  have  any  doubts, their  interim  shall  be  three  months. As for those who do not menstruate, and  discover  that  they  are  pregnant, their  interim  ends  upon  giving  birth. Anyone  who  reverences  GOD,  He makes everything easy for him.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 65:4 distinguishes concrete situations. It speaks of a three-month interim for women who have reached menopause when there is doubt, and of women who do not menstruate and discover that they are pregnant, whose interim ends with childbirth. I cannot compress both statements into one universal rule and remain faithful to their wording.

Nor can I take this verse as a license to announce conclusions about every medical condition or circumstance it does not describe. The three menstruations in 2:228 and the absence of an interim in the specific situation of 33:49 already remind me that the text is attentive to differences. I must not erase them because a simpler sentence would be easier to repeat.

For the person whose life is affected, the calculation is not an abstract exercise. An unjustified extension or an unwarranted shortening can shape the next chapter of a life. Reverence for GOD ought to make me more careful with such judgments, not more confident than the text allows.

Verse connections: 65:4.

## 16. Housing, Pregnancy, and Payment for Nursing

### Verse

> **65:6** — You  shall  allow  them  to  live  in  the same  home  in  which  they  lived  with you,  and  do  not  make  life  so  miserable  for  them  that  they  leave  on their  own.  If  they  are  pregnant,  you shall  spend  on  them  until  they  give birth.  If  they  nurse  the  infant,  you shall  pay  them  for  this  service.  You shall  maintain  the  amicable  relations  among  you.  If  you  disagree, you  may  hire  another  woman  to nurse the child.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 65:6 puts immediate, practical questions before me. Where does the woman live? Who provides for her during pregnancy? How is nursing work compensated? Housing, protection against pressure, support until childbirth, payment for nursing, and maintaining amicable relations are all named.

A child's arrival does not dissolve the adults' responsibilities. Much of care can become invisible labor. By explicitly requiring payment for nursing, the verse challenges any easy assumption that such work needs no recognition because the worker is a mother. At the same time, it permits another woman to nurse the child if agreement cannot be reached. The passage leaves room for practical resolution rather than compulsory conflict.

I cannot claim that it provides an exhaustive schedule for every expense or every future caregiving arrangement. It does, however, keep me from looking away from housing, provision, payment, and the quality of the relationship after separation. Fairness means facing what remains to be done after the decision has been made.

Verse connections: 65:6.

## 17. Let Actual Means Guide Financial Responsibility

### Verse

> **65:7** — The  rich  husband  shall  provide  support  in  accordance  with  his  means, and  the  poor  shall  provide  according  to  the  means  that  GOD  bestowed  upon  him.  GOD  does  not impose  on  any  soul  more  than  He has  given  it.  GOD  will  provide  ease after difficulty.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 65:7 calls me to consider not a magic number but a measure. The husband with ample means provides according to those means; the one with limited means according to what GOD has given him. Need and ability must be considered together. Crushing a person under an impossible burden is no ideal of justice, but hiding one's actual ability in order to evade responsibility hardly fits the moral direction of the verse.

The passage does not claim that every person who values financial privacy is dishonest. I do not need such an overstatement. My personal question is simpler: When provision must be discussed, can I be truthful about what I can afford and attentive to what the other person needs?

The promise of ease after difficulty should not be turned into a guarantee that every financial complication will disappear tomorrow. There can be hardship even while someone is doing what is right. I want to meet that hardship without denying what GOD has granted me and without pretending to possess resources I do not have.

Verse connections: 65:7.

## 18. Arbitrators, Reconciliation, and Sometimes the Need to Part

### Verse

> **4:35** — If a couple fears separation, you shall appoint an arbitrator from his family and an arbitrator from her family; if they decide to reconcile, GOD will help them get together. GOD is Omniscient, Cognizant. 

> **4:128** — If  a  woman  senses  oppression  or desertion  from  her  husband,  the couple  shall  try  to  reconcile  their differences,  for  conciliation  is  best for  them.  Selfishness  is  a  human trait,  and  if  you  do  good  and  lead  a righteous  life,  GOD  is  fully  Cognizant of everything you do.

> **4:130** — If  the  couple  must  decide  to  part, GOD  will  provide  for  each  of  them from  His  bounties.  GOD  is  Bounteous, Most Wise.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 4:35 calls for an arbitrator from each family when a couple fears separation. One from his side, one from hers. That balance matters. It suggests an attempt to hear a troubled relationship without surrendering the whole account to one person's voice.

Verse 4:128 explicitly considers a woman who senses oppression or desertion from her husband. Reconciliation is presented as good, while human selfishness is acknowledged. I cannot turn that into 'remain silent and endure everything.' Trying to reconcile must also bring my own selfishness into view. It must not become a respectable name for keeping another person's injury in place.

Then 4:130 opens a space I am grateful for: If the couple must part, GOD will provide for each from His bounty. The possibility of reconciliation deserves serious care, but separation is not presented as the end of all hope. I cannot make the continuation of every marriage a certificate of virtue, nor its ending a spiritual triumph. I can ask whether those involved have acted truthfully and justly.

Verse connections: 4:35, 4:128, 4:130.

## 19. Testimony and the Oneness of GOD: Justice Even Against Our Own

### Verse

> **3:18** — GOD  bears  witness  that  there  is  no god except He, and so do the angels and  those  who  possess  knowledge. Truthfully  and  equitably,  He  is  the absolute  god;  there  is  no  god  but He, the Almighty, Most Wise.

> **4:135** — O  you who  believe,  you  shall  be absolutely  equitable,  and  observe GOD,  when  you  serve  as  witnesses, even  against  yourselves,  or  your parents,  or  your  relatives.  Whether the  accused  is  rich  or  poor,  GOD takes  care  of  both.  Therefore,  do not  be  biased  by  your  personal wishes.  If  you  deviate  or  disregard (this  commandment),  then  GOD  is fully  Cognizant  of  everything  you do.

> **5:8** — O  you  who  believe,  you  shall  be  absolutely  equitable,  and  observe GOD,  when  you  serve  as  witnesses. Do  not  be  provoked  by  your  conflicts  with  some  people  into  committing  injustice.  You  shall  be  absolutely  equitable,  for  it  is  more  righteous. You  shall  observe  GOD.  GOD  is fully  Cognizant  of  everything  you do.

### Reflection on the Verse

Anger after a marriage ends is not surprising. Still, 5:8 warns against allowing conflict to provoke injustice, and 4:135 requires equitable testimony even against ourselves and those closest to us. I find that the most difficult courtroom may be the one inside my own mind. Defending the rights of someone I resent, or admitting the wrongdoing of someone I love, can cost me something.

In 3:18, witness to the oneness of GOD stands alongside knowledge and equity. I do not see that as a distant theological subject unrelated to divorce. If I say I submit to GOD but knowingly diminish another person's rights, there is a fracture between my testimony and my conduct. I cannot heal it by repeating the words more loudly.

The questions I explored in <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/responding_to_evil_with_what_is_best_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Responding to Evil with What Is Best: Where Does Justice End and Forgiveness Begin?</a> and <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/the-weight-of-testimony/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The Weight of Testimony</a> return here in a painfully practical form. Do I defend justice only while it benefits me? Can I carry the weight of testimony when a relationship is already lost? These verses do not give me a technical resolution to every property or custody dispute. They do give me an uncompromising direction: Do not bury the truth, do not use power to crush the vulnerable, and remember before whom I stand.

Verse connections: 3:18, 4:135, 5:8.

## On the Arabic Roots of the Key Words

The Arabic vocabulary gives this reflection another layer. The root **ط ل ق (ṭ-l-q)** appears in forms relating to divorce, including *aṭ-ṭalāq* in 2:229 and *ṭallaqtum* in 65:1. But the word for divorce does not stand alone. Forms associated with **ر ب ص (r-b-ṣ)** express waiting in 2:226 and 2:228; 65:1 also contains *ʿiddah*, associated with **ع د د (ʿ-d-d)**, and *aḥṣū*, from **ح ص ي (ḥ-ṣ-y)**, concerning careful enumeration. The decision and the interval around it belong to the same moral field.

Forms of *maʿrūf*, related to **ع ر ف (ʿ-r-f)**, recur in 2:229, 2:231, and 65:2. Its associations with what is recognized as fitting or proper matter, but the word alone is not a complete legal tariff for every dispute. In 65:2, *ʿadl* from **ع د ل (ʿ-d-l)**, and in 4:135, *qisṭ* from **ق س ط (q-s-ṭ)** bring the language of equity into sharp focus. The root **ش ه د (sh-h-d)**, connected to testimony in 65:2 and 4:135, places truthfulness beside justice rather than after it.

The Arabic of 2:233 and 65:6 uses forms associated with **ض ر ر (ḍ-r-r)**, concerning harm. I find that especially striking: The verses that speak of rights also refuse to overlook injury. The *mīthāq* of 4:21, from **و ث ق (w-th-q)**, evokes the seriousness of the pledge. And *faḍl* in 2:237, related to **ف ض ل (f-ḍ-l)**, makes room to consider goodness beyond a strictly calculated entitlement.

A word root cannot replace a verse's actual sentence. I do not use etymology to manufacture new rules or cancel explicit ones. Yet the pattern is worth hearing: divorce and waiting; rights and equity; witnesses and truthful accountability; material duties and the refusal to harm. The language itself keeps pulling my attention back to the person who might otherwise disappear behind the dispute.

## Other Claims

Traditional and school-based discussions of divorce differ on questions such as how three divorces are counted, how waiting periods are applied, how dowry and maintenance are determined, the dissolution of marriage, and the role of family arbitration. Some approaches give greater weight to formal procedures and inherited authorities, while others emphasize reconciliation within families. It would be inaccurate to pretend that all such approaches say the same thing.

Modern rules for dividing marital property can also be confused with the Quranic passages that explicitly speak of dowry, compensation, provision, and support. Different legal systems may treat shared assets, debts, and parenting arrangements differently; I do not attribute those arrangements directly to the verses. Historical readings of 2:230 likewise sometimes connect it to particular marriage practices, but an established practice does not become morally justified merely because it is customary.

The distinction matters to me. The age of an interpretation does not turn it into the verbatim text of a verse. I can listen to religious leaders and scholars without giving their words the authority that belongs to GOD. Whatever interpretation is being offered, I still have to face the clear responsibilities concerning restraint, equitable separation, and truthful testimony in 2:229, 65:2, and 4:135.

## Conclusion

I began at 2:226 with four months in which a decision could be reconsidered. Looking back across these verses, I no longer see the question as merely one of time. Even near the end of a marriage, I still have choices: tell the truth or conceal it; honor a right or delay it; protect a home or use it as leverage; notice the child's needs or make my anger the only voice in the room.

I do not find a universal percentage that settles the division of every marital asset. But I do not find moral emptiness in its place. Verses 2:229 and 4:20–21 resist the arbitrary recovery of what was given. Verses 2:236–237 address compensation and dowry in stated circumstances. Verse 2:241 attends to the divorcee's needs; 2:233 and 65:6–7 address provision and care; 65:1–2 address housing and equitable testimony. Can I dismiss these responsibilities simply because a relationship has ended and still call that submission to GOD?

When I read the witness to GOD's oneness in 3:18 alongside the demand for justice in 4:135 and 5:8, my own testimony acquires a heavier meaning. Monotheism cannot remain a declaration on my lips while my conduct moves in the opposite direction. I should protect rights as far as I am able, avoid pretending to possess means I lack, and resist issuing final judgments where the words do not give me that authority. A marriage can end. My responsibility to justice must not end with it.

One question remains with me. When I think of the person I have left behind, will I be able to say that, even though our love did not continue, I tried to protect what was rightfully theirs? The submission I seek begins with answering that question truthfully.
