# [47:19] What Does La Ilaha Illa Allah Mean? Nineteen Reflections on Oneness, Testimony, Knowledge, and Submission

*To bear witness to oneness is also to examine my allegiances, my limits, and the way I live.*

**Author:** Berk KÖKSAL  
**Date:** November 12, 2026  
**Place:** Mersin

## Introduction

**Peace. Peace be upon you.**

A sentence can become so familiar that I stop hearing it. I may repeat it in a song, see it written on a wall, or hear it on other people's lips. Then one morning a simple question returns: what am I actually saying? When I pronounce “La Ilaha Illa Allah,” what am I denying, and what am I affirming? More difficult still: how should a person live after making such a declaration?

For me, this is not only a question of language. As someone who makes music and studies words and their connections across the Quran, I encounter the same declaration in several settings. A phrase that can be repeated through a melody appears in one verse with knowing, in another with testimony, elsewhere with arrogance, and in other passages with exclusive worship and justice. I do not want to flatten those connections into a single dictionary line.

Verse 47:19 joins the call to know with seeking forgiveness and the reminder that GOD knows our decisions. Verse 3:18 brings divine oneness together with knowledge and justice. Verse 37:35 describes arrogance in response to the declaration itself. Starting here, the question becomes larger than “How many gods are there?” It reaches the authority I accept, the help I seek, the truth I protect, and the finite things I may quietly elevate.

There is a danger even in defending a statement I believe to be true: I can begin to treat my own explanation as if it were unquestionable. Quoting a verse and explaining an inference from it are not the same action. Both may appear in an article, but their difference must remain visible. Speaking with certainty about what I have not established does not serve the truth.

I want to consider the declaration through language, knowledge, testimony, worship, human authority, ethics, relationships, work, remembrance, and art. Historical schools and interpretive traditions will be considered separately rather than made the foundation of the reflection. I am not searching for an easy license to judge others. I am searching for a way to say “La Ilaha Illa Allah” with less habit, more understanding, and greater responsibility.

## 1. More Than a Phrase: A Truth I Am Asked to Know

### Verse

> **47:19**
>
> <span lang="ar" dir="rtl">فَٱعْلَمْ أَنَّهُۥ لَآ إِلَـٰهَ إِلَّا ٱللَّهُ وَٱسْتَغْفِرْ لِذَنۢبِكَ وَلِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَٱلْمُؤْمِنَـٰتِ ۗ وَٱللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ مُتَقَلَّبَكُمْ وَمَثْوَىٰكُمْ</span>
>
> *You shall know that: "There is no other god beside GOD," and ask forgiveness of your sins and the sins of all believing men and women. GOD is fully aware of your decisions and your ultimate destiny.*
>
> Şunu bil ki: “TANRI’nın yanında başka tanrı yoktur”* ve hem kendi günahların için hem de tüm inanan erkeklerin ve kadınların günahları için bağışlanma dile. TANRI, kararlarınızın ve nihai varışınızın tamamen farkındadır.

Source: <a href="https://kuranteyit.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">kuranteyit.com</a>

### Reflection on the Verse

The first thing I notice in 47:19 is that the command does not begin with sound. It begins with knowing. The verse then calls for forgiveness and reminds me that GOD knows my decisions and ultimate destiny. Knowledge, repentance, and accountability sit together in one sentence. I cannot responsibly reduce “La Ilaha Illa Allah” to something I merely pronounce.

As a musician I can play a note correctly and still misunderstand its place in the composition. With this declaration I face an analogous risk: my pronunciation may be confident, my repetition sustained, and my voice moving. But what is happening in my choices? I may still need forgiveness even when my performance sounds assured. The second part of the verse prevents me from treating the first as a badge of personal perfection.

To know that no other god exists beside GOD does not mean that my understanding is divine. He fully knows decisions whose motives I sometimes fail to recognize even in myself. It is easy to explain a choice in flattering language. It is harder to recognize its consequences for another person. The verse names both the direction of worship and the limits of my self-knowledge.

So my question changes. After uttering these words, what will I examine again? Which mistake am I willing to admit? Where will I allow the awareness of GOD's knowledge to interrupt my certainty? Verse 47:19 does not give me a certificate of superiority. It opens a demanding life of learning, asking forgiveness, and taking responsibility.

**Verse references:** 47:19.

## 2. Can Testimony Be Separated from Knowledge?

### Verse

> **3:18** — TANRI şahitlik eder ki O’ndan başka tanrı yoktur, melekler ve bilgi sahibi olanlar da böyle şahitlik ederler. Doğru ve adil bir şekilde O, mutlak tanrıdır; O’ndan başka tanrı yoktur, Kudretlidir, En Bilgedir.

> **3:17** — Onlar kararlı, doğru sözlü, teslimolan, hayırsever ve şafak vaktinde tefekkür edenlerdir.

### Reflection on the Verse

In 3:18, testimony to GOD's oneness is associated with angels and those who possess knowledge. It is also joined to justice. This is not merely a ceremonial sentence. The preceding verse describes people who are steadfast, truthful, submitting, charitable, and reflective at dawn. Reading the two together, I find it difficult to separate an affirmation of faith from the moral texture of a life.

If I testified about an ordinary event, I would understand the seriousness of claiming to have seen what I had not seen. Why should I abandon that restraint when speaking about GOD? I do not read the reference to people of knowledge as permission to award myself a title. I read it as a challenge to distinguish what I know from what I assume. The distance between the One who knows everything and the person trying to learn remains immense.

The presence of justice in the verse matters. What would it mean to defend monotheism while knowingly misrepresenting a person, hiding evidence, or protecting my own circle through a double standard? The verse does not provide a complete checklist of every human situation. But it refuses to isolate testimony from truthfulness and equity.

I therefore resist the shortcut, “I have said it, so I have arrived.” A better question is whether my conduct is becoming more answerable to the truth I profess. Verses 3:17–18 place the test of testimony not only before an audience but also within the person's own conscience.

**Verse references:** 3:18; 3:17.

## 3. Why Might a Declaration of Oneness Meet Arrogance?

### Verse

> **37:35** — Onlara, “La ilahe illa Allah” [TANRI’nın yanında başka tanrı yoktur] denildiğinde, kibirlendiler.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 37:35 is brief and unsettling. The expression “La ilahe illa Allah” appears explicitly, and the response described is arrogance. It would be tempting to use that line to label other people. Yet that would allow the very danger under examination to grow inside my own judgment.

The difficulty of the declaration may not be limited to accepting that a Creator exists. It may require me to reconsider the place I have granted my own opinions, position, cherished plans, or favored people. The verse does not name these modern examples; they are questions I bring to myself in light of its warning about pride. Where have I treated something finite as if it had final authority?

I do not always resist truth because I possess better evidence. Sometimes I resist because admitting a truth would require changing a habit, abandoning an impressive claim, or losing approval. I can appear calm while inwardly arranging arguments to protect my standing. A very short verse can therefore provoke a lengthy examination of the self.

I cannot diagnose the final condition of another person's heart. But I can observe my own response when a verse challenges me. Do I investigate the challenge, or immediately protect my previous position? That answer may reveal more about my relationship to the declaration than the force or volume with which I pronounce it.

**Verse references:** 37:35.

## 4. Who Is the One? Name, Uniqueness, and Mercy

### Verse

> **2:163** — Sizin tanrınız tek tanrıdır; O’ndan başka tanrı yoktur, En Lütufkâr, En Merhametli.

> **20:8** — TANRI: O’ndan başka tanrı yoktur. En güzel isimler O’na aittir.

> **112:1** — İlan et, “O, Bir ve tek TANRI’dır.

> **112:2** — “Mutlak TANRI.

> **112:3** — “O, asla bir evlada baba olmadı. Asla bir babaya evlat da olmadı.

> **112:4** — “O’na denk hiçbir şey yoktur.”

### Reflection on the Verse

What do I mean when I say “one”? Verse 2:163 describes the one god while immediately speaking of graciousness and mercy. Verse 20:8 says that the most beautiful names belong to Him. Chapter 112 adds absolute uniqueness, the rejection of divine parentage, and the absence of anything comparable. I hear something more demanding than the number one: a uniqueness that resists ordinary comparison.

Writing GOD's name into a sentence does not mean I have fitted Him inside my categories. Human beings resemble one another, share responsibilities, and belong to family lineages. If I transfer these familiar structures to GOD without examination, I have missed the force of chapter 112. Yet 2:163 also prevents me from imagining absolute oneness as a cold abstraction severed from mercy.

In daily life, one usually means a count: one recording, one decision, one object. The statement in 112:4 interrupts that habit. GOD's oneness is not simply like an object being the only object on a table. Human understanding often begins with analogies, but these verses identify a point where analogy must stop.

When I say “La Ilaha Illa Allah,” I therefore hear not only a denial of competing gods but also a correction of my insufficient comparisons. Restraint in describing GOD is not a refusal to think. It is an effort to allow the terms supplied by the verses to govern what I claim.

**Verse references:** 2:163; 20:8; 112:1; 112:2; 112:3; 112:4.

## 5. The Problem of Competing Absolute Powers

### Verse

> **21:22** — Eğer onların içinde (göklerde ve yeryüzünde) TANRI’nın yanında başka tanrılar olsaydı, kaos olurdu. TANRI’ya yücelik olsun; mutlak otorite sahibi Rab. O, onların iddialarının çok üstündedir.

> **23:91** — TANRI hiçbir zaman bir oğula baba olmadı. O’nun yanında asla başka bir tanrı da yoktu. Aksi takdirde, her bir tanrı kendi yarattıklarıyla bağımsızlık ilan ederdi ve hakimiyet için birbirleri ile çekişirlerdi. TANRI yüceltilsin; onların iddialarının çok üstünde.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verses 21:22 and 23:91 examine the supposition of multiple gods through disorder, rival creation, and competing dominion. These are not philosophical proofs I have invented and imposed on the Quran. They are the counterfactual arguments present in the verses, and I want to preserve their actual shape.

I am familiar with conflicts of authority in ordinary work. When two people each claim an unrestricted final say, even a promising project can collapse into confusion. Yet an organizational dispute is not evidence about the physics of the universe. It may sharpen a question, but it cannot replace the scriptural reasoning: can absolute authority meaningfully coexist with another competing absolute authority?

Verse 23:91 connects creation with sovereignty. This suggests that oneness in these passages concerns more than the direction of prayer; it also concerns ultimate dominion. That is an immense claim. I will not pretend that these verses alone give me a detailed scientific theory of the cosmos or a complete account of every physical law.

They do turn the question back on me. While professing GOD's absolute authority, am I attributing unlimited power or unquestionable command to something finite? An argument that begins with the heavens and the earth ends by challenging the hierarchy I maintain within my own life.

**Verse references:** 21:22; 23:91.

## 6. The Messengers’ Shared Call: Message Before Person

### Verse

> **21:25** — Senden önce hiçbir elçi göndermedik ki, şu vahiyle göndermiş olmayalım: “Benden başka tanrı yok; yalnızca Bana tapın.”

> **16:36** — Biz her topluluğa, “TANRI’ya tapın ve putperestlikten uzak durun” diyen bir elçi gönderdik. Devamında, bazılarına TANRI tarafından rehberlik edildi, oysa diğerleri sapmaya bırakıldı. Yeryüzünü dolaşın ve reddedenlerin akıbetlerine dikkat edin.

> **20:14** — “Ben TANRI’yım; Benim yanımda başka tanrı yoktur. Yalnızca Bana tap ve Beni hatırlamak için İletişim Dualarını (Namazı) gözet.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 21:25 describes the essential message given to earlier messengers as worship of GOD alone. Verse 16:36 presents the call to each community in terms of worshiping GOD and avoiding idolatry. In 20:14, the address to Moses joins exclusive worship to remembrance. Across distinct scenes, the center remains remarkably consistent.

I want to distinguish recognizing a messenger from making that messenger the object of worship. A messenger matters because a message has been entrusted and delivered. But the message described in 21:25 is not an invitation to redirect devotion toward its bearer. I will consider later, under “Other Claims,” how different communities interpret the boundaries of religious declarations. Here I am concerned with what these verses themselves place at the center.

Verse 20:14 also turns a statement of belief into an instruction about conduct: contact prayer is linked to remembrance. Exclusive devotion is not left as an abstraction. It has a remembered direction and a practice. As someone capable of forgetting what matters in the middle of ordinary work, I find that connection personally demanding.

I will not construct detailed histories of every messenger from passages that do not supply them. Their shared call is already clear enough to challenge me: no other god; worship directed to GOD. If I keep that message distinct from arguments about the status of human figures, perhaps I can hear it more clearly.

**Verse references:** 21:25; 16:36; 20:14.

## 7. Where Does the Declaration Stand in My Life?

### Verse

> **1:5** — Yalnızca Sana taparız. Yalnızca Senden yardım isteriz.

> **6:162** — De ki: “İletişim Dualarım (Namazım), tapınma uygulamalarım, hayatım ve ölümüm; hepsi mutlak şekilde yalnızca kâinatın Rabbi TANRI’ya adanmıştır.

> **6:163** — “O’nun ortağı yoktur. İnanmakla emrolunduğum şey budur ve teslim olması gereken ilk benim.”

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 1:5 directs worship and requests for help to GOD alone. Verses 6:162–163 place contact prayers, worship practices, life, and death together in the same declaration of devotion. Reading them, I find it difficult to confine monotheism to a phrase repeated during one small portion of the day and forgotten for the rest.

People work, care for families, make things, rest, and face difficulties. I do not claim these verses automatically classify every ordinary act as worship. But if an entire life is described as devoted to GOD, it seems equally careless to remove everyday choices completely from the question. Keeping a promise, giving fair credit, or correcting a known error may disclose something about the direction of my intentions.

When I finish a recording, I can evaluate technical results: balance, clarity, completion. Another result is harder to measure. Did the process inflate my ego, or did I remain faithful to the purpose for which I began? Verse 6:162 is not a specific ruling about music production. It is a statement broad enough to force me to consider how my creative work fits into a life of devotion.

To say “La Ilaha Illa Allah” need not mean withdrawing from every human relationship. For me it means struggling not to give any relationship the position that belongs to GOD. This is a reflection on the verses, not a detailed program I can impose on everyone. Yet I cannot shake the sense that the declaration asks to be remembered beyond the moment it is spoken.

**Verse references:** 1:5; 6:162; 6:163.

## 8. Seeking Nearness Through Intermediaries: Where Is My Worship Directed?

### Verse

> **39:2** — Biz sana bu kutsal yazıyı doğru bir şekilde indirdik; dinini yalnızca O’na adayarak TANRI’ya tap.

> **39:3** — Kesinlikle, din yalnızca TANRI’ya adanmalıdır. O’nun yanında putlar edinenler, “Biz sadece bizi TANRI’ya yaklaştırsınlar diye onları putlaştırıyoruz; zira onlar daha iyi bir konumdalar!” derler. TANRI ihtilaflarına dair onları yargılayacaktır. TANRI böyle yalancılara, inkârcılara rehberlik etmez.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 39:2 directs devotion to GOD alone. The next verse describes people who adopt idols on the explanation that those figures will bring them closer to GOD. What strikes me is that their stated justification is not necessarily a denial that GOD exists. They claim to seek nearness. Yet a reassuring explanation does not automatically resolve the question of whom one worships.

Receiving information from another person, seeking practical assistance, or working together is not identical to making that person an object of worship. Verse 39:3 describes the latter. If I erase the distinction, I may begin condemning all forms of human cooperation. The passage does not warrant that leap. Its sharper challenge is whether I have granted any finite being the place reserved for GOD.

I know how easily the wish for reassurance can hide beneath elevated words. Under pressure, it may feel easier to trust someone's imagined infallibility or guaranteed ability to save me than to confront my own uncertainty before GOD. These are questions I ask of my habits, not examples listed verbatim by the verse. The desire for nearness may sound beautiful, but the direction of worship matters.

Thus the declaration becomes more than a negation. It expresses direct devotion. I may respect a teacher or value a person's help without confusing that relationship with worship. I read the serious warning of 39:2–3 to examine the order of my own commitments, not to issue effortless judgments against other people's hearts.

**Verse references:** 39:2; 39:3.

## 9. Learning from People Without Making Them Lords

### Verse

> **9:31** — Onlar TANRI’nın yerine kendi dinî liderlerini ve âlimlerini efendiler* edinmişlerdir. Başkaları da, Mesih Meryem’in oğlunu tanrılaştırdı. Onların hepsine, tek bir tanrıya tapmaları emredildi. O’ndan başka tanrı yoktur. O yüceltilsin, herhangi bir ortağa sahip olmanın çok üstünde.

> **42:21** — Onlar, TANRI tarafından asla yetkilendirilmemiş dinî yasaları onlar için hükme bağlayan putları takip ederler. Eğer önceden belirlenmiş karar olmasaydı, onlar derhâl yargılanmış olurlardı. Gerçekten de, haddi aşanlar acı veren bir azabı üzerlerine çekmişlerdir.*

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 9:31 warns against taking religious leaders and scholars as lords, while 42:21 questions religious laws ascribed to authorities not authorized by GOD. I could immediately turn these warnings outward. But the more difficult task is to examine the moments when I accept a person's pronouncement as binding without asking how it has been established.

I can value an insightful speaker and learn from a careful scholar. Rejecting all learning from people would make little sense; I depend on others in many fields. The issue is whether a human explanation has been given the standing of revelation. A title does not confer infallibility. Equally, my suspicion of institutional authority does not miraculously make my own interpretation perfect.

In my earlier reflection <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/who-are-those-in-charge-among-you-4-59_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Who Are Those in Charge Among You?</a>, I explored related tensions. Following another person does not transfer my conscience and responsibility to them. Questioning another person's teaching does not transfer unlimited authority to me. I still owe the claim a fair hearing, evidence, and an honest assessment of uncertainty.

I do not want to use the severe language of 9:31 as an easy weapon against individuals. Disagreeing with a teacher is not the same as knowing that person's final standing before GOD. “La Ilaha Illa Allah” teaches me not contempt for people but proportion: human voices are human voices. Mine is one of them.

**Verse references:** 9:31; 42:21.

## 10. What Changes When GOD Alone Is Mentioned?

### Verse

> **39:45** — TANRI TEK BAŞINA anıldığı zaman Ahirete inanmayanların kalpleri tiksintiyle daralır. Ancak O’nun yanında başkaları da anıldığı zaman, onlar tatmin olurlar.*

> **12:40** — “Siz, O’nun yanında uydurduğunuz bidatlerden başkasına tapmıyorsunuz, siz de ebeveynleriniz de. TANRI bu gibi putlara asla yetki vermemiştir. Bütün hüküm TANRI’ya aittir ve O, O’ndan başkasına tapmamanızı emretmiştir. Mükemmel olan din budur, ancak insanların çoğu bilmez.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 39:45 contrasts mentioning GOD alone with mentioning others beside Him. Verse 12:40 speaks about invented names, unauthorized idols, and judgment belonging to GOD. Names in these passages are not harmless decoration. The name I invoke may reveal the authority on which I think a claim rests.

I know the temptation to make my position sound stronger by placing a celebrated name before it. Do I cite someone because their evidence is sound, or because I hope their reputation will end the discussion? These are contemporary questions I bring to the text, not situations explicitly itemized in 39:45. Yet the emphasis on GOD alone makes me ask why I might feel a need to supplement truth with borrowed prestige.

There is still an essential distinction between mentioning a person and making that person a partner in worship. Verse 12:40 does not prohibit ordinary acknowledgment of every human name. Crediting an artist, engineer, or researcher is not identical to granting them ultimate religious authority. The verse concerns fabricated objects of devotion and the source of judgment.

I raised a related question in <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/how-can-tawhid-be-distorted-by-one-word-one-god-or-highest-of-the-high/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">How Can Tawhid Be Distorted by One Word?</a>. Now it returns through the names I use. Making GOD the center does not require erasing human beings. It requires refusing to install another sacred center beside Him.

**Verse references:** 39:45; 12:40.

## 11. Taking Idolatry Seriously Without Weaponizing the Warning

### Verse

> **31:13** — Hani Lokman oğluna, onu aydınlatırken demişti ki: “Ey oğlum, TANRI’nın yanında hiçbir put edinme; putperestlik büyük bir haksızlıktır.”*

> **4:48** — TANRI putperestliği bağışlamaz,* fakat O irade ettiği kimse için daha küçük suçları bağışlar. TANRI’nın yanında put edinen kimse korkunç bir suç uydurmuş olur.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 31:13 calls idolatry a great injustice. Verse 4:48 contains an especially severe warning concerning it. These are not presented as trivial differences in vocabulary. Their seriousness deserves attention. Yet the gravity of the warning does not grant me exhaustive knowledge of the inner lives of other people.

I can examine a public action, present evidence, and explain why I believe a statement conflicts with a verse. I cannot thereby claim to know every relevant intention, level of understanding, opportunity, or final outcome. My concern is not to soften 4:48 but to resist turning a judgment belonging to GOD into an authorization for my own condemnation of particular souls.

I am also struck that 31:13 portrays a father addressing his son. That setting makes me consider the relationship between care and warning. Can I take a danger seriously without humiliating a loved one? Can affection become an excuse for withholding a difficult truth? The verse does not give a complete handbook for such conversations, but the act of instructing a son asks me to consider how I speak to those nearest to me.

I must not excuse myself too quickly either. Loving something, depending on someone's practical help, and treating that being as a god are not automatically the same act. These verses do not give me a complete catalog of every boundary case. They do tell me the matter is grave enough to examine my attachments without self-flattery.

**Verse references:** 31:13; 4:48.

## 12. Knowing Versus Repeating Until I Feel Certain

### Verse

> **17:36** — Kendin için teyit etmediğin sürece, hiçbir bilgiyi kabul etme. Ben sana işitmeyi, görmeyi ve beyni verdim ve sen onları kullanmaktan sorumlusun.

> **39:18** — Onlar tüm sözleri inceleyen, ardından en güzeline uyanlardır. Bunlar TANRI’nın rehberlik ettiği kimselerdir; bunlar akıl sahibi olanlardır.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 17:36 makes me answerable for hearing, seeing, and thinking. Verse 39:18 praises people who examine words and follow what is best. For a declaration I want at the center of my life, both commands matter. Studying what I repeat does not dishonor it. It may be one way of treating it with seriousness.

A claim appearing in hundreds of online places has not necessarily been confirmed by hundreds of independent witnesses. It may all trace back to one unverified account. I considered that risk in <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/a_thousand_artificial_witnesses_same_lie_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">If a Thousand Artificial Witnesses Tell the Same Lie</a>. When I read a verse, a related version of the same discipline applies: which words belong to the verse, which to a commentary, and which to my own inference?

I do not turn 39:18 into a claim that every opinion is equally true. Examination matters because accounts differ in evidence and clarity. It can be particularly uncomfortable to submit my favorite explanation to critical questions. Saying a phrase frequently does not prove that I have exhausted its meaning. A different passage may require me to return to a word I thought I understood.

I also do not want to confuse humility with endless paralysis. The fact that I cannot resolve every question does not mean nothing is knowable. Verse 17:36 challenges me not to pretend to know what I have not established. Verse 39:18 challenges me to continue examining. Together they give repeated words a responsibility deeper than habit.

**Verse references:** 17:36; 39:18.

## 13. Testimony and Justice: Those I Love and Those I Dislike

### Verse

> **4:135** — Ey inananlar, şahitlik ettiğiniz zaman kendinizin veya ebeveynlerinizin ya da akrabalarınızın aleyhine bile olsa mutlak suretle adil olun ve TANRI’yı gözetin. Sanık ister zengin olsun ister fakir olsun, TANRI her ikisini de gözetmektedir. Bu nedenle, şahsi arzularınızın etkisiyle yanlı davranmayın. Eğer saparsanız veya (bu buyruğu) göz ardı ederseniz, TANRI yaptığınız her şeyden tamamen Haberdardır.

> **5:8** — Ey inananlar, şahitlik ettiğiniz zaman tümüyle adil olun ve TANRI’yı gözetin. Bazı insanlarla olan çatışmalarınız sizi adaletsizlik yapmaya kışkırtmasın. Tümüyle adil olun, çünkü bu daha doğrudur. TANRI’yı gözetin. TANRI yaptığınız her şeyden tamamen Haberdardır.

### Reflection on the Verse

Read together, 4:135 and 5:8 test justice from two directions: affection and hostility. I am not to bend testimony for the benefit of relatives, my group, or my own interests. Anger toward another person does not authorize distortion either. If I profess GOD's oneness, these obligations cannot become irrelevant whenever a dispute is personal.

It is often easy to identify an error made by an opponent. But when inaccurate information benefits my own side, do I correct it with equal care? I may want to protect my family; that desire does not justify concealing another person's right. I may feel deeply wronged; that feeling does not make a baseless allegation true. The challenge of the verses becomes especially visible when emotions are strong.

Verse 3:18 had already joined testimony and justice. Verses 4:135 and 5:8 make the connection concrete. They do not supply a complete procedure for every legal dispute, but they clearly identify pressures that can corrupt fairness. I encounter those pressures in work decisions, family disagreements, and the claims I share publicly.

If I later discover that I have treated someone unjustly, my profession of faith should not become a shield against correction. The hardest moment of truthful testimony may not be when I prove myself right, but when I admit I was wrong. That is one place where the cost of the declaration becomes real.

**Verse references:** 4:135; 5:8.

## 14. Truthful Speech: Not Mixing Truth with Falsehood

### Verse

> **2:42** — Bilerek, hakikate yalan karıştırmayın ve hakikati gizlemeyin.

> **33:70** — Ey inananlar, TANRI’ya derin saygı duyun ve sadece doğru söz söyleyin.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 2:42 warns against mixing falsehood with truth or concealing truth. Verse 33:70 joins truthful speech to reverence for GOD. I think about these verses while reviewing my writings, conversations, and small explanations. Beginning a statement with “for GOD” does not make everything that follows reliable.

Falsehood does not always arrive as a wholly invented story. I can mislead by selecting accurate fragments while hiding the fact that would change the picture. If I quote half a verse and quietly make it serve my preferred argument, the fact that some words were copied accurately does not free me from responsibility. Exact quotation and honest handling of context need to remain together.

In creative work, I might conceal the source of a voice, overstate my role, or fail to credit someone's contribution. Verse 2:42 is not a technical manual about recording or digital attribution. But the warning against concealing truth gives me a reason to examine the accuracy of my public account of a project. Who did what, and how am I representing it?

My declaration of monotheism claims allegiance to truth. Other words I say can nevertheless contradict that allegiance. I want my ordinary speech to become more careful, more transparent, and sometimes less certain. Reverence is not made more convincing by overstating what I know.

**Verse references:** 2:42; 33:70.

## 15. Can Faith Be Produced by Force?

### Verse

> **2:256** — Dinde hiçbir zorlama yoktur: doğru yol artık yanlış yoldan ayrıdır. Şeytanı kınayan ve TANRI’ya inanan herkes en güçlü, asla kopmayan bağa tutunmuştur. TANRI İşitendir, Her Şeyi Bilendir.

> **6:108** — Cehaletten TANRI’ya saygısızlık edip sövmesinler diye TANRI’nın yanında edindikleri putlara sövmeyin.  Biz her grubun işlerini kendi gözlerinde süsledik. En nihayetinde onlar Rablerine geri dönerler, sonra da O, yapmış oldukları her şeyi onlara haber verir.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 2:256 rejects compulsion in religion. Verse 6:108 instructs believers not to insult the idols worshiped beside GOD and gives a reason for that restraint. Together they make me consider the ethics of communicating a conviction. Believing a position to be false does not give me unlimited freedom in how I treat the person who holds it.

If someone refuses to listen, does speaking louder help them think? Does humiliating a person improve my understanding of their question? The concern expressed in 6:108 includes the possibility that insult will provoke further disrespect. I cannot turn that into a guarantee that everyone will eventually agree. The verse draws a boundary around conduct; it does not give me control over another person's inner response.

I discussed the ethical boundary in <a href="https://www.berkkoksal.com/writings/no_compulsion_in_religion_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">No Compulsion in Religion</a>: conviction and coercion are not the same thing. Here I ask a different question: can I maintain a firm belief without confusing respect with agreement? Respecting a person need not mean endorsing their claim, just as refusing insult need not mean concealing my conviction.

The difficulty is to speak about what I regard as an essential truth without forgetting the humanity of the listener. The person receiving the words remains responsible for their own response. I cannot acquire their conscience by force. Verse 2:256 therefore challenges not only how I view another person's freedom but also how I understand my own power.

**Verse references:** 2:256; 6:108.

## 16. Sincerity Where Intention Cannot Be Seen

### Verse

> **39:11** — De ki: “Ben, dini mutlak şekilde yalnızca O’na adayarak TANRI’ya tapmakla emrolundum.

> **40:14** — Bu nedenle, tapınmanızı mutlak şekilde yalnızca TANRI’ya adayın, inkârcılar bundan hazzetmeseler bile.

> **73:8** — Rabbinin adını, O’na daha da ve daha da yaklaşmak için an.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 39:11 describes worship directed exclusively to GOD. Verse 40:14 maintains that direction even when others dislike it. Verse 73:8 connects remembrance with drawing nearer. These passages make me think about the distance between a public declaration and an intention no audience can inspect completely.

I can say that I created a work to remember GOD alone. I also enjoy appreciation, feel criticism, and hope that a project succeeds. I cannot classify every ordinary emotion as a religious fault or virtue on the basis of these verses; they do not offer a comprehensive psychological inventory. But I can ask whether the audience's approval has quietly replaced the purpose for which I began.

Verse 40:14 also makes me consider disagreement. Other people may not share my purpose. Staying faithful to a conviction is not identical to refusing to hear criticism. Sometimes a critic identifies a real mistake I should correct. Sometimes they simply prefer a different approach. Saying “I do this for GOD” must not become a way of dismissing every possibility that I have done it poorly.

I do not want to measure nearness in 73:8 as if it were a streaming statistic. Frequency, audience size, and musical form cannot by themselves prove sincerity. I hope to examine my direction most carefully when recognition is absent or uncertain. I want the decision I offer before GOD to matter more than the performance through which it becomes audible.

**Verse references:** 39:11; 40:14; 73:8.

## 17. Remembrance, Silence, and Art: What Sound Cannot Carry

### Verse

> **13:28** — TANRI’yı hatırlamaktan kalpleri sevinç duyan onlardır. Kesinlikle, kalpler TANRI’yı hatırlamaktan sevinç duyar.

> **29:45** — Kutsal yazıdan sana vahyedileni oku ve İletişim Dualarını (Namazı) gözet; zira İletişim Duaları kötülüğü ve ahlâksızlığı önler. Ancak (Namaz yoluyla) TANRI’nın hatırlanması en önemli amaçtır.* TANRI yaptığınız her şeyi bilir.

> **17:44** — Yedi evren, yeryüzü ve onların içindeki herkes O’nu yüceltir. Hiçbir şey yoktur ki O’nu yüceltmiyor olsun, ama siz onların yüceltmelerini anlamazsınız. O Hoşgörülüdür, Bağışlayıcıdır.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 13:28 associates remembering GOD with joy in the heart. Verse 29:45 describes contact prayer in relation to the prevention of wrongdoing and the importance of remembrance. Verse 17:44 says that the heavens, the earth, and everything within them glorify GOD, while we do not understand their glorification. Together these passages keep remembrance from being reduced to sounds made by human beings.

I work with sound. I can place a repeated phrase inside different rhythms, guitar textures, choirs, or silences. Yet the fact that I find a composition moving does not prove that every listener undergoes the same spiritual change. Verse 13:28 is not a list of musical techniques. Verse 29:45 does not specify the arrangement of a concert or recording. I should not make either verse into an artistic rulebook.

In <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/silent-remembrance-does-remembrance-continue-when-the-sound-ends/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Silent Remembrance</a> and <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/you_do_not_understand_their_glorification_en/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">You Do Not Understand Their Glorification</a>, I had already struggled with this boundary. Sound ends, yet the question of remembrance remains. Verse 17:44 places an even greater limit on me: I do not understand every form of glorification. My artistic output cannot be the measure of all creation's praise.

Repeating “La Ilaha Illa Allah” through nineteen different compositions may express a personal musical decision and devotional intention. It cannot by itself establish a scripturally required musical number, guarantee divine acceptance, or prove what every listener remembers. Art may open a door. I should not claim to own the experience of those who walk through it.

**Verse references:** 13:28; 29:45; 17:44.

## 18. Does the Declaration Reach Work, Trade, and Justice?

### Verse

> **16:90** — TANRI adaleti, bağış yapmayı ve akrabaların haklarına riayet etmeyi savunur. Ve O, kötülüğü, ahlâksızlığı ve haddi aşmayı yasaklar. O sizi aydınlatıyor ki dikkate alasınız.

> **6:152** — Onlar olgunluğa erişene kadar, en doğru yöntemin dışında, yetimlerin parasına dokunmayın. Ticaret yaptığınızda, adil bir şekilde tam ağırlığında ve tam ölçüsünde verin. Biz hiçbir nefse imkânlarının ötesinde yük yüklemeyiz. Şahitlik ettiğinizde, akrabalarınıza karşı bile olsa kesinlikle adil olun. TANRI ile olan antlaşmanızı yerine getirin. Bunlar O’nun size buyruklarıdır ki dikkate alasınız.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 16:90 places justice, giving, and obligations to relatives within one moral frame. Verse 6:152 moves from the protection of an orphan's property to fair measure, testimony, and a covenant with GOD. These verses matter to my study of “La Ilaha Illa Allah” because I do not want the declaration to fall silent precisely where another person's rights are at stake.

When I deliver a product, manage an order, or agree on payment for work, an abstract moral concern becomes concrete. A concealed shortage, a false date, or an uncredited contribution has consequences. I do not claim that 6:152 supplies a complete technical legal code for modern contracts. But its insistence on fair measure challenges conduct, not merely private belief.

Verse 16:90 also makes me consider the difference between kindness and fairness. A generous gesture does not automatically erase a right I have withheld. Caring deeply for my relatives cannot serve as a blank check for injustice toward others. These are practical reflections prompted by the verse; they are not a claim that two passages resolve every dispute without further facts or expertise.

Here the declaration becomes uncomfortable in a necessary way. If I affirm that GOD alone is my god, how do I respond when money, approval, or professional success pulls me toward an unfair choice? Directing worship correctly cannot excuse neglecting another person's right. The words acquire weight when they approach decisions that cost something.

**Verse references:** 16:90; 6:152.

## 19. To Whom Do I Finally Submit?

### Verse

> **3:19** — TANRI’nın onayladığı tek din “Teslimiyet”tir. İronik bir şekilde, almış oldukları bilgiye rağmen kıskançlık yüzünden bu gerçeğe itiraz edenler kutsal yazıyı almış olanlardır. Böylesi TANRI’nın vahiylerini reddedenler için TANRI hesap görmede en sıkı olandır.

> **3:20** — Eğer seninle tartışırlarsa, o zaman de ki: “Ben kendimi yalnızca TANRI’ya teslim ettim; ben de bana uyanlar da.” Kutsal yazıyı almış olanlara da, almamış olanlara da ilan et: “Teslim olacak mısınız?” Eğer teslim olurlarsa onlar rehberlik edilmişlerdir, ancak eğer yüz çevirirlerse, senin tek vazifen bu mesajı iletmektir. TANRI tüm insanları Görendir.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 3:19 calls the religion approved by GOD “Submission.” Verse 3:20 speaks of submitting oneself to GOD alone in the face of dispute and distinguishes delivering a message from controlling another person's response. The command to know in the opening verse thus returns to me as a question of choice. How will I live before the truth I say I recognize?

Submission cannot be merely a word for comfortable days. What happens when I must correct my error, withdraw an argument I enjoyed winning, or stop defending a loved one's conduct because it harmed someone else? The distinction in 3:20 between communicating a message and forcing an outcome matters. I can explain what I believe and present my reasons. I cannot claim to manufacture another person's faith through my own power.

I began with “La Ilaha Illa Allah” as a sentence, then followed its implications through knowledge, testimony, justice, authority, intention, remembrance, and rights. At each stage I have tried to keep the verse distinct from my reflection upon it. I end with a need to speak no more confidently than my evidence permits. Monotheism does not make me infallible. It may make the pretense of infallibility harder to defend.

When I say these words today, I cannot guarantee that I will make no mistake tomorrow. I can, however, choose where to return when a mistake becomes clear. I can resist making people, success, or my own ego unquestionable. Perhaps the deepest change this declaration should bring into my life is not an authority to rule others, but a willingness to learn what it means to be a servant.

**Verse references:** 3:19; 3:20.

## Arabic Word Roots: Lā, Ilāh, Illā, Knowledge, and Testimony

Examining word roots does not mean replacing revelation with a dictionary. But the Arabic structure matters if I want to understand why a short declaration carries such force. In 47:19 the key expression is **لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا ٱللَّهُ**; the essential wording also appears in 37:35. The linguistic observations here do not alter the supplied translation or create new divine instructions.

**Lā (لَا)** is a particle of negation. In this construction its force is broad, not merely the denial of one particular named deity. **Illā (إِلَّا)** introduces an exception. The expression therefore combines a sweeping negation with an explicit exception. Neither particle should be assigned a fabricated three-letter lexical root simply because many Arabic nouns and verbs can be studied through that system.

**Ilāh (إِلَٰه)** is the common noun for “god,” usually associated in lexical discussion with the consonants **أ ل ه** and the semantic field of worship. Passages such as 21:25 and 20:14 help situate the word within commands about devotion. But a dictionary gloss cannot settle every question concerning worship or the forms of allegiance people may adopt. Context remains decisive.

**Allāh (ٱللَّهُ)** appears in the statement as the name identified by the exception. The common noun *ilāh* and the proper name *Allāh* should not be treated as interchangeable grammatical items without distinction. Scholars discuss different possibilities concerning the name's historical derivation; those debates do not by themselves reverse the verse's affirmation of one GOD. Nor does a negative statement about other gods imply that those other gods really exist. Naming an idol and establishing the reality of a divine rival are not the same thing.

At the beginning of 47:19, **faʿlam (فَٱعْلَمْ)** is related to **ع ل م**, the field of knowing. The instruction to know is not an addition I have introduced. The verb **istaghfir (ٱسْتَغْفِرْ)** is related to **غ ف ر**, associated here with seeking forgiveness. The verse joins knowledge and forgiveness. I take that pairing as a reason to ask whether deeper understanding can be accompanied by humility. The ethical reflection comes from reading the verse as a whole, not from claiming grammar alone proves it.

In 3:18 the language of testimony is connected with **ش ه د**; the term for equity is related to **ق س ط**. The description of truthful people in 3:17 belongs to the semantic field of **ص د ق**. It would be careless to assign every Turkish or English phrase translated “truthful speech” to the same Arabic root: different verses can use different words while raising connected ethical questions.

Further fields help me follow the wider subject: **ع ب د** for worship and servanthood, **ذ ك ر** for remembrance, **ش ر ك** for partnership or association, **س ل م** in the sphere of submission, and **د ي ن** in the sphere of religion and binding accountability. No root is a magic key that provides the meaning of every verse in which related forms occur. Verses 6:162, 9:31, 29:45, and 3:19 remain indispensable for context.

The main linguistic insight is modest but significant: the movement from *lā* to *illā* denies rival divinity and specifies the exclusive object of devotion. Yet even that insight cannot live truthfully on my behalf. Roots can open words. They cannot perform justice, truthfulness, or submission in place of the person who pronounces them.

**Verse references:** 47:19; 37:35; 21:25; 20:14; 3:18; 3:17; 33:70; 6:162; 9:31; 29:45; 3:19.

## Other Claims

The declaration has been discussed through distinct grammatical, theological, devotional, and historical approaches. These should not be compressed into one uniform tradition. Nor should any commentary silently take the place of a verse. Interpretations may be illuminating without acquiring the authority of revelation.

**The declaration of oneness and the two-part testimony of faith.** In widely established Muslim traditions, the testimony of faith joins belief in GOD's oneness to acknowledgment of Muhammad's messengership. The <a href="https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/kelime-i-sehadet" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">TDV Encyclopedia entry on the shahada</a> explains this practice and argues that the reference to Muhammad as a servant and messenger emphasizes his humanity rather than divinity. By contrast, Rashad Khalifa's approach to 3:18 and 47:19 places the formula of GOD's oneness at the center and criticizes requiring an additional human name in the declaration. One statement of that interpretation appears in <a href="https://submission.org/Shahadah.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">a Submission-oriented discussion of shahada</a>. Recording the difference does not establish every conclusion of either approach or authorize judgments about the intentions of individuals who follow them. The wording of 3:18, 37:35, and 47:19 must remain distinct from the dispute.

**Does *ilāh* mean merely “god,” or “one worthy of worship”?** Some theological and linguistic explanations supply a longer paraphrase emphasizing the right to receive worship. Others favor retaining the statement's more direct wording. The <a href="https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/ilah" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">lexical discussion of *ilāh*</a> and <a href="https://asjp.cerist.dz/en/article/95199" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">scholarly work on the grammar of the exception</a> show how much can lie behind a short expression. Such explanations deserve study. But an expanded paraphrase must not silently replace the wording of Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation or the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation.

**Mystical and interior interpretations.** Some devotional traditions read the first half as a renunciation of false dependencies and the second as an affirmation of GOD, connecting this with the inner life, repetition, and attention. An <a href="https://yaqeeninstitute.org/read/paper/the-meaning-of-la-ilaha-illa-allah-an-annotated-translation-of-imam-al-zarkashis-treatise-on-the-islamic-testimony-of-faith" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">annotated study of a historical treatise</a> documents varieties of such reflection. They may be helpful for thinking, but a particular breathing pattern, count, or spiritual stage cannot be made into a command of the verse without separate grounds. Poetry and meditation do not become identical to revelation.

**Intercession, status, and religious mediation.** Different approaches disagree about the role of revered figures and the meaning of drawing near to GOD. Verses 39:2–3 place the claim of seeking nearness through idols before the reader. Yet ordinary cooperation, seeking knowledge from people, and directing worship to them are distinct matters. The verse should not be stretched into a verdict on every human relationship.

**Numbers, repetition, and divine acceptance.** Some of Rashad Khalifa's appendices advance interpretations concerning a mathematical structure associated with the number nineteen. Each numerical claim requires its own verification. My decision to explore nineteen musical compositions around one devotional phrase does not show that the Quran mandates nineteen repetitions or tracks. Neither a number nor a production technique can guarantee spiritual acceptance.

I may learn from these approaches, question them, and acknowledge their differences. But I do not want any of them to determine the reflection before I have returned to the text itself. My explanation is a limited attempt by a human being. The verses remain the measure I am trying to understand.

## Conclusion

I began by asking what a short sentence means. “La Ilaha Illa Allah” has a plain answer: there is no other god beside GOD. What has become less simple is the life opened by that answer. Knowing a truth and living answerably to it are not the same achievement.

Verse 47:19 begins with knowledge and continues with seeking forgiveness. I want to stay with that order. If the call to know also contains a call to ask forgiveness, knowledge cannot easily become a certificate that I have made no mistakes. GOD knows decisions that I may explain to myself too generously. A strong public declaration should not be a cover for an unexamined private life.

Verse 3:18 joined testimony to knowledge and justice; 3:17 described steadfastness, truthfulness, submission, and generosity. Verse 37:35 presented the danger of arrogance. I find it very easy to read such a warning and immediately imagine myself on the right side of the story. Pride may also show itself in a refusal to correct a known error. That is my personal reflection, not a claim to see another person's heart.

Verses 2:163, 20:8, and chapter 112 challenged my habitual comparisons. Verses 21:22 and 23:91 rejected the picture of competing absolute gods. Verses 21:25, 16:36, and 20:14 brought the focus back to the common call associated with messengers. I do not take these passages as a ready-made social theory that abolishes every human role. I take them as a warning not to place a finite person where GOD belongs. This warning applies to anyone who claims religious authority, including me.

Verse 1:5 and 6:162–163 expanded exclusive worship into the direction of a life. Verses 39:2–3 brought the danger of sanctifying an intermediary into view. Verses 9:31 and 42:21 challenged my willingness to outsource judgment to a person. Verse 39:45 forced another question: why might I seek a famous name to reinforce a truth that I say belongs to GOD alone? The purpose is not to erase proper acknowledgment of others. It is to avoid granting them a divine standing.

The severe warnings in 31:13 and 4:48 leave me no reason to treat idolatry casually. Nor do they entitle me to set up a personal court over the hidden intentions of others. Verses 17:36 and 39:18 call me to examine claims. Verses 4:135, 5:8, 2:42, and 33:70 bring testimony face to face with fair dealing and honest speech. Concealing a known fact or disguising injustice in religious language cannot be made consistent with these responsibilities merely by attaching a sacred phrase.

Verses 2:256 and 6:108 challenged how I communicate conviction without claiming ownership of another person's will. Verses 39:11, 40:14, and 73:8 returned me to sincerity. Verses 13:28, 29:45, and 17:44 opened remembrance beyond what I can produce and measure with sound. I can compose music; I cannot command the listener's heart. I can repeat a declaration thousands of times, but numbers alone will not prove that I have become more just or truthful.

With 16:90 and 6:152 I came back to ordinary work: do I keep a fair measure? Do I conceal another person's loss? Do I credit those who helped build what I now present? The direction of my faith must be allowed to question these practical choices. I do not want to recommend submission to others while refusing to learn it in arguments, professional decisions, family relations, and private thought.

The declaration is therefore not a victory slogan for me. It is not a way of exalting myself. It makes me reconsider the temporary supports to which I have assigned excessive authority. Sometimes the correction may be painful. Sometimes I may discover that I do not know enough. In those moments I hope the words call me to return to what is right rather than merely to speak louder.

There remains much I cannot claim to know. But the question has become clearer: if I testify that GOD alone is god, what will I do today so that my speech, my sense of justice, my work, and my intention do not betray that testimony? I cannot carry every other person's final account. I should not evade my own. That is where I want this declaration to begin its work in my life.
