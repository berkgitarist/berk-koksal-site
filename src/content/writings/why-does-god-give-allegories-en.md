# [2:26] What Did GOD Mean by Such an Allegory? Why Does GOD Give Allegories?

**Author:** Berk KÖKSAL
**Date:** November 25, 2026
**Place:** Mersin

The purpose of this reflection is to consider 2:26 alongside related verses, asking why GOD gives allegories and what bearing witness to His oneness means in human life in terms of knowledge, justice, truthfulness, and submission.

Peace. Peace be upon you.

A mosquito is hardly the creature I would instinctively choose to explain a great truth. It is small enough to dismiss without thought. Yet 2:26 interrupts that instinct. By what measure do I decide that a subject is too small to carry meaning? Why does GOD sometimes address us through a direct statement and at other times ask us to consider fire, rain, a tree, foam, a spider's home, or the light of a lamp?

What concerns me is not merely the beauty of these images. Verse 2:26 speaks about guidance and straying. Its sentence continues into 2:27, where the subject becomes a broken covenant, severed bonds, and evil deeds. There is a moral question inside this literary question. How do I respond when a sign does not arrive in the form I expected? Am I trying to learn, or have I already decided what GOD ought to say? Can I admit that an image may teach me something while also admitting how much I do not yet know?

In [If a Thousand Artificial Witnesses Tell the Same Lie, Does Truth Change?](https://berkkoksal.com/writings/a_thousand_artificial_witnesses_same_lie_en/), I considered the difference between repetition and reliable testimony. In [Leaders and Followers](https://berkkoksal.com/writings/leaders-and-followers-satans-leadership-en/), I examined the responsibility that does not disappear when one person follows another. These concerns meet here. When I seek the meaning of an allegory, am I listening to the scripture, or simply finding a more impressive voice for an opinion I already wanted to hold?

## 1. Why a Mosquito at the Center of the Question?

### Verse

> **2:26**
>
> ۞ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَا يَسْتَحْىِۦٓ أَن يَضْرِبَ مَثَلًۭا مَّا بَعُوضَةًۭ فَمَا فَوْقَهَا ۚ فَأَمَّا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ فَيَعْلَمُونَ أَنَّهُ ٱلْحَقُّ مِن رَّبِّهِمْ ۖ وَأَمَّا ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ فَيَقُولُونَ مَاذَآ أَرَادَ ٱللَّهُ بِهَـٰذَا مَثَلًۭا ۘ يُضِلُّ بِهِۦ كَثِيرًۭا وَيَهْدِى بِهِۦ كَثِيرًۭا ۚ وَمَا يُضِلُّ بِهِۦٓ إِلَّا ٱلْفَـٰسِقِينَ

### Reflection on the Verse

The first thing I notice in 2:26 is the refusal to treat a small subject as an embarrassment. The mosquito is not presented as a defect in the message. The physical size of an object does not determine the seriousness of what an example can communicate. More striking still, the verse moves from the legitimacy of the example to the contrasting responses of those who encounter it.

I must be careful with the question that appears in the verse. I cannot honestly claim that every person who asks what an allegory means is thereby rejecting it. A sincere student also asks questions. What matters is the stance from which the question is asked, and the verse does not leave the matter wholly unexplained. Its sentence carries on into 2:27. To stop before that continuation and condemn every questioner would be to separate what the scripture has connected.

For me, the first lesson is a correction of scale. Instead of deciding that an image is beneath serious consideration, I ought to ask what distinction it is making visible. I am not entitled to manufacture a grand secret out of every small detail. But neither am I entitled to dismiss a divine example because its subject seems ordinary.

**Verse references:** 2:26, 2:27.

## 2. The Familiar Language of Paradise

### Verse

> **2:25** — Give good news to those who believe and lead a righteous life that they will have gardens with flowing streams. When provided with a provision of fruits therein, they will say, "This is what was provided for us previously." Thus, they are given allegorical descriptions. They will have pure spouses therein, and they abide therein forever.

> **47:15** — The allegory of Paradise that is promised for the righteous is this: it has rivers of unpolluted water, and rivers of fresh milk, and rivers of wine— delicious for the drinkers—and rivers of strained honey. They have all kinds of fruits therein, and forgiveness from their Lord. (Are they better) or those who abide forever in the hellfire, and drink hellish water that tears up their intestines?

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 2:25 speaks of gardens, flowing streams, provisions, and pure spouses, and explicitly calls the descriptions allegorical. Verse 47:15 begins its account of the promised Paradise by naming it an allegory. Reading them together, I am drawn to a necessary distinction: allegorical description is not a declaration that the promise is unreal. Yet it also does not give me permission to reduce what is promised to the limits of things I have already experienced.

If I try to imagine a life I have never known, I will reach for words already familiar to me. I know something about water, fruit, milk, honey, and gardens. I do not therefore know every feature of the reality to which those images point. Perhaps part of the mercy of such language is that it gives the human mind something it can contemplate without pretending that the unknown has become fully known.

I want to keep the boundary intact. I cannot derive from these descriptions a complete physical map of Paradise, a fixed sequence of experiences, or an inventory of every detail of the Hereafter. The allegory can awaken hope and moral seriousness without granting me knowledge the verses do not claim to provide.

**Verse references:** 2:25, 47:15.

## 3. Responding to Allegory and the Ethics of the Covenant

### Verse

> **2:27** — who violate GOD's covenant after pledging to uphold it, sever what GOD has commanded to be joined, and commit evil. These are the losers.

### Reflection on the Verse

The last clause of 2:26 runs directly into 2:27. That grammatical continuation matters. When I ask whom the preceding verse is describing, I do not find a mere inability to solve a figure of speech. I find people described in terms of breaking a covenant, cutting what GOD commanded to be joined, and committing evil. The discussion of allegory has moved into the conduct of a life.

It is often easier to spend an afternoon arguing about the precise meaning of a word than to repair a promise I have broken. It is easier to defend an interpretation than to face what it might require of me. This is where the verse becomes personally uncomfortable. Am I using an intellectual question to postpone an ethical responsibility? That is a question about myself, not a license to issue verdicts about the hidden motives of other people.

The verse does not allow me to call everyone who has suffered hardship wicked, nor to invent motives for every person who disagrees with me. It names deeds. I should pay attention to those deeds. If an example can guide or mislead, the surrounding text makes it difficult for me to isolate interpretation from the moral direction in which I am actually moving.

**Verse references:** 2:27.

## 4. The Limits of Knowledge in Multivalent Verses

### Verse

> **3:7** — He sent down to you this scripture, containing straightforward verses—which constitute the essence of the scripture—as well as multiple-meaning or allegorical verses. Those who harbor doubts in their hearts will pursue the multiple-meaning verses to create confusion, and to extricate a certain meaning. None knows the true meaning thereof except GOD and those well founded in knowledge. They say, "We believe in this—all of it comes from our Lord." Only those who possess intelligence will take heed.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 3:7 places straightforward verses alongside verses that have multiple meanings or are allegorical. I do not read this as an invitation to make the scripture say whatever I desire. Rather, the verse warns about seeking a predetermined meaning in order to create confusion. The warning is especially relevant whenever an interpretation feels exciting, exclusive, or flattering to the person presenting it.

When I think I have found a new connection, I have to separate three things: what the verse explicitly states, what its immediate context supports, and what I am proposing as an inference. Confusing them would allow my own words to borrow an authority they have not earned. Being well founded in knowledge cannot mean merely possessing an impressive collection of facts. It must also involve being honest about where evidence ends.

The declaration of faith near the end of 3:7 has a quiet force. I can take the whole message seriously without insisting that every uncertain detail be settled by me today. That does not make investigation unnecessary. It gives investigation a discipline. Seeking understanding is one thing; announcing a private inference as GOD's unmistakable meaning is another.

**Verse references:** 3:7.

## 5. Understanding an Example Is Not Owning Knowledge

### Verse

> **29:43** — We cite these examples for the people, and none appreciate them except the knowledgeable.*

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 29:43 is brief, but its brevity leaves me nowhere to hide. Examples are presented for people, and an appreciation of them is associated with knowledge. The verse does not say that an academic title, a public reputation, or the number of people listening to me makes my interpretation automatically right.

I think of the patience required to understand an example before claiming to explain it. With the spider's home, the first question is not how many imaginative similarities I can invent; it is whom the verse is comparing and why. With another passage, I may need to examine the verses around it before even deciding what the image is addressing. Knowledge is not merely the speed with which I offer an answer. It is also the willingness to check the question.

If my learning makes me increasingly incapable of correction, that troubles me. I would rather let 29:43 challenge the quality of my attention than turn it into a certificate of superiority over people who read differently. Appreciation of an allegory should lead to greater care, not greater self-importance.

**Verse references:** 29:43.

## 6. The Firelight and the Fear of the Storm

### Verse

> **2:17** — Their example is like those who start a fire, then, as it begins to shed light around them, GOD takes away their light, leaving them in darkness, unable to see.

> **2:19** — Another example: a rainstorm from the sky in which there is darkness, thunder, and lightning. They put their fingers in their ears, to evade death. GOD is fully aware of the disbelievers.

> **2:20** — The lightning almost snatches away their eyesight. When it lights for them, they move forward, and when it turns dark, they stand still. If GOD wills, He* can take away their hearing and their eyesight. GOD is Omnipotent.

### Reflection on the Verse

The fire in 2:17 gives light and then leaves its subjects in darkness. The rainstorm of 2:19 and 2:20 is filled with noise, fear, sudden brightness, and interruption. What stays with me is not merely the vocabulary of light and darkness but the pattern of movement: advancing while the way is illuminated, stopping when darkness returns.

I do not want to turn these verses into a clinical diagnosis of anyone's inner life. They do, however, place hearing, seeing, fear, and direction in a vivid relation. A person may feel quite certain when circumstances are easy and then discover how little steadiness remains when conditions change. I can ask whether my own commitment to what I recognize as right lasts only while the road is comfortable.

These examples also precede 2:26. The question about a mosquito is not isolated from the flow of the chapter. The reader has already been asked to imagine a fire and a storm. The sequence matters: familiar experiences are used to draw attention to responses that are far less visible than fire or rain.

**Verse references:** 2:17, 2:19, 2:20.

## 7. Foam, the Current, and What Remains

### Verse

> **13:17** — He sends down water from the sky, causing the valleys to overflow, then the rapids produce abundant foam. Similarly, when they use fire to refine metals for their jewelry or equipment, foam is produced. GOD thus cites analogies for the truth and falsehood. As for the foam, it goes to waste, while that which benefits the people stays close to the ground. GOD thus cites the analogies.

### Reflection on the Verse

In 13:17 the valleys fill, the current rises, and foam gathers on the surface. Foam also appears when metals are refined by fire. The verse explicitly uses the scenes to speak of truth and falsehood. The foam passes away; what benefits people remains. It is a striking way of separating what attracts immediate attention from what has enduring value.

Foam can be the most visible part of moving water. It spreads across the surface and seems to announce itself. Yet visibility is not the measure given in the verse. I think of public arguments, where a claim can become louder with every repetition while remaining unsupported. Still, I should resist the easy reversal that everything quiet must be true. The passage does not offer such a rule either.

An allegory may help me recognize a distinction that an abstract definition would leave untouched. I can see the foam and ask a question about my own work: Am I leaving something beneficial behind, or am I merely producing a moment of excitement? Not every impressive expression carries the weight of truth.

**Verse references:** 13:17.

## 8. The Rooted Good Word and the Rootless Bad Word

### Verse

> **14:24** — Do you not see that GOD has cited the example of the good word as a good tree whose root is firmly fixed, and its branches are high in the sky?

> **14:25** — It produces its crop every season, as designed by its Lord. GOD thus cites the examples for the people, that they may take heed.

> **14:26** — And the example of the bad word is that of a bad tree chopped at the soil level; it has no roots to keep it standing.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verses 14:24–26 describe the good word as a tree with a firm root and recurring fruit, and the bad word as a tree without roots to hold it upright. In this image, speech does not simply vanish after it is uttered. It has a source, a structure, and consequences that extend beyond the instant of speaking.

Words can establish trust or destroy it. They can offer clarity or conceal a wrong. The verses do not catalogue every possible effect of language, but the image of roots and fruit makes me ask about both origin and result. Is the sentence beautiful only in its sound? Is it true? Can I stand behind it when the audience is gone? Does my own conduct undermine the values I claim to defend?

This matters especially when writing about the Quran. I do not want the force of my prose to conceal a weak connection to the verse. If I present a personal reading as a direct command from GOD, my sentence may sound confident while lacking the root it needs. A reflection is more trustworthy when the scripture remains scripture and the author's inference remains identifiable as an inference.

**Verse references:** 14:24, 14:25, 14:26.

## 9. What a Fly Reveals About Powerlessness

### Verse

> **22:73** — O people, here is a parable that you must ponder carefully: the idols you set up beside GOD can never create a fly, even if they banded together to do so. Furthermore, if the fly steals anything from them, they cannot recover it; weak is the pursuer and the pursued.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 22:73 asks people to ponder a fly. The subject is not the insect as an object of contempt, but the powerlessness of the idols set up beside GOD. Their inability to create even a fly—or to recover something taken by it—exposes a contradiction between the power attributed to them and the limits they actually possess.

Human beings can be impressed by scale. Institutions are large, leaders command crowds, and words become grand through repetition. Yet size and prestige alone do not establish ultimate power. The verse makes me ask why I trust a thing, what I expect from it, and whether I have assigned it a place that belongs to GOD alone. That does not mean every human responsibility or institution is automatically an idol. The specific comparison must be kept in view.

The mosquito of 2:26 and the fly of 22:73 meet in my mind. Neither requires physical greatness to carry an important question. A very small creature can expose an exaggerated claim made on behalf of something much larger. Perhaps the difficulty is not understanding the insect at all; it is surrendering the illusion of power I have attached to something else.

**Verse references:** 22:73.

## 10. The Spider’s Home and the Search for Other Masters

### Verse

> **29:41** — The allegory of those who accept other masters beside GOD is that of the spider and her home; the flimsiest of all homes is the home of the spider, if they only knew.*

### Reflection on the Verse

The spider's home in 29:41 is an image of those who accept other masters beside GOD. I do not need to mock the spider or claim that the verse is giving a scientific assessment of every kind of web. Its comparison is about the reliability of the support on which a person depends.

Teachers, researchers, friends, and people who take responsibility can all offer real help. Learning from them is not the same as granting them final authority over what is true. When I lose that distinction, I may also surrender the duty to examine what I am being told. The danger does not begin only when someone explicitly asks to be obeyed without question; it may begin when I find unquestioning obedience more convenient than careful judgment.

In my earlier reflection on [Leaders and Followers](https://berkkoksal.com/writings/leaders-and-followers-satans-leadership-en/), I considered the accountability of the person who follows. The spider's home returns me to that concern through an image. A shelter can have a recognizable form and still fail to be the foundation I imagined. The verse invites me to examine the object of my trust, not merely its appearance.

**Verse references:** 29:41.

## 11. An Image That Speaks of Light

### Verse

> **24:35** — GOD is the light of the heavens and the earth. The allegory of His light is that of a concave mirror behind a lamp that is placed inside a glass container. The glass container is like a bright, pearl-like star. The fuel thereof is supplied from a blessed oil-producing tree, that is neither eastern, nor western. Its oil is almost self-radiating; needs no fire to ignite it. Light upon light. GOD guides to His light whomever He wills. GOD thus cites the parables for the people. GOD is fully aware of all things.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 24:35 unfolds through a concave mirror, a lamp, glass, oil, a tree, and light. The richness of the image is beautiful, but I hesitate to convert it into a literal diagram of a physical mechanism. The verse itself calls it an allegory of GOD's light. That description should govern the care with which I approach every smaller detail.

Multiple features of the image work together. I can reflect on them without claiming to have found their one compulsory hidden correspondence. The verse speaks of guidance and ends by affirming that GOD is fully aware of all things. Those statements give me firmer ground than a private theory that each object must represent a specific historical person, invention, or stage of the universe.

I want to remain with the image long enough for it to examine me. When I say that I am seeking guidance, am I merely asking for more information? Am I prepared to let what I learn change my decisions? Read beside 2:26, this passage suggests a distinction worth making: admiration for the beauty of an allegory is not necessarily the same as living with its moral challenge.

**Verse references:** 24:35.

## 12. Mirage and Darkness: Can Perception Be Trusted?

### Verse

> **24:39** — As for those who disbelieve, their works are like a mirage in the desert. A thirsty person thinks that it is water. But when he reaches it, he finds that it is nothing, and he finds GOD there instead, to requite him fully for his works. GOD is the most efficient reckoner.

> **24:40** — Another allegory is that of being in total darkness in the midst of a violent ocean, with waves upon waves, in addition to thick fog. Darkness upon darkness—if he looked at his own hand, he could barely see it. Whomever GOD deprives of light, will have no light.

### Reflection on the Verse

A thirsty person sees a mirage in 24:39 and expects water. In 24:40 there is a violent ocean, waves upon waves, thick fog, and almost no ability to see. The scenes are different: in one, something appears to be present when it is not; in the other, the light required to see is missing. Together they make me wary of treating my first perception as the whole truth.

Need can turn an appearance into certainty. The thirsty traveler hopes for water, and that hope gives the image unusual power. I recognize a human danger here without pretending to identify other people's unseen condition. The verses speak about disbelief and deeds in their own terms. I have no authority to take the imagery and diagnose anyone who disagrees with me.

What I can do is ask a difficult personal question: Has my desire for a particular answer made the appearance of that answer enough for me? A vivid impression is not by itself independent evidence. These allegories invite me to pause between what I feel sure I see and what I can honestly establish.

**Verse references:** 24:39, 24:40.

## 13. What Rain Teaches About Impermanence

### Verse

> **18:45** — Cite for them the example of this life as water that we send down from the sky to produce plants of the earth, then they turn into hay that is blown away by the wind. GOD is able to do all things.

> **57:20** — Know that this worldly life is no more than play and games, and boasting among you, and hoarding of money and children. It is like abundant rain that produces plants and pleases the disbelievers. But then the plants turn into useless hay, and are blown away by the wind. In the Hereafter there is either severe retribution, or forgiveness from GOD and approval. This worldly life is no more than a temporary illusion.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verses 18:45 and 57:20 return to the growth and disappearance of plants. Rain comes, the earth grows green, and later the plants become dry matter carried away by the wind. The image is familiar enough to be overlooked. Yet the verses place it beside our attachment to the life that seems so stable while we are living it.

I do not read this as a command to despise honest work, affection, or the gifts of daily life. Rather, I am reminded not to mistake their temporary circumstances for permanence. Music I have made, projects I have built, and recognition I may receive all belong to a world in which seasons change. If I make them the final measure of myself, I am asking fragile things to bear more than they can carry.

The rain image moves from nature to conscience. What am I proud of today? Would the value I assign to myself survive the loss of the thing that presently makes me feel important? The scripture does not require me to deny the beauty of growth. It does make me face the fact that growth, by itself, does not abolish the passing of time.

**Verse references:** 18:45, 57:20.

## 14. The Garden of Giving and the Response of the Soil

### Verse

> **2:265** — The example of those who give their money seeking GOD's pleasure, out of sincere conviction, is that of a garden on high fertile soil; when heavy rain falls, it gives twice as much crop. If heavy rain is not available, a drizzle will suffice. GOD is Seer of everything you do.

> **7:58** — The good land readily produces its plants by the leave of its Lord, while the bad land barely produces anything useful. We thus explain the revelations for people who are appreciative.

### Reflection on the Verse

Verse 2:265 compares those who give in pursuit of GOD's pleasure, with sincere conviction, to a productive garden. Heavy rain yields twice the crop, and even a drizzle is enough when heavy rain does not come. Verse 7:58 places good and bad soil side by side. Both images invite attention not only to what falls upon the ground but also to the condition in which growth takes place.

I cannot turn this into a mathematical guarantee that every charitable act will produce an identical visible return. The language of 2:265 connects giving to intention and conviction. That is where I feel examined. What do I hope to gain from generosity? Do I give to relieve a need, or do I use a gift to make someone feel indebted to me? The size of an action cannot be the whole account of its moral significance.

Nor may I take the comparison of soils in 7:58 and label those who suffer poverty or illness as morally deficient. That would require claims the verse does not make. I would rather let the images ask whether my own intentions allow an act of goodness to take root. An allegory can be a measure for self-examination without becoming a weapon against the vulnerable.

**Verse references:** 2:265, 7:58.

## 15. Bearing Witness Requires Knowledge and Justice

### Verse

> **3:18** — GOD bears witness that there is no god except He, and so do the angels and those who possess knowledge. Truthfully and equitably, He is the absolute god; there is no god but He, the Almighty, Most Wise.

> **5:8** — O you who believe, you shall be absolutely equitable, and observe GOD, when you serve as witnesses. Do not be provoked by your conflicts with some people into committing injustice. You shall be absolutely equitable, for it is more righteous. You shall observe GOD. GOD is fully Cognizant of everything you do.

### Reflection on the Verse

In 3:18, testimony to the oneness of GOD stands beside knowledge, truthfulness, and equity. Verse 5:8 then insists that a witness must be fair even when conflict with another group makes injustice tempting. I cannot easily separate these concerns. To bear witness to what is true while knowingly using dishonest means would be a contradiction in my own conduct.

I may need to correct an inaccurate quotation that favors my position. I may need to acknowledge a valid point made by someone with whom I deeply disagree. I may have to stop myself from treating an attractive theory as established fact. These are ordinary acts, yet their importance becomes more visible when I read the verses about testimony and justice. An admirable conclusion does not authorize a false statement offered in its support.

In [If a Thousand Artificial Witnesses Tell the Same Lie, Does Truth Change?](https://berkkoksal.com/writings/a_thousand_artificial_witnesses_same_lie_en/), I considered why a crowd of repeated claims is not the same as independent evidence. The point applies to allegory as well. Even if many people repeat an interpretation, that repetition does not alter the words of the verse. Witnessing the oneness of GOD calls me to examine the integrity of my own words, not only the error I think I see in others.

**Verse references:** 3:18, 5:8.

## 16. The Same Question, a Number, and the Limits of Human Authority

### Verse

> **74:31** — We appointed angels to be guardians of Hell, and we assigned their number (19) (1) to disturb the disbelievers, (2) to convince the Christians and Jews (that this is a divine scripture), (3) to strengthen the faith of the faithful, (4) to remove all traces of doubt from the hearts of Christians, Jews, as well as the believers, and (5) to expose those who harbor doubt in their hearts, and the disbelievers; they will say, "What did GOD mean by this allegory?" GOD thus sends astray whomever He wills, and guides whomever He wills. None knows the soldiers of your Lord except He. This is a reminder for the people.

> **9:31** — They have set up their religious leaders and scholars as lords,* instead of GOD. Others deified the Messiah, son of Mary. They were all commanded to worship only one god. There is no god except He. Be He glorified, high above having any partners.

### Reflection on the Verse

The question in 74:31 is remarkably close to the question in 2:26. In the setting of the number nineteen, the verse again mentions an allegory and the contrasting outcomes of guidance and straying. I ought to read carefully what 74:31 actually attributes to the number. It would be a different act to take that number and treat every further theory connected with it as though the verse had already affirmed it.

Verse 9:31 raises another boundary: religious leaders and scholars can be taken as lords instead of GOD. Read together, these passages bring the responsibility of interpretation into focus. I may benefit enormously from another person's work. But if I make the person's explanation beyond examination, I risk allowing a human voice to displace the authority of the words it claims to explain.

This is not an argument that human teachers possess no useful knowledge or that consulting a scholar is inherently wrong. The verse does not say that. The danger lies in confusing learning with surrendering ultimate authority. Even when an interpreter offers a compelling explanation of an allegory, that interpreter does not become the owner of GOD's example. I need to keep that distinction visible, especially when I feel most convinced.

**Verse references:** 74:31, 9:31.

## 17. Arabic Word Roots and the Language of Allegory

### Verse

> **2:26** — GOD does not shy away from citing any kind of allegory,* from the tiny mosquito and greater. As for those who believe, they know that it is the truth from their Lord. As for those who disbelieve, they say, "What did GOD mean by such an allegory?" He misleads many thereby, and guides many thereby. But He never misleads thereby except the wicked,

> **29:43** — We cite these examples for the people, and none appreciate them except the knowledgeable.*

### Reflection on the Verse

The word **مَثَلًا (mathalan)** in 2:26 belongs to the Arabic root **م ث ل (m-th-l)**, a family of words concerned with likeness and example. **يَضْرِبَ (yadriba)** comes from **ض ر ب (d-r-b)** and forms part of the expression for citing an example in this context. Listing the first dictionary meaning of each isolated word would not be enough to understand the sentence. Words speak together, inside a construction.

The same verse associates **فَيَعْلَمُونَ (faya'lamuna)** with knowing, from **ع ل م ('-l-m)**; **ٱلْحَقُّ (al-haqq)** with truth, from **ح ق ق (h-q-q)**; **وَيَهْدِى (wa-yahdi)** with guidance, from **ه د ي (h-d-y)**; **يُضِلُّ (yudillu)** with going astray, from **ض ل ل (d-l-l)**; and **ٱلْفَـٰسِقِينَ (al-fasiqin)** with **ف س ق (f-s-q)**. These relationships can help me observe the vocabulary of the passage. They do not grant me permission to disregard the description of conduct in 2:27 and apply the final term to anyone I choose.

Verse 29:43 connects appreciation of examples with knowledge. Studying roots can serve that knowledge; it cannot replace attention to grammar, context, and the boundaries of an argument. I want Arabic study to make me more careful about what I claim, not more confident in claims that no verse actually establishes.

**Verse references:** 2:26, 29:43.

## 18. Other Claims

### Verse

> **3:7** — He sent down to you this scripture, containing straightforward verses—which constitute the essence of the scripture—as well as multiple-meaning or allegorical verses. Those who harbor doubts in their hearts will pursue the multiple-meaning verses to create confusion, and to extricate a certain meaning. None knows the true meaning thereof except GOD and those well founded in knowledge. They say, "We believe in this—all of it comes from our Lord." Only those who possess intelligence will take heed.

### Reflection on the Verse

Different traditions have offered different accounts of why a mosquito appears in 2:26. Some historical explanations connect it to objections raised against the preceding images of fire and rain. Others concentrate on the allegorical language used for Paradise and Hell. These approaches may draw attention to features of the neighboring text, but I do not have to accept any one historical reconstruction as the only possible account.

Another style of interpretation seeks a fixed secret counterpart for every feature of an allegory. Some readers also propose scientific or numerical schemes based upon particular images. Such proposals can be discussed as claims, but the truth of a proposed scheme is not established merely because an image appears in the Quran. The warning in 3:7 against forcing a particular meaning and creating confusion should restrain the certainty with which any interpreter speaks.

I do not want to dismiss an interpretation solely because it is old, traditional, or associated with a particular school. Neither do I want to grant it final authority because many people have repeated it. Historical commentary may be informative, but it belongs in a different category from the words of the verse itself. When the text does not decide an issue, caution is not weakness.

**Verse references:** 3:7.

## 19. Conclusion

### Verse

> **2:26** — GOD does not shy away from citing any kind of allegory,* from the tiny mosquito and greater. As for those who believe, they know that it is the truth from their Lord. As for those who disbelieve, they say, "What did GOD mean by such an allegory?" He misleads many thereby, and guides many thereby. But He never misleads thereby except the wicked,

> **2:27** — who violate GOD's covenant after pledging to uphold it, sever what GOD has commanded to be joined, and commit evil. These are the losers.

### Reflection on the Verse

I began with a mosquito. Returning to 2:26, I now see a question about the size of my own certainty. GOD cites examples; the human response can involve guidance or straying. Verse 2:27 carries the sentence forward into covenant and conduct. I do not want to separate those subjects simply because arguments about imagery are easier than the work of correcting a life.

Fire and rain describe lost direction. Foam passes while what benefits remains. A tree has roots, or lacks them. A spider's home challenges misplaced reliance. The mirage exposes false expectation, and light recalls the need for guidance. These images do not give me the power to read another person's heart or pronounce every private inference to be GOD's final meaning. They invite me to think, speak truthfully, and remain fair.

So I will continue to ask, “What did GOD mean by such an allegory?” But I need to place another question beside it: Am I asking in order to understand, or am I using the question to defend a resistance I do not wish to examine? Sometimes the smallest image confronts the greatest arrogance. I can write about what I have understood. I must also be willing to say what I do not know. To me, that humility belongs alongside knowledge, justice, and submission whenever I try to bear witness to the oneness of GOD.

**Verse references:** 2:26, 2:27.
