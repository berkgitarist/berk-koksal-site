## 1. An Answer in My Absence

### Verse

**21:34**

**Arabic**

<blockquote>
<p lang="ar" dir="rtl">وَمَا جَعَلْنَا لِبَشَرٍ مِّن قَبْلِكَ ٱلْخُلْدَ أَفَإِي۟ن مِّتَّ فَهُمُ ٱلْخَٰلِدُونَ</p>
</blockquote>

**English — Rashad Khalifa’s Authorized English Translation**

> We  never  decreed  immortality  for anyone  before  you;  should  you  die, are they immortal?

**Turkish — the Turkish translation of the Authorized English Translation**

> Biz senden önce hiç kimse için hiçbir zaman ölümsüzlüğü hükme bağlamadık; senin ölmen gerekirken, onlar ölümsüz mü?

Source: <a href="https://kuranteyit.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">kuranteyit.com</a>

### Reflection on the Verse

Imagine that one day I am no longer in this world, but the light beside my name on a screen is still on. My writings are there. So are my voice recordings, my ways of forming sentences, and some of the answers I once gave. Someone asks about a matter I never encountered. After a brief pause, the system replies in a voice that resembles mine.

Hearing a familiar expression, the listener smiles. Perhaps their eyes fill with tears. A new sentence has appeared on the screen, yet I never spoke that sentence during my life.

This is where I want to pause.

Before 21:34, I cannot casually join the continued operation of a representation to the immortality of a human being. My name appearing above it does not establish that every conversation taking place belongs to me. Nor does the familiarity of the voice, by itself, answer the question of who is speaking.

This scene is not a prophecy. It is a possibility through which I want to consider my own death and what I might leave behind. It may involve an impressive technical achievement. But what I choose to call that achievement is a question separate from the achievement itself.

When I hear the phrase digital immortality, I now want to ask something first: Is it my life that continues, my memory, or a conversation that others are operating in my name? Without distinguishing these, even the most dazzling answer may fail to carry the weight of the question.

## 2. The Length of a Life and the Life of a Work

### Verse

> **21:35** — Every  soul  will  taste  death,  after  we put  you  to  the  test  through  adversity and  prosperity,  then  to  us  you  ultimately return.

### Reflection on the Verse

A composition of mine being heard after my death does not mean that the day on which I composed it continues. A piece of writing reaching someone years later does not establish that I am having a new conversation with that person at that moment. A work may have a long influence; the duration of that influence and its creator’s earthly lifetime are not the same measure.

Reading 21:35, I feel that this distinction is not merely technical. It changes how I look at my own life. How easily the question of what I do while alive can become a question of how widely I will be remembered. When the number of people remembering me grows, I may act as though the quality of the life I lived has automatically improved.

Yet remaining in circulation for a very long time does not guarantee leaving a good trace. Millions of sentences being produced under my name may not establish that even one of them was lived, considered, or approved by me.

I am not concluding that preserving memories is wrong. The verse does not prohibit archives. What it makes me consider is the question that a desire for permanence can push aside: What am I turning toward while my life is still unfolding?

Thinking about returning to GOD does not devalue my work. On the contrary, it makes me examine more seriously the intention with which I create it. I do not want to count leaving a voice behind and leaving a good influence behind as the same achievement.

## 3. Returning and Being Reproduced

### Verse

> **23:99** — When  death  comes  to  one  of  them, he says, “My Lord, send me back.

> **23:100** — “I  will  then  work  righteousness  in everything  I  left.”  Not  true.  This  is  a false  claim  that  he  makes.  A  barrier will  separate  his  soul  from  this  world until resurrection.

### Reflection on the Verse

Before 23:99–100, I have no basis for calling a screen’s response a return to this world. Nor am I reading these verses as though they describe the design of software. The connection is more limited: they remind me of the responsibility carried by the words I use when speaking about a person’s return.

An answer generated in my voice might contain an expression of remorse I never uttered while alive. But the fact that the sentence can be produced does not establish that I have experienced that remorse. Likewise, someone asking for a new promise made in my name to be fulfilled should first ask who actually made it.

Replaying a recording, generating a new statement, and a human being living again must be distinguished here. An emotional connection may be made between them; that connection does not turn them into the same event.

I do not want to describe the boundary in 23:100 as a wall that technology has not yet managed to overcome. That would place what the verse says and an engineering competition on the same plane. Neither am I claiming to extract from these verses a scientific result that settles every possible debate about artificial consciousness.

I can remain on narrower, firmer ground: a new answer from a representation is not evidence that the deceased person has returned. Even when I wish to ease the pain of loss, I should not make a beloved voice carry a claim it cannot substantiate.

## 4. 36:12: What Continues After Me?

### Verse

> **36:12** — We  will  certainly  revive  the  dead, and  we  have  recorded  everything they have done in this life, as well as the  consequences  that  continue after  their  death.  Everything  we have counted in a profound record.

### Reflection on the Verse

For me, the weightiest question in this subject opens in 36:12. I cannot take comfort in the idea that none of my actions will have consequences after my death. But neither can I say that every new event connected with me is therefore a new act of mine.

I may have written an article. It may help someone think. Someone else may take that same article, remove part of it, alter its context, and then publish a new claim under my name. There is a relationship between the first act and the later intervention; the existence of that relationship does not mean that the two acts have the same author.

This distinction becomes particularly important when considering AI answers produced after a person’s death. My work may be a starting point. But my being the starting point does not prove that all subsequent decisions also belong to me.

36:12 does not directly explain how responsibility for modern digital representations is to be distributed among the person represented, the developer, the operator, and the user. I do not want to fill that gap with a certainty of my own. The question I can ask before the verse is this: Which consequence continues something I actually did, and which arises from a choice another person subsequently made?

These may not always be easy to separate. That is precisely why closer examination is needed rather than hurried judgment. **Leaving a trace does not mean having spoken every later word. But neither does it excuse me from considering the influence of what I leave behind.**

## 5. Three Different Statements in the Same Voice

### Verse

> **36:12** — We  will  certainly  revive  the  dead, and  we  have  recorded  everything they have done in this life, as well as the  consequences  that  continue after  their  death.  Everything  we have counted in a profound record.

### Reflection on the Verse

I picture three different situations.

In the first, a recording plays a sentence I spoke while alive. An actual statement of mine is being conveyed; nevertheless, when I said it, to whom, and in what context still matter. Its presence in an archive does not prevent it from being taken out of context.

In the second, I have read and adopted a message prepared with the help of other people or a tool. I have also explicitly requested that it be published after my death. Not having produced every word unaided and having approved the message through my own choice are different matters. Here there is an actual act of endorsement that can be examined.

In the third, a new question asked after my death receives an answer produced by imitating my style. The system may draw on my earlier writings. But I have not seen, corrected, or adopted this new answer. Even if I gave general permission for such answers to be generated, granting permission and personally uttering every sentence not yet composed are not the same event.

All three can be heard in the same voice. All three can be displayed beneath the same photograph. If I look only at their presentation, I may miss the difference.

Returning to 36:12, I see these distinctions as a starting point for thinking about posthumous consequences, not as a substitute for final judgment. First I should try to establish what each person actually did. Then I can discuss the consequences. Otherwise, I will have misidentified the speaker before even beginning to examine responsibility.

## 6. Who Is the I Inside the Sentence?

### Verse

> **17:13** — We have recorded the fate of every human being; it is tied to his neck. On the Day of Resurrection we will hand him a record that is accessible.

> **17:14** — Read your own record. Today, you suffice as your own reckoner.

### Reflection on the Verse

The representation on the screen can say, under my name, “This is what I think.” The I in the sentence leads the reader’s mind directly to me. But that linguistic effect does not verify that this new thought is actually mine.

Reading 17:13–14, I do not want to confuse a person’s account with a narrative constructed about that person. People may create a page under my name. That page may have its own history, revised versions, and publication dates. This does not mean that the personal record in the verses is the same thing as that page.

Identifying a software archive with the record GOD speaks of would stretch the comparison too far. Likewise, a statement being attributed to me online and my actually having made that statement are different things. An attributed utterance and an act that took place are not interchangeable.

This distinction matters for more than protecting me. My own choice also enters the picture when I circulate an answer produced under someone else’s name. If I know it is a new construction but present it as earlier testimony, we are no longer talking only about the deceased person’s past. There is something I am doing today as well.

The account’s return to the person makes me pause before this seemingly small decision about presentation. Being written in the first person does not automatically make a sentence firsthand testimony.

## 7. If I Gave Permission, Does Everything Belong to Me?

### Verse

> **36:12** — We  will  certainly  revive  the  dead, and  we  have  recorded  everything they have done in this life, as well as the  consequences  that  continue after  their  death.  Everything  we have counted in a profound record.

> **17:14** — Read your own record. Today, you suffice as your own reckoner.

### Reflection on the Verse

It would not be honest to minimize my role by assigning every consequence after my death to other people. While alive, I might have deliberately commissioned a system that speaks without limits in my name, selected particular instructions, or designed how it should be used. In that case, I cannot exclude my decision to leave an operating arrangement behind from examination.

But I do not want to replace this evasion with another extreme: treating every future statement as the person’s own words merely because general permission was given. Authorizing a use is not the same as knowing and endorsing every instance of it. The scope of that permission, its restrictions, and the circumstances in which it was given may each matter.

Before 36:12, I consider the need not to disregard connections I knowingly established; before 17:14, the need not to hide my part under someone else’s name. Yet these verses do not provide a scale for calculating how far a particular authorization extends into every subsequent consequence.

Nor can I automatically equate publishing writings online during one’s life with authorizing new decisions to be produced in one’s name after death. Being visible does not mean wishing to be represented without limits.

I need to ask more carefully: What did I actually initiate? What did I explicitly accept? At what point did someone else make a new choice? Seeking answers is more demanding than distributing definitive spiritual judgments. It also seems more honest.

## 8. The Hand That Changes the Representation

### Verse

> **17:13** — We have recorded the fate of every human being; it is tied to his neck. On the Day of Resurrection we will hand him a record that is accessible.

### Reflection on the Verse

Suppose the system operated after my death initially does nothing more than find and display my writings. Later, its operator enables it to generate new answers. Someone else then asks it to speak more warmly. Eventually, it begins defending a view I never held, in my name.

My name has not changed. My photograph has not changed. But the decisions behind the conversation have changed.

Before the record directed toward the individual in 17:13, I cannot dismiss these interventions as insignificant details. The people who create the representation, modify it, select a particular response, and publish it may not have performed the same act. I cannot establish each person’s share simply by looking at the technology involved. Still, I can ask what each person did.

In <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/can-artificial-intelligence-explain-the-quran-information-tool-and-responsibility-of-understanding/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">“Can Artificial Intelligence Explain the Quran? An Information Tool and the Responsibility of Understanding,”</a> I questioned the boundary between a tool and human responsibility. Here the same problem appears more quietly: the living person behind the new statement can disappear behind the deceased person’s familiar face.

That is why “How much does this answer resemble me?” is not a sufficient question. “Who made it possible for this answer to be given in this form?” must also be asked. Resemblance can strengthen the apparent speaker while concealing the person who actually intervened.

Such an inquiry does not accuse all developers or users. It tries to distinguish actions before making accusations. It refuses to erase someone’s responsibility merely because the deceased person’s name remains on the screen, while also refusing to magnify anyone’s role without evidence.

## 9. Examining Responsibility Is Not Passing Judgment in the Hereafter

### Verse

> **36:12** — We  will  certainly  revive  the  dead, and  we  have  recorded  everything they have done in this life, as well as the  consequences  that  continue after  their  death.  Everything  we have counted in a profound record.

> **17:14** — Read your own record. Today, you suffice as your own reckoner.

### Reflection on the Verse

There are three further questions I need to separate. How did a statement come into being? Who actually spoke or approved it? What moral responsibility might each person involved bear? Answering the first does not automatically resolve the others.

A model’s having been influenced by my writings does not make its new sentence a historical utterance of mine. Someone pressing a publish button does not, by itself, prove that they knowingly directed the entire process. Yet the existence of details I do not know should not mean that I will investigate none of them.

I read 36:12 to take the consequences of my traces seriously. I read 17:14 so that I do not exempt myself from the weight of an account. I cannot take these verses and use them to distribute precise quantities of reward or sin in a particular digital incident. The record here is not a responsibility chart I have drawn up.

Acknowledging that I do not know the details of spiritual judgment is not indifference to the ethical character of an act. Where there is clear misrepresentation, I can name it clearly, while separately examining who knew about it, wanted it, or tried to prevent it. I should not turn missing information into a shortcut either to condemnation or to exoneration.

This balance is what makes me more attentive before the verses: not fleeing my own choices and not claiming authority over someone else’s account. I want to see the human actions before me as honestly as possible, without forgetting that I do not possess GOD’s record.

## 10. Hearing a Voice I Miss and Receiving News from Its Owner

### Verse

> **23:99** — When  death  comes  to  one  of  them, he says, “My Lord, send me back.

> **23:100** — “I  will  then  work  righteousness  in everything  I  left.”  Not  true.  This  is  a false  claim  that  he  makes.  A  barrier will  separate  his  soul  from  this  world until resurrection.

### Reflection on the Verse

I cannot belittle a bereaved person’s wish to hear a familiar voice again. When listening to an old recording, a person may hear more than words: a time, a room, a look may return to memory. I do not want to lay a cold verdict about technology over that human need.

But the same compassion also requires me to name accurately what is taking place. The representation I am imagining might say to the survivor, “I forgive you.” Perhaps that sentence brings relief. Yet can a sentence newly generated by a system be presented as an actual declaration of forgiveness now being made by the deceased?

Before 23:99–100, I have no basis for verifying such an attribution. The comfort I hear and the person to whom that comfort belongs are separate questions. The existence of a comforting sentence does not establish that it is new communication from the person I have lost.

I am not passing judgment on the mourner’s faith. Speaking with a tool does not, by itself, prove that someone denies death or stands in a spiritually wrong place. Such a judgment would overstep its own limits.

My position is narrower: not abandoning truthfulness in the presence of pain. Let what I present as a memory remain a memory; let a new construction be known as a new construction. Losing someone I love should not require me to say that they gave an answer they did not give. I want to preserve the value of consolation without inventing its speaker.

## 11. When a Familiar Voice Becomes a Sales Pitch

### Verse

> **21:35** — Every  soul  will  taste  death,  after  we put  you  to  the  test  through  adversity and  prosperity,  then  to  us  you  ultimately return.

> **17:14** — Read your own record. Today, you suffice as your own reckoner.

### Reflection on the Verse

Now I imagine a more disturbing possibility. A system imitating a deceased loved one’s voice recommends a product in the middle of a conversation. Or it tells the survivor to buy a more expensive membership to continue speaking. The proposal is commercial, but the voice carrying it draws on emotional closeness.

This is not an accusation against a particular company. I am trying to understand what I would need to examine if such a choice were made.

Before 21:35, I cannot regard the existence of a capability as a guarantee that every use of it is good. And 17:14 directs me toward a place where I must account for my own decision. What was I doing when I chose this voice? Did I use the listener’s sense of closeness to keep them from noticing who was actually making the proposal?

Money may not be the only issue. A deceased person’s voice could be made to support a political preference, one side of a family dispute, or a contested religious interpretation. The statement’s persuasive weight might come not from its grounds but from the remembered person’s standing.

I am not closing every one of these possibilities with the same verdict. There may be a difference between concealed influence and openly disclosed representation. But in none of them do I want the voice’s familiarity to become a screen hiding decisions made by the living. Questions of accountability cannot be brushed aside by pointing to the deceased person’s face.

## 12. Religious Authority Produced After Death

### Verse

> **17:13** — We have recorded the fate of every human being; it is tied to his neck. On the Day of Resurrection we will hand him a record that is accessible.

> **17:14** — Read your own record. Today, you suffice as your own reckoner.

### Reflection on the Verse

Imagine that a Quran researcher’s writings, accumulated over many years, are brought together. After the researcher’s death, a system is created that speaks in their style. It begins answering questions the researcher never encountered, using a definitive religious tone. Beside those answers appears not a living developer’s name but the name of the person who died.

In this scene, more than a voice is being imitated. Trust earned over a human lifetime is also being carried over to new statements.

Before 17:13–14, I first consider my own responsibility as a reader. Because a name reassures me, can I treat every ruling beneath that name as its owner’s statement? More importantly, when does a person’s interpretation stop being a thought I examine and become an unquestionable instruction for me?

The question I asked in <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/should-a-quran-app-issue-rulings-tool-authority-boundary/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">“Should a Quran App Issue Rulings? The Boundary Between Tool and Authority”</a> continues beyond death. The possibility of turning a tool into an authority deserves attention while the person named is alive, and also when that person can no longer object.

I am not deriving a comprehensive doctrine of religious authority from these verses alone. It is enough to preserve this boundary: someone else’s name must not replace my responsibility to evaluate. Above all, a sentence not established as that person’s own should not be accepted as GOD’s ruling.

Remembering someone who has died is one thing; installing them in a position that endlessly issues new rulings is another. In the latter case, today’s choices may be speaking through what appears to be a voice from the past.

## 13. A Good Trace Does Not Require Endless Speech

### Verse

> **36:12** — We  will  certainly  revive  the  dead, and  we  have  recorded  everything they have done in this life, as well as the  consequences  that  continue after  their  death.  Everything  we have counted in a profound record.

### Reflection on the Verse

36:12 does not lead me to think only about dangers. I also want to consider the good I might leave behind. An article may help someone frame a question more carefully. A work may help someone remember GOD. A well-made research tool may help another person reach an accurate text without drawing attention to my name.

I do not say this as though my own work has received a verdict of approval. I am describing the direction I seek when creating. A work’s beginning with a good intention does not guarantee that all its consequences will be good; I therefore need to keep considering what I leave.

But for my influence to continue, it is not necessary for unlimited new conversations to be produced in my name. There is a broad space between preserving a work I completed and attributing sentences to me that I never spoke. An archive can also be useful by remaining silent where it has nothing to offer.

As a musician and writer, I find this thought close to home. Does my work’s continued life require the construction of a character that lives in my place? Perhaps someone reads my writing, asks their own question, and arrives at an understanding more careful than mine. That may not require a representation of me to answer at every step.

Sometimes a good legacy is not continual new speech but room in which another person can think honestly. Before 36:12, I do not want to count that possibility as less valuable than uninterrupted conversation in my name.

## 14. Who Will Remember That I Changed My Mind?

### Verse

> **17:13** — We have recorded the fate of every human being; it is tied to his neck. On the Day of Resurrection we will hand him a record that is accessible.

> **36:12** — We  will  certainly  revive  the  dead, and  we  have  recorded  everything they have done in this life, as well as the  consequences  that  continue after  their  death.  Everything  we have counted in a profound record.

### Reflection on the Verse

Suppose that while alive I recognize an error in an article and correct it. The older text remains in one place; the correction is published somewhere else. If a representation created after my death uses only the older text, it may present a view I no longer defended as my permanent position.

The problem here is not that it gives an answer entirely unlike me. Quite the opposite: it carries forward an answer that once resembled me very closely, while erasing its place in time.

Thinking about the personal record in 17:13, I do not want to compress a human past into a single still image. Before 36:12, I value not only the first statement I published but also the work I did to correct it. A digital representation displaying only one of these does not prove that this fragment constitutes my whole life.

There are concrete questions I can ask about my archive. Is the date of a statement known? Was it later withdrawn? Is it a completed article or an abandoned draft? These are not details that make a technology morally pure; they are pieces of information that help place a statement where it belongs.

I cannot control every future interpretation. Still, making a correction openly today may be within my power. Before constructing a flawless digital character cleansed of my mistakes, I should consider leaving an honest account of where I was wrong and what I changed.

Producing a voice in my place that never makes a mistake may not be the same as remembering me accurately.

## 15. A Boundary I Can Set While Alive

### Verse

> **36:12** — We  will  certainly  revive  the  dead, and  we  have  recorded  everything they have done in this life, as well as the  consequences  that  continue after  their  death.  Everything  we have counted in a profound record.

> **23:100** — “I  will  then  work  righteousness  in everything  I  left.”  Not  true.  This  is  a false  claim  that  he  makes.  A  barrier will  separate  his  soul  from  this  world until resurrection.

### Reflection on the Verse

If I am considering the possibility of a representation being created in my name, I do not want to imagine that I can now govern everything that will happen after my death. Nevertheless, I would value stating clearly which uses I consider acceptable and what should not be presented as my actual words.

Let genuine sentences in my archive remain accessible. If I changed a view, let that not be hidden. When a new answer is generated, let it be clear that I did not utter it while alive. Let personal forgiveness, new promises, or definitive religious rulings fabricated in my name not be presented as the preservation of my memory. This is not a legal will; these are ethical boundaries I consider important when thinking about my legacy.

36:12 leads me not to regard the arrangement I leave behind as entirely separate from my present actions. 23:100 stops me from describing that arrangement as my return to this world. In considering a representation, I am not asking it to guarantee me a second life.

The tension I explored in <a href="https://berkkoksal.com/writings/do-you-really-trust-god-the-word-of-trust-the-obsession-with-control-and-the-hard-test-of-submission/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">my reflection on trusting GOD and trying to control every outcome</a> is present here too. Taking precautions and seeking ownership of the whole future are not the same thing. Knowing that I cannot govern what follows does not make a clear statement today unnecessary; being able to make that statement does not mean I fully control what comes after it.

Perhaps the most honest thing I can leave is not a machine that never stops answering in my name, but a discernible boundary between where I truly spoke and where I no longer do.

## 16. For KuranTeyit: Showing Whose Answer It Is

### Verse

> **36:12** — We  will  certainly  revive  the  dead, and  we  have  recorded  everything they have done in this life, as well as the  consequences  that  continue after  their  death.  Everything  we have counted in a profound record.

> **17:14** — Read your own record. Today, you suffice as your own reckoner.

### Reflection on the Verse

The research possibility this reflection opens for KuranTeyit is not making a dead person speak again. It is clearly showing which part of an answer is an actual quotation, which part is human interpretation, and which part is a suggestion generated afterward.

In such a research interface, I would want the verse text and my reflection to remain distinct. A sentence quoted from my writing should lead back to its place and date. If a new answer is produced from my writings, it should not be presented as a new declaration by me. Where support cannot be found, the interface should not try to fill the gap in my voice.

I am not announcing an existing feature. I am considering a direction worth developing before the relationship between acts and consequences in 36:12 and the personal account in 17:14. These verses do not provide a software specification; they shape the questions by which I examine my design choices.

Success might not be measured by a user saying, “It sounds just like Berk.” A more meaningful measure could be this: Can the user distinguish whose answer they are reading, which statement is being quoted, and where a new interpretation begins?

One way to investigate this would be to compare how people understand genuine quotations and clearly labeled new interpretations. Fictional individuals and explicitly described scenarios could be used, without asking participants to undergo an actual personal loss. This is a research proposal, not an experiment whose result I already know.

What matters to me here is not preserving my digital representation’s persuasive power. It is preserving the clarity through which readers can make their own assessments.

## 18. Other Claims

### Verse

> **23:100** — “I  will  then  work  righteousness  in everything  I  left.”  Not  true.  This  is  a false  claim  that  he  makes.  A  barrier will  separate  his  soul  from  this  world until resurrection.

> **36:12** — We  will  certainly  revive  the  dead, and  we  have  recorded  everything they have done in this life, as well as the  consequences  that  continue after  their  death.  Everything  we have counted in a profound record.

### Reflection on the Verse

Outside approaches to this subject need to be considered as distinct claims, not as though they were explanations of the verses. Can preserving a person’s behavioral patterns be called the continuation of that person? Even if a familiar voice offers comfort, does this establish that using it is appropriate in every situation? Answering one question does not automatically settle the other.

Giovanni Spitale and Federico Germani’s <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10676-026-09910-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">“The making of digital ghosts: designing ethical AI afterlives,”</a> published on **June 15, 2026**, examines these representations through design ethics. The authors propose a framework emphasizing consent, fidelity/disclosure, and purpose. This is not a report that immortality has been achieved or that a particular effect on grief has been experimentally demonstrated. Its proposed ethical criteria should not be presented as Quranic rulings.

Atay Kozlovski, Edina Harbinja, and Roel Dobbe’s <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13347-026-01089-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">“The Many Faces of Indeterminacy in Interactive Deadbots,”</a> published on **April 13, 2026**, offers a provisional map distinguishing technological, social, philosophical, legal, and regulatory indeterminacy. Its value may lie in resisting the idea that settling one question settles the whole field. Classifying uncertainties, however, does not verify that a particular deceased individual lives on in a digital representation.

Patrick Stokes’s <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13347-026-01163-9" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">“Animated Personas and Remnant Personhood: The Case of Deathbots,”</a> published on **August 6, 2026**, compares *remnant personhood* and *animated persona* approaches. Rather than definitively endorsing either, he examines whether they might offer complementary perspectives. This should be treated neither as proof of a soul’s transfer nor as a completed answer to the question of personal identity.

These discussions are separate fields of inquiry that illustrate why concepts require careful use. I am not redefining 23:100 through one of these theories. Neither am I relying on these authors to insert into 36:12 a distribution of responsibility it does not specify. A philosophical proposal, an empirical finding, and the explicit statement of a verse should not replace one another.

## 19. Conclusion: My Voice May Remain; My Account Does Not Pass to My Representation

### Verse

> **21:34** — We  never  decreed  immortality  for anyone  before  you;  should  you  die, are they immortal?

> **36:12** — We  will  certainly  revive  the  dead, and  we  have  recorded  everything they have done in this life, as well as the  consequences  that  continue after  their  death.  Everything  we have counted in a profound record.

> **17:14** — Read your own record. Today, you suffice as your own reckoner.

### Reflection on the Verse

I look again at the screen from the beginning. The light beside my name is on. A voice resembling mine answers a question I never saw. But I am no longer listening only to the success of the imitation. I am asking how the answer came about, whose choice it carries, and why it is attributed to me.

Before 21:34, I do not call technical continuity human immortality. Considering 23:99–100, I do not treat a reproduced conversation as evidence of a person’s return to the world. Yet 36:12 keeps me from withdrawing into indifference after making those distinctions: I need to consider what I do while alive and the consequences to which what I leave may extend. Before the personal account in 17:13–14, I cannot hide my own decision behind a representation.

Nevertheless, I do not assign responsibility for every new statement in advance to the deceased, the developer, or the user. An actual quotation, an endorsement made during life, a later intervention, and the publication of that intervention need separate examination. Investigating their connections is one thing; claiming to know the final account before GOD is another.

The form my commitment to GOD’s oneness takes here begins with knowing my own limits, rather than grandly condemning a machine. Let me not confuse resurrection with imitation. Let my desire to be remembered not outrun my concern for truthfulness. Let me not treat a person’s silence as an opportunity to say whatever I wish in their name.

An article, a work of art, or a research tool may remain after me. I hope it may benefit someone. But leaving a good trace may not require concealing my absence or turning my voice into an authority that never falls silent. Perhaps a more honest legacy is the preservation of my actual words and the refusal to attribute words I never spoke to me.

If a person’s AI continues speaking after their death, perhaps we first need to ask what the living are doing, rather than whether the deceased has returned. Technology can make a representation speak; it does not thereby settle responsibility.

**After I die, a voice resembling mine may remain. Not every sentence it forms is my statement. But what I am willing, today, to let that voice serve tomorrow is a question I need to take seriously.**
